Butcher for Wyoming Senate District 21 #ago Formal Senate training starts in December and continues in January, but I’m not much of a wait-until-then person.
Since the primary, I’ve been using this time to prepare.
I’ve been reviewing past committee work and legislation, spending time with the state budget, talking with people who know these issues well, and looking closely at what may be coming in 2027.
I’m also exploring some possible legislation of my own, based on conversations with constituents during the campaign.
I’ve been thinking about how easy it would be, as a new legislator, to feel like I need to prove myself by introducing bills, and I don’t want to fall into that trap.
Good legislation starts with a real problem, then asks whether a new law is actually the right way to solve it.
That means understanding what’s happening, who is affected, what is causing the problem, and what else might change if we act.
Property tax is a good example.
If voters approve the 50% residential property tax initiative in November, the Legislature will have to deal with the effects on schools, counties, towns and other local services.
That will require more than a quick fix.
I think this is also part of restoring confidence in how the Legislature does its work.
Do the homework.
Ask the hard questions.
Understand the tradeoffs.
Listen to the people who will live with the results.
Passing more bills isn’t the measure of a good session.

January will be here before we know it, and I'm trying to make good use of the time between now and then to prepare for the job I've been nominated to do.
I'm following the legislative committees that meet between sessions.
A lot of that work grows out of the last legislative session.
Some issues needed more study, some questions weren't resolved, and some things simply need a closer look before lawmakers take them up again.
Those committees are also developing proposals that may come before us in 2027.
I'm following work on property taxes, education and school finance, elections, energy, health care, state spending and local government.
That means reading committee materials and recent legislation, looking at what's been tried before and talking with current legislators and others who know these issues well.
There is no shortage of reading material. 🙂 I'm also building the systems I would use to evaluate legislation, track bills and amendments and follow fiscal impacts.
Just as important, I don't want what I hear from constituents to simply land in an inbox somewhere.
I want to connect those conversations to the issues I'm working on and be able to report back on what happened.
The primary is behind us, but the questions Wyoming Republicans have been asking about spending, taxes, property rights, elections, individual liberty and the proper role of government are still very much in front of us.
Those issues don't belong to one candidate or one caucus.
We're not always going to agree on the answer.
That's just reality when you're dealing with complex, layered issues and systems.
If I'm elected in November, my job will be to understand the problem we're trying to solve and what the Constitution and current law require, then look hard at the costs, consequences and whether a proposal actually makes things better.
Over the next several months, I'll share some of what I'm following and working through, including some of the questions I have along the way.
And please keep telling me what you're seeing and thinking, too. ...
