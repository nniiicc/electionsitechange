Data Centers & AI: How and Where Matters
You already use AI every day
It's probably on the phone in your hand right now — running your searches, your maps, your bank's fraud alerts, your kids' homework help.
This technology isn't coming; it's here.
And the race to lead it will decide whether America or China writes the rules for the next century.
We can't turn back the clock.
And we can't afford to lose.
That race runs on data centers, and they are going to get built.
So the real question isn't whether.
It's how, and where — and who gets a say.
That's the fight Ron Kokinda is taking on.
Ron's Bottom Line
A data center that can't survive an honest, public look at its full impact doesn't deserve to be built here.
The community decides - not Lansing, not the utilities, not the money.
Ron isn't anti–data center.
He's anti–getting steamrolled.
Done right, these projects can bring investment without dumping the costs and risks on your family.
Done in the dark, they leave communities with higher bills, drained water, noise, and broken promises.
The difference is entirely in the terms.

Informed Consent, Not Engineered Consent
Ron isn't asking anyone to take these projects on faith.
He's insisting on a process that gives every resident a clear line of sight into the real impacts - good and bad - before anything is signed.
Here's how he'd hold developers to it:
- Bills: Independent, public rate-impact studies.
Full cost recovery from the data center.
Any system savings flow to lower rates for everyone.
- Water: Public disclosure of projected use and cooling technology, with independent hydrological review so residents can judge the watershed impact themselves.
- Noise: Independent acoustic studies with enforceable property-line limits, published early - not after the concrete is poured.
- Secrecy: Contested, on-the-record utility contracting for big power loads, with real public access - no ex-parte handling and heavy redactions.
- Land & aftermath: Up-front alternatives analysis (brownfields first), cumulative-impact review, and binding decommissioning and community-benefit commitments with clawbacks if promises are broken.
- The ledger: Honest accounting of incentives versus permanent jobs and long-term costs, so the community can see whether the net is actually positive.
Why This Wins
When people can see that their rates are protected, their water is safe, the noise is contained, and the risks land on the developer instead of the public, opposition stops being the default.
A well-designed project can earn genuine support - and anyone still opposed is arguing the merits, not fighting suspicion.
That clear line of sight is the whole difference between projects that breed lasting distrust and projects a community can actually get behind.
Ron chooses daylight.
