Assessment of the Logan River Watershed Project Draft Environmental Impact Statement Patrick Belmont, PhD I.
Introduction I care deeply about this place we get to call home.
Not only as a hydrologist who has spent 25+ years studying rivers, watersheds, floods, droughts, and ecological change, but as an outdoor recreationalist, a gardener, a community organizer, and someone who hopes to leave the next several generations a Cache Valley that is as healthy, resilient, and beautiful as possible.
For that reason, I have spent considerable time reviewing the Logan River Watershed Project Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS), attending public meetings, speaking with hundreds of community members, and listening to both supporters and critics of the project.
I want to begin by acknowledging the enormous amount of work that has gone into this proposal.
The project team has clearly identified legitimate challenges facing Cache Valley.
Aging irrigation infrastructure, flood risk, maintenance costs, water supply reliability concerns, and instream flows are all real issues that deserve serious attention.
We are decades overdue in modernizing Logan's century-old practice of using streets and gutters as irrigation canals, a system that now carries toxic pollutants (oil, rubber, heavy metals) into our yards and downstream.
Further, both branches of the Logan River have been degraded by more than a century of maximizing water diversion while paying too little attention to the ecological needs of the rivers and open space that support our community.
The people involved in this effort have invested years of work trying to address those concerns.
However, I have several concerns regarding the proposed projects.
Most broadly, my concern is that the options presented reflect an artificially narrow framing of the challenges facing Cache Valley and therefore an artificially narrow set of solutions.
The project team has done a thoughtful job optimizing within the defined framework.
The problem is that the framework itself is incomplete.
I would characterize the proposed projects as rational approaches to modernizing water delivery infrastructure.
However, I believe the deeper question we should be seeking to answer is: How do we build a water system that serves people, restores rivers, reduces demand, strengthens communities, and contributes to the recovery of Great Salt Lake?
I do not believe the proposed alternatives fully answer that question.
In my opinion, an inadequate range of alternatives have been considered.
Rather than building on the faulty premise of the two primary options proposed, I suggest the ‘no action’ option under this current proposal and request that the project team take a step back, evaluate a wider range of options, and come forward again with a wider range of alternatives that are fiscally responsible and better meets the needs of our community.
My comments and suggestions are offered as an invitation to broaden the conversation.
The challenges identified in the DEIS are real.
The proposed solutions deserve careful consideration.
But before making a decision that could shape water management in Cache Valley for the next half century, I believe we should ask whether we have fully explored the range of futures available to us.
II.
A Broader Vision for Cache Valley's Water Future The proposed alternatives are largely variations on a common theme: delivering irrigation water more efficiently while reducing flood risks.
Those are worthwhile goals.
But Cache Valley faces broader challenges.
We are confronting increasing pressure on rivers, aquifers, wetlands, and Great Salt Lake.
Communities continue to grow.
We keep losing agricultural land to low density housing developments.
Infrastructure costs are rising.
More people are looking to rivers, parks, and open spaces as natural infrastructure for recreation, connection, health, and quality of life.
A truly forward-looking watershed project would ask not only how to move water more efficiently, but how to create a healthier relationship between people, water, and place.
The costs of this project are considerable, with over $300 million up-front costs and $2 million ongoing for management and maintenance.
Given the complexity of the engineered infrastructure and escalating costs of labor, materials, and energy, the ongoing cost estimate provided may be a considerable underestimate.
I have heard advocates for the project argue ‘but most of the money won’t be local, it will come from the federal government.’ But of course, those are our tax dollars and they inevitably contribute to the already excessive and rapidly escalating national debt.
With a project of this scale and cost, regardless of where the funds come from, it is essential that we invest to get the most benefits for the greatest number of Cache Valley residents.
The conclusions of the DEIS are largely a function of the objectives and success metrics selected during the planning process.
The preferred alternative may indeed be the best alternative for maximizing irrigation efficiency and pressure irrigation capability, while also attaining some flood risk, recreation, and ecological co-benefits.
The question raised throughout my assessment below is whether those metrics adequately capture the broader public interests at stake.
We would likely end up with a very different set of proposals if the objectives had included reducing outdoor water demand, protecting groundwater recharge, restoring urban river corridors, improving community health, prioritizing recreation, contributing to Great Salt Lake recovery, and increasing long-term urban and agricultural resilience.
The Logan River is part of the Bear River watershed, and the Bear River is central to the future of Great Salt Lake.
Decisions made in Cache Valley influence communities and ecosystems downstream for generations.
In my view, the Logan River Watershed Project should be understood not only as an engineering project, but as a broader water- and community-resilience project, within which engineering has a useful, but not dominant, role to play.
Below, I discuss several technical concerns with the proposed projects and make suggestions for consideration in the broader conversation about the future we want to create together.
III.
Key Technical Concerns 1.
Demand Reduction Is Largely Absent from the Proposed Alternatives Perhaps the most striking technical omission in the DEIS is the absence of any serious demand-reduction alternative.
The proposed alternatives focus extensively on how water is delivered.
They devote far less attention to how much water is actually needed.
We live in a dry part of the world.
We are clearly over-allocated for water rights.
All predictions for the foreseeable future indicate generally drier conditions in northern Utah.
Future plans should reflect those realities.
Before investing more than $300 million in new infrastructure and locking ourselves into $2 million or perhaps more in annual maintenance, I believe the public deserves a more clear and comprehensive understanding of what could be achieved through conservation, landscape transformation, and reduced outdoor water demand.
This is especially relevant given that many Utah communities are actively pursuing waterwise landscaping, conversion from turf-grass to low-water demand perennials, smart irrigation technologies, and other conservation measures.
These programs not only reduce water consumption, but often also benefit pollinators, birds, aesthetic value, and food production.
Several Cache Valley communities have adopted the ordinances necessary for residents to participate in Utah's landscape transformation incentive programs.
Notably, Logan has opted not to participate, thereby making Logan residents ineligible for tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars each year in water conservation incentives.
This raises an important question: Have participating communities fully pursued the lowest-cost conservation opportunities available before proposing one of the largest water infrastructure investments in Cache Valley history?
The DEIS appears to assume that future demand will remain high and that the primary challenge is delivering water more efficiently.
In contrast, a conservation-focused alternative could include: water-wise landscaping incentives, non-functional turf-grass conversion, updated landscaping ordinances, smart irrigation technologies, conservation pricing, public education, and voluntary demand-reduction targets.
In my view, these failures indicate that the current DEIS includes an inadequate range of alternatives and fails to utilize the best available science.
Such measures may not eliminate the need for infrastructure improvements.
But they could substantially reduce the scale, cost, and complexity of those improvements while generating benefits that extend well beyond water savings alone.
In the event that NRCS provides approval to move forward to the design phase, prior to moving forward with detail-oriented design work, I would encourage NRCS to require answers to key questions regarding the general principles that might shed light on how a much more effective residential water demand reduction might be achieved: What percentage of homes will have meters?
Is pressurized water delivered to only new homes or also to existing homes?
What role will water pricing play in demand reduction?
Why would we not require cities benefitting from these projects to have water-efficient landscaping ordinances in place to ensure we are not investing in conservation while simultaneously making the problem worse in new developments?
2.
Public Value and Financial Accountability The preferred alternative would require more than $300 million in initial investment and approximately $2 million annually for operations and maintenance.
That is an extraordinary public investment by any standard.
For that reason, I believe the project should be evaluated not only on whether it improves water delivery, but on whether it maximizes long-term public value.
The preferred alternative provides real and substantial benefits to the South Branch of the Logan River, substantially increasing flows and dissolved oxygen.
This is an important potential benefit.
However, I am concerned that potential benefit will be contingent on an increasingly complex, fragile, and expensive-to-maintain infrastructure.
And to my knowledge, no efforts were made to determine whether similar benefits could be achieved for the South Branch of the Logan for far less cost, greater ecological benefits, and fewer systemic operational vulnerabilities (pumps, pipelines, potential contaminants from Logan Sewer Treatment Lagoons, etc.).
Both alternatives presented are estimated to provide a very modest benefit-to-cost ratio of 1.1.
That is a thin margin of error, and is considerably lower than many other projects attempting to achieve similar outcomes.
What if construction or maintenance costs are higher than projected?
What if pressurized irrigation participation is lower than expected?
What if energy costs to run pumps are higher than anticipated?
A substantial body of scientific literature has demonstrated that healthy rivers, riparian corridors, and open canals provide measurable benefits to communities, including improved physical and mental health, increased recreation, enhanced property values, flood mitigation, wildlife habitat, urban cooling, and improved water quality (Hartig et al., 2014; ).
These benefits are increasingly recognized as forms of natural infrastructure that complement, and in some cases reduce the need for, more expensive engineered solutions.
The DEIS does a reasonable job describing engineering benefits.
It is less persuasive in demonstrating that the preferred alternative provides the greatest overall benefit to the broader community when compared against potentially lower-cost, lower-maintenance, and more conservation-oriented alternatives.
A project of this scale should clearly answer several questions: Who benefits directly?
What are the long-term risks and costs, and who bears those?
How sensitive are projected benefits to growth assumptions?
How sensitive are they to future irrigation demand or water availability?
How sensitive are they to construction or maintenance cost escalation?
How sensitive are they to changing water-use patterns over the next fifty years?
In my opinion, the DEIS fails, to an unacceptable degree, in addressing these key questions.
Public investment in water, rivers, parks, flood protection, and resilience is both appropriate and necessary.
But with a project of this scope and complexity, it is important to question whether this particular investment provides the greatest public value.
Public value should be measured not only in acre-feet delivered or flood damages avoided, but also in healthier rivers, stronger communities, protected aquifers, restored wetlands, expanded recreation opportunities, improved public health, resilience to future uncertainty, and the inheritance left to future generations.
3.
Recreation and Restoration Opportunities The DEIS includes recreation and ecological improvements, and those should be acknowledged.
Yet they remain secondary considerations within a project whose primary focus is water delivery and flood management.
Cache Valley's rivers, wetlands, riparian corridors, and open spaces are forms of natural infrastructure that provide benefits every bit as real as pipes, pumps, and diversion structures.
They improve physical health.
They improve mental health.
They reduce urban heat.
They create habitat.
They attract visitors.
They strengthen community identity.
They provide places where kids can explore, learn, and develop a connection to the natural world.
And in many cases, they reduce flood risks and maintenance costs at the same time.
A project of this scale should more seriously evaluate opportunities to: restore urban river corridors, reconnect rivers with portions of their floodplains, reduce and prevent future development on floodplains, create parks and open space that could be flooded with minimal damage in emergency situations, improve river access, expand trail connections, restore riparian habitat, support urban cooling, and create recreational assets that serve the entire community.
While I can’t offer a comprehensive vision for how recreation could be enhanced across the entire spatial extent of this project, I would argue that far too little consideration of recreational opportunities has been provided in current project plans.
I request a more thorough study, including key members of Cache Valley’s active, engaged, and forwardthinking outdoor recreation community and local experts in ecological restoration and bioregional planning.
4.
Return Flows and Seepage One of the central assumptions underlying the project is that reducing seepage creates water savings.
At the scale of an individual canal or irrigation company, that may be true.
At the scale of the watershed, however, those water delivery efficiencies may have detrimental unintended consequences.
Water that seeps into the ground is not lost.
It may recharge aquifers, support springs and wetlands, sustain mature trees, contribute to late-season streamflow, or provide water that is later used by downstream communities and ecosystems.
This distinction is important because the DEIS frequently refers to "water savings" without always distinguishing between reduced diversions and reduced consumptive use.
Those are not the same thing.
A growing body of research (Grafton and others, 2018; Morrisett and others, 2023; Ward & Pulido-Velazquez, 2008) has shown that efficiency projects often do not reduce consumptive use and can sometimes reduce return flows in ways that create unintended consequences elsewhere in a watershed.
In some cases, water that was previously considered "lost" was actually performing important ecological or hydrologic functions.
The DEIS acknowledges some of these issues, but I do not believe it fully evaluates them.
Before large portions of the system are converted from open conveyance to buried and pressurized infrastructure, the public deserves a clearer understanding of how those changes may affect groundwater recharge, springs, wetlands, riparian vegetation, downstream users, and long-term watershed resilience.
5.
Protections for the North Branch of the Logan River The North Branch of the Logan River (often called the Little Logan) is a natural water course that has been altered in places.
Questions remain regarding whether or not those alterations were done legally, and those questions should be answered before proceeding.
Nevertheless, the North Branch Logan River is one of Cache Valley's most visible and accessible waterways.
It provides ecological, recreational, educational, and cultural benefits that extend far beyond its role in water delivery.
While the DEIS appears to have made some small and uncertain concessions to community members that have been advocating for protection of the North Branch of the Logan River, several critical questions remain unanswered.
Exactly what flows are guaranteed under normal conditions, moderate drought, severe drought, and extreme drought?
Are these enforceable commitments, operational targets, or simply possible outcomes?
How will water quality, habitat, fish and aquatic organisms, recreation, and public enjoyment be affected under each scenario?
Have opportunities for ecological restoration of the North Branch of the Logan River been fully explored?
To what extent might we use this project as an opportunity to reduce past harms that have been done to the North Branch of the Logan River ecosystem?
Given the role this river plays in our community, a project of this scale should seek to improve it.
And those improvements should be protected in perpetuity.
6.
Lagoon Storage and Late-Season Water Use I commend the effort to leverage this proposed project to rehabilitate the defunct Logan Sewer Treatment Lagoons.
However, the proposed use of the storage lagoons deserves additional scrutiny.
Storage is not inherently problematic.
In many situations, it can improve operational flexibility and system reliability.
However, storage can also change the timing and availability of water in ways that increase consumptive use.
Water that currently returns to streams, aquifers, or downstream systems may instead be stored and applied later in the irrigation season.
That may be beneficial from the perspective of water delivery.
It may be less beneficial from the perspective of rivers, wetlands, aquifers, or Great Salt Lake.
The DEIS should more clearly evaluate how proposed storage facilities affect late-season diversions, consumptive use, return flows, and downstream water availability.
I appreciate that the project team appropriately recognized the need to characterize lagoon sludge before converting former wastewater treatment cells into irrigation storage reservoirs.
From Figure 3-2 in appendix E, it appears that large areas of the lagoons were not sampled.
The authors of the DEIS should explain why and what uncertainties the incomplete sample design creates regarding the potential for contamination to exist in the lagoons.
This appears to be a flawed dataset and additional sampling may be needed before the risks associated with using the wastewater treatment cells for irrigation storage can be assessed.
After decades of storing contaminated water, I would expect the sediments to be contaminated with some levels of lead, zinc, copper and other heavy metals, oil and asphalt-derived compounds, microplastics, nutrients, PFAS, pesticides, and herbicides.
The DEIS indicates that no toxins were found in the samples collected.
However, there is no indication that I was able to find in the document of which specific toxins were tested for, nor are the data provided so the public can see the results.
I requested access to the dataset and, as of the time of writing, have been denied.
This raises a clear red flag in transparency.
The DEIS should be required to answer the following: Which analytes were tested?
What detection limits were used?
Were microplastics, heavy metals, hydrocarbon compounds, estrogen-disrupting compounds, pharmaceuticals, and PFAS all measured?
If all of these were measured, the concentrations in each sample should be provided.
If not all these potential contaminants were sampled, additional analyses should be conducted before the project is allowed to move forward.
Unless remediation of those sediments is more completely addressed in the DEIS, I would argue it may represent a failure to disclose cumulative impacts, as contaminated water could have detrimental impacts on the environment, sensitive fish/wildlife, and/or agricultural production.
7.
Long-Term Lock-In Effects Infrastructure shapes behavior.
Once a community invests hundreds of millions of dollars in a particular system, it creates strong incentives to continue using that system for decades.
That reality deserves greater attention in the DEIS.
An underlying assumption of the DEIS appears to be that growth is coming, so we must build infrastructure to support it.
Another legitimate question is: What kinds of growth should this infrastructure encourage?
What type of infrastructure would promote growth that also preserves the quality of life for all residents of Cache Valley?
Many state and local leaders are increasingly recognizing the need to reduce outdoor water demand, encourage water-wise landscaping, and adapt to a drier future.
Yet the proposed alternatives appear largely built around the assumption that existing irrigation patterns will continue indefinitely.
If those assumptions prove incorrect, future generations could inherit infrastructure that is expensive to operate, expensive to maintain, and increasingly misaligned with the needs of the community.
The proposed alternatives appear designed to preserve and expand reliable pressure irrigation service throughout a rapidly urbanizing portion of Cache Valley.
While the DEIS treats this as a benefit, it does not appear to evaluate whether expanded secondary-water infrastructure may also encourage additional low-density development patterns, larger irrigated landscapes, and continued conversion of agricultural land and open space.
Decisions about water infrastructure are, in practice, decisions about the future pattern of growth.
Once development occurs, those landscapes are rarely recovered as farms, wetlands, wildlife habitat, or open space.
Further, this type of low-density housing locks us into decades of escalating traffic and air pollution problems in the valley.
From my conversations with people in the valley, I believe this is not the type of growth most people want to see, let alone subsidize, which both proposed alternatives inevitably do.
Given the scale of public investment involved, the relationship between water infrastructure, landuse decisions, landscape water demand, and long-term growth patterns deserves much more explicit consideration.
Insofar as these issues are not addressed in the DEIS, I believe it qualifies as a failure to disclose cumulative impacts.
This is not an argument against infrastructure investment.
It is an argument for ensuring that today's investments remain valuable under a wide range of future conditions, including more thoughtful growth, changing landscaping preferences, reduced water availability, and evolving community priorities.
8.
Great Salt Lake The Logan River is part of the Bear River system.
The Bear River is the largest source of inflow to Great Salt Lake.
For that reason, I was surprised by how little attention Great Salt Lake receives within the DEIS.
The Great Salt Lake is one of the most important ecological and economic assets in the American West.
Its wetlands and open waters support millions of migratory birds, thousands of jobs, billions of dollars in economic activity, and contribute to the snowpack that sustains much of Utah's recreation economy.
Yet the lake has declined dramatically in recent decades as water has been diverted from the rivers that sustain it.
Scientists, public health experts, business leaders, and elected officials from across the political spectrum have warned that continued decline threatens wildlife, air quality, public health, and long-term economic stability.
As more lakebed becomes exposed, toxic dust containing arsenic and other contaminants can be carried into surrounding communities.
This dust contamination has recently been demonstrated to be a significant threat to agriculture as recent research showed accumulation of Li, Be, Na, As, Sr, Sb, and U in cabbage leaves exposed to Great Salt Lake dust (Blakowski et al., 2026).
Every major water project in the Great Salt Lake watershed should be evaluated not only for its local benefits, but also for its implications for the long-term health of the lake.
The DEIS acknowledges Great Salt Lake as a potential downstream beneficiary of water conservation, but largely treats that benefit as incidental.
In my view, that is not just a missed opportunity, it is a substantial oversight that qualifies as a failure to disclose cumulative impacts, a lack of utilizing the best available science, and indicates that an inadequate range of alternatives has been presented in the DEIS.
Recent legislative changes have created new mechanisms for leasing, dedicating, and marketing water for environmental purposes, including Great Salt Lake recovery.
Water conserved through projects like this may represent not only an opportunity to restore one of Utah's defining ecosystems, but also a potential source of economic value for Cache Valley communities and water users.
A project expected to conserve thousands of acre-feet annually (and could likely conserve considerably more) should explicitly evaluate multiple possible uses for that water, including local irrigation reliability, instream flows, groundwater recharge, wetland support, and Great Salt Lake recovery.
