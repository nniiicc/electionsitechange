It's Time We Act Like It.
Sep 10 Written By Wyatt Nordvik Unless you live in Amador or Calaveras Counties, you've probably never heard about the 2025 Tiger Fire.
Last summer, that fire started downslope above Pioneer and ran uphill fast through heavy brush, with the community of Buckhorn right in its path.
Then it reached a fuel break, and it did what everyone hopes a wildfire will do.
The flames dropped out of the treetops and stayed on the ground, where crews and aircraft could reach them.
Not a single home was lost.
For seasons before that fire, crews did the hard, unglamorous work of clearing brush, thinning trees, and maintaining a fuel break on Bureau of Land Management land.
Nobody filmed the months of planning, cutting, and hauling that made it possible.
Nobody held a press conference when that work was finished.
Good prevention is invisible.
You only notice it when it is missing.
The fuel break that stopped the Tiger Fire cost $830,000, a hefty sum until you consider that over 1,000 people and over 400 homes were threatened.
Instead of potentially hundreds of families being forced to rebuild and insurance companies paying out millions in benefits that would have further raised insurance premiums on all Californian households, everyone walked away unscathed.
These projects should be the rule, not the exception.
The forests around us are overloaded.
For a hundred years, federal policy has been to stop every fire as fast as we can.
But a natural Californian forest is built to burn from time to time.
Small fires clear underbrush, thin out small trees, destroy harmful pests like the bark beetle, and create space for new plant life to grow.
When you take that away, the fuels stack up, year after year, leading to larger, hotter, and more catastrophic fires.
The strategy intended to protect us has been loading the gun.
The answer is clear, and we already know it works.
More fuel breaks, more thinning, and more prescribed fire.
Tribes throughout the Sierra Nevada have shown the way for centuries with cultural burning, practices that continue to this day.
Researchers at UC Davis put numbers to it.
After examining nearly 300 wildfires across eleven western states, they found that forests treated before a fire arrives are far more likely to slow it down, reduce damage, and protect communities.
Every dollar invested in fuel treatments returned $3.73 in avoided losses.
Those losses are a major reason our insurance premiums are so high.
We need funding to reach communities before fire season, not after.
And the people who lead the work should be the ones closest to it: elected officials, tribal leaders, nonprofits, and wildfire experts, in partnership with the federal and state crews stationed here.
We also need funding to maintain these projects every year, which costs only about 10% of the initial work.
A fuel break nobody maintains can turn back into brush within a few years.
Tying annual maintenance to the original grant keeps that investment from going to waste.
California has the best firefighters in the world.
They will keep answering every call because that is who they are.
We can help them by putting our tax dollars toward aggressively preparing for wildfire season every year.
This is what common sense wildfire policy looks like.
The same tax dollars invested in wildfire defense can protect our families and our homes, reduce fire insurance premiums, create new local jobs, help our firefighters, and support the natural environment.
For wildfires, the best outcome is the one you never hear about.
Another fire that never becomes a name you remember.
Prevention pays.
It's time we act like it.
