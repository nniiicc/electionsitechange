They told us I-194 would keep dark money out of Montana politics.
That's the pitch.
It's not what the law does.
I-194 would bar corporations, LLCs, family farms and ranches organized as businesses, trade groups, and nonprofits from spending a dime, or "anything of value," on candidates, parties, or ballot issues.
Miss that last part and you miss the trap.
This isn't just about Super PACs.
It's about who still gets to speak when someone comes after your industry, your school levy, your town.
So, who's still allowed to spend?
Individuals.
No new limit at all.
A billionaire can write personal checks until the ads bury you.
But a Montana family that organized their operation as an LLC, the way almost every small business is told to for basic liability protection, can't answer from the business they built.
Political committees stay legal too.
The money doesn't disappear.
It just changes clothes.
That's the betrayal.
They sold you a cleanup.
They wrote you a gag order.
Dark money didn't start with corporate checks routed through PACs, and I-194 won't end it.
A lot of it already moves through 501(c)(4) groups that never have to name a single donor.
Look at Stacie Anderson's A Better Big Sky.
It's a 501(c)(4), not a charity.
Gifts to it aren't tax-deductible, and you'll never see a clean list of who's really behind it.
Groups like that have already poured money into Montana politics, into voter operations, into the exact fights that decide who holds power here.
I-194 lists nonprofits on paper and calls it a day.
Wealthy individuals stay untouched.
Committees stay open for business.
You, the voter, end up no closer to knowing who paid for the mailer in your box.
Now look at who's selling you the broom.
The Yes campaign itself is largely funded through a 501(c)(4), the Transparent Election Initiative, that hides its own donors.
They're asking for your vote against secret money while quietly running on secret money.
Montana businesses aren't wrong to feel like they're being set up.
A restaurant couldn't lift a finger to help pass a public-safety levy that keeps its town safe.
A ranch couldn't spend a cent fighting a ballot measure aimed straight at its livelihood.
Meanwhile, the people targeting them are still free to spend whatever they want, as individuals.
That's not handing power back to Montanans.
That's taking their voice away and calling it reform.
If this were really just about stopping corporations from buying off candidates, the fight would look completely different, and industry groups have said so themselves.
They're fighting I-194 because ballot issues are how their livelihoods land on the chopping block, and this law would tell them to sit down and stay quiet while it happens.
We've heard "reform" before.
We know exactly what a stacked fight feels like from the inside.
Don't let a slogan talk you out of thinking it through.
Vote no on I-194.
