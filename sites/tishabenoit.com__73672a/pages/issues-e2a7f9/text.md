TISHA BENOIT Here’s the Plan DEBATE TALKING POINTS: I’m running to represent the people of Arizona, not a political party.
This campaign is about bringing people together around shared concerns, practical solutions, and a government that listens.
No matter how you are registered or whether you identify with any party at all, you deserve to be heard, respected, and represented.
ECONOMY — Cost of Living, Inflation & Jobs When I talk to people across Arizona, I hear the same thing over and over: “I’m working, but it still feels like I’m falling behind.” That is the economic issue we should be talking about.
Government can tell us inflation is improving, but families experience the economy at the grocery store, at the gas pump, when they pay their insurance bill, and when the rent or mortgage comes due.
Arizona’s unemployment rate was about 4.9% in August, but simply having a job isn't enough if that paycheck doesn't cover basic living expenses.
My approach is neither Republican nor Democrat.
It's practical.
First, we need to stop policies that unnecessarily increase the cost of everyday goods, energy and housing.
Second, I want to make it easier to start and grow a small business.
Small businesses create opportunity, but they're buried under paperwork, regulations and rising costs.
Third, we need workforce training tied to actual jobs: construction, healthcare, advanced manufacturing, technology and skilled trades.
Not everyone needs a four-year degree.
I would support apprenticeships, vocational education and micro-grants that allow someone to gain a marketable skill quickly.
And Washington must recognize something very simple: government spending has consequences.
We cannot spend endlessly and then act surprised when Americans feel the consequences.
The economy should work for the nurse, the teacher, the small-business owner, the retiree and the young family trying to buy their first home.
My economic philosophy is simple: make it easier to work, easier to build, easier to start a business and easier for families to keep more of what they earn.
HOUSING Housing has become one of the clearest examples of how government can fail people from several directions at once.
Young people wonder whether they'll ever be able to buy a home.
Seniors worry about rising property taxes and rents.
Working families may have good jobs and still struggle to find something they can afford.
I don't believe the answer is simply another giant federal housing program.
We have to increase supply.
That means working with state and local governments to reduce unnecessary barriers that make housing more expensive and slower to build.
It means encouraging starter homes, townhomes, smaller homes, accessory dwelling units and housing designed for seniors who want to downsize.
We should look at federal land where appropriate and determine whether portions can responsibly support housing and infrastructure.
But I also believe we need to protect existing neighborhoods and recognize that Arizona cannot approve unlimited development without considering water, roads, schools and public safety.
Those two ideas can coexist.
I also want greater transparency around institutional investors buying large numbers of single-family homes.
Families shouldn't have to compete on an uneven playing field with enormous investment funds.
And homelessness requires more than simply putting someone into temporary housing.
Mental health, addiction treatment, employment services and transitional housing need to work together.
Housing policy doesn't have to be ideological.
Build more.
Remove unnecessary barriers.
Protect Arizona resources.
Help people move toward ownership rather than permanent dependency.
Because the American dream shouldn't become something only wealthy Americans can afford.
WATER AND CLIMATE In Arizona, water isn't an environmental talking point.
Water is economic security.
Water is housing security.
And frankly, water is Arizona's future.
As of late August, Lake Mead was only about #% full and Lake Powell about #% full.
Arizona is already transitioning from simply banking excess Colorado River water toward using stored reserves to help manage shortages.
So I don't think we have the luxury of turning water into another partisan fight.
I support conservation, but conservation alone isn't enough.
Arizona needs an aggressive water innovation strategy.
That means wastewater recycling, aquifer recharge, better agricultural technology, leak reduction, capturing stormwater where practical, and researching desalination and new water-generation technologies.
Agriculture matters, cities matter and economic development matters.
The goal shouldn't be pitting one against another.
I also believe future large developments and water-intensive industries should demonstrate where their water is coming from before taxpayers inherit the problem later.
On climate, I take a similar approach.
I believe in science, innovation and environmental responsibility.
But I don't believe working families should be punished with dramatically higher energy bills in the name of environmental policy.
We should pursue solar, nuclear, geothermal, storage, efficiency and emerging technologies while maintaining a reliable electrical grid.
Arizona can become a laboratory for water technology and next-generation energy.
My position is conservation without extremism, innovation without ideology and a commitment that Arizona families come first when we make decisions about our natural resources.
BORDER SECURITY AND IMMIGRATION I believe two things can be true at the same time.
A sovereign country has an absolute right and responsibility to secure its border.
And America can still be a country that welcomes legal immigration.
For too long, Washington has forced Americans into choosing between those two ideas.
I reject that choice.
Border security means adequate Border Patrol personnel, technology, physical barriers where they're effective, surveillance, roads and infrastructure, and stopping fentanyl, trafficking and criminal organizations from entering this country.
Arizona understands this better than Washington because we live with the consequences.
But our immigration system itself also needs reform.
Creating opportunities instead of dependency on welfare empowers people to live with dignity and prosperity in their own countries, reducing the need to enter the United States illegally.
Someone who wants to immigrate legally shouldn't have to navigate a dysfunctional system for years while people who ignore the process move ahead of them.
We need faster immigration courts, faster decisions on asylum claims, stronger verification systems and a modern legal immigration system that responds to America's workforce needs.
And Congress needs to do its job.
Immigration should not remain permanently broken because both political parties find it useful during elections.
People who commit violent crimes or pose national-security threats should be removed.
At the same time, enforcement should be professional, constitutional and humane.
This shouldn't be about being anti-immigrant or pro-open border.
I'm neither.
I'm pro-legal immigration, pro-border security and pro-common sense.
Secure the border.
Fix the legal system.
Stop trafficking and fentanyl.
And give people who follow the rules a functioning pathway.
Most Americans are somewhere in the middle on this issue.
So am I.
HEALTHCARE I've spent much of my professional life in healthcare, and one thing is painfully obvious: America doesn't really have a healthcare system.
We have a sick-care payment system.
We need to end the chronic disease epidemic.
We spend enormous amounts of money, yet patients still delay care because they don't know what something will cost.
In 2025, approximately 26.7 million Americans went the entire year without health insurance.
I was one of them.
But insurance coverage is only part of the problem.
You can have insurance and still struggle to afford the deductible, medication or specialist visit.
I want healthcare reform centered around four things.
First: price transparency.
Patients should know the price before receiving routine care.
Second: competition .
Hospitals, insurers, pharmaceutical companies and pharmacy benefit managers should not be protected from competition.
Third: prevention .
Medicare and other programs spend enormous amounts treating chronic illness after it develops.
We should place much greater emphasis on nutrition, metabolic health, early screening and evidence-based preventive care.
And fourth: patient choice .
Patients should be able to choose qualified practitioners and treatments without Washington unnecessarily standing between the patient and their healthcare provider.
I don't support throwing out everything we have and starting over with a massive government-controlled system.
But maintaining the status quo isn't acceptable either.
My goal is not government-run healthcare or corporate-run healthcare.
It's patient-centered healthcare.
More transparency.
More prevention.
More competition.
More choice.
And ultimately, healthcare that ordinary Americans can actually afford to use.
EDUCATION Arizona has fallen to 50th on the national education lists.
We need to fix that now!
Education should prepare a young person to build a life.
There’s no reason why Arizona couldn’t be in the top 10 when we are leading the way in technology and engineering with some of the best companies at forefront of these industries.
Somewhere along the way, we started acting as though success meant every student following exactly the same path.
I don't believe that.
Some students should go to universities.
Others may become electricians, nurses, welders, coders, dental hygienists, plumbers, technicians or entrepreneurs.
Every one of those paths has value.
Arizona should have excellent public schools, strong charter schools, career and technical education and meaningful options for parents.
I support school choice, but I also believe public schools must be strong because the majority of our children will continue to attend them.
Teachers should spend their time teaching, not drowning in bureaucracy, or spending their own money on school supplies.
We need to reallocate funding to support our teachers who are preparing the next generation of our future.
And we need much stronger financial literacy, civics and practical life skills.
Before a student graduates, they should understand credit, compound interest, taxes, budgeting, student loans and basic entrepreneurship.
I also want employers partnering directly with high schools and community colleges so education reflects the jobs that actually exist.
Washington's role should be limited.
Most education decisions belong with parents, teachers, school boards and states, not federal administrations.
But Congress can support apprenticeships, career training and greater transparency around federal education spending.
My standard isn't whether an education policy came from the left or the right.
It's much simpler: Can a child read?
Can they think critically?
Do they understand their choices?
And when they graduate, do they have a skill that allows them to support themselves?
If the answer is yes, we're succeeding.
DATA CENTERS Arizona has become one of America's major technology hubs, and that's good for our economy.
But growth has to make sense.
Data centers are a perfect example.
They support artificial intelligence, cloud computing and technologies our economy increasingly depends upon.
But they also consume enormous amounts of electricity, and some facilities can require significant water and infrastructure.
APS says an average-sized data center in its territory can require as much electricity as roughly 64,000 homes , and Phoenix ranks second in North America for proposed data-center development.
SRP similarly says large-scale facilities can consume hundreds of megawatts and that data centers are projected to be its fastest-growing customer segment.
So my position is not, “Stop data centers.” My position is: growth should pay for growth.
If a billion-dollar corporation needs new substations, transmission infrastructure or significant water resources, Arizona families shouldn't unknowingly subsidize those costs through their utility bills.
We should require transparency regarding water consumption, electricity requirements, backup generators, noise and infrastructure needs before projects are approved.
And I'd encourage next-generation facilities that use recycled water, closed-loop cooling, renewable and nuclear energy, battery storage and technologies that dramatically reduce resource consumption.
Arizona should absolutely welcome innovation.
But economic development should improve the quality of life of the people already living here.
Technology should serve Arizona.
Arizona shouldn't become a utility company for the technology industry.
That's the balance I would bring to Congress.
FEDERAL SPENDING AND NATIONAL DEBT This may be the least exciting subject in Washington, but it may eventually become one of the most important.
The federal government has normalized spending far more money than it collects.
For fiscal year 2026, the Congressional Budget Office projected approximately $7.4 trillion in spending against $5.6 trillion in revenue.
That’s a $1.9 trillion deficit.
And net interest on the debt alone is projected to exceed $1 trillion this year.
Think about that.
We're spending roughly a trillion dollars just servicing debt and, money that can't fix a bridge, educate a child, protect the border or help a veteran.
And both parties contributed to this.
Republicans cannot call themselves fiscally conservative while ignoring deficits under Republican administrations.
Democrats cannot pretend every problem can be solved with another federal spending program.
As an Independent, I don't owe either side an excuse.
I would require major legislation to clearly identify what it costs and how we're going to pay for it.
I'd push for aggressive audits of federal programs, elimination of duplicative programs and stronger efforts against fraud and improper payments.
I also want a bipartisan long-term commission addressing our fiscal trajectory before a crisis forces decisions on us.
I don't support suddenly cutting benefits people spent their lives paying into.
But refusing to discuss long-term solvency isn't compassion.
It's avoidance.
We inherited this country.
We don't own it.
We have a responsibility to leave the next generation opportunity, not simply a stack of unpaid bills.
FOREIGN POLICY My foreign policy begins with a very simple question: How does this protect the security, prosperity and freedom of the American people?
I don't believe America should retreat from the world.
And I don't believe America should police every conflict in the world.
Those are the two extremes.
America should maintain the strongest military in the world, not because we want war, but because strength helps prevent war.
Our allies matter.
NATO matters.
Our relationships in Asia matter.
But alliances have responsibilities on both sides, and our partners must contribute meaningfully to their own defense.
China is both an economic competitor and our most significant long-term strategic challenge.
We should protect American technology and critical infrastructure, strengthen domestic manufacturing, protect semiconductor supply chains and reduce dangerous dependencies without pretending we're going to completely disconnect the world's two largest economies.
Regarding conflicts such as Ukraine and the Middle East, I believe Congress has a responsibility to ask hard questions before committing American money or weapons: What is the objective?
What is the cost?
What is the exit strategy?
And how does this advance American interests?
We should support our allies while continuously pursuing diplomacy and preventing regional wars from becoming global wars.
And Congress must reclaim its constitutional responsibility over war.
Presidents: Republican or Democrat, shouldn't have unlimited authority to involve America indefinitely in foreign conflicts.
My philosophy is peace through strength, diplomacy before war, strong alliances and America strategically engaged without becoming the world's permanent policeman.
That's not isolationism.
And it's not interventionism.
It's responsible American leadership.
America’s strength and prosperity are powered by energy and we have everything we need right here at home.
It’s time to fully develop our resources, from oil and natural gas to emerging energy innovations, and stop limiting our own potential.
True energy independence means lowering costs for families, creating high-paying jobs for Arizona workers, and strengthening our national security.
It means we’re no longer relying on unstable foreign sources or policies that drive prices up.
In Congress, I’ll work to remove unnecessary barriers, support responsible and renewable energy development, and invest in the infrastructure needed to keep America strong and self-reliant.
