Fusion is the process that powers the sun.
Instead of splitting heavy atoms (like nuclear fission plants do today), fusion combines light atoms — releasing enormous amounts of energy.
The most developed form of fusion uses two forms of hydrogen: Deuterium (found naturally in seawater) Tritium (produced from lithium inside the reactor) When they fuse: Deuterium + Tritium → Helium + Neutron + Energy That energy is captured as heat and converted into electricity.
Where Does the Fuel Come From?
Deuterium is extracted from water.
Tritium is bred from lithium inside the reactor.
There is enough deuterium in Earth’s oceans to power civilization for millions of years.
And the fuel required is astonishingly small: A 400-megawatt fusion plant would consume less than a couple of tons of fuel per year — not millions of tons like coal.
In terms of burned fuel, the amount is measured in tens of kilograms per year .
That’s why you often hear the phrase: “A year’s worth of fusion fuel could fit in the back of a pickup truck.” That statement is essentially true.
How Does a Fusion Reactor Actually Work?
Fusion requires three things: Extremely high temperatures — about 100–150 million degrees Celsius (hotter than the sun’s core) Strong magnetic confinement — powerful superconducting magnets keep the plasma from touching the reactor walls Energy input to start the reaction — external heating systems bring the plasma to fusion temperature Once the plasma is hot and dense enough, fusion reactions release energy.
That energy becomes heat, which: Boils water Spins a turbine Generates electricity Early fusion plants will look similar to other large thermal power plants — except they won’t burn fuel or emit carbon.
What About Hydrogen-Boron Fusion?
Some companies are pursuing an advanced version called hydrogen-boron fusion .
Instead of producing high-energy neutrons, it produces mostly charged helium particles.
In theory, that energy could be converted directly into electricity without steam turbines — potentially increasing efficiency and reducing waste.
But hydrogen-boron fusion requires much more extreme conditions and is likely further from commercialization.
What Are the Energy Inputs?
Fusion is not “free energy.” To initiate and sustain fusion: Large magnetic systems must be powered Plasma heating systems inject energy Cryogenic systems keep magnets superconducting Tritium breeding systems must operate reliably The challenge is achieving: Net energy gain — where the reactor produces more energy than it consumes.
Major scientific milestones have already demonstrated net energy gain in experimental conditions.
The next step is engineering plants that can operate continuously and economically.
