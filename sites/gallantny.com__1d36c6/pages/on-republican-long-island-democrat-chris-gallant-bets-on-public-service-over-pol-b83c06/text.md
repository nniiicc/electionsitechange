Chris Gallant, an openly gay Democrat running for New York’s 1st Congressional District, which includes the eastern side of Long Island, is trying to capitalize on what he sees as growing frustration with the Republican Party and its ability to deliver for everyday Americans.
The Blade sat down with Gallant to discuss his move from civil servant to the fight for public office, how he has been personally impacted by the changes from the Trump administration, and the recent release of photos from his past that have brought the national spotlight his way.
Gallant has a long record of public service that, on paper, would make him an unconventional but compelling candidate for a heavily Republican district.
His résumé includes New York Army National Guard service that began when he was 17, multiple overseas deployments, including multiple tours in Kuwait, work as an FAA air traffic controller, union leadership and service as a volunteer firefighter.
That record of service is central to how Gallant describes himself — particularly as he navigates the unusual combination of being an openly gay candidate, a military veteran, and a Democrat running in a district where Republicans have historically held the advantage.
“My fiancé and I both serve in the military and we built our relationship through trust, timing, and a lot of miles between us,” his campaign website says, highlighting the idea of family values that has become a central part of winning the area.
“I’ve been openly gay in the military since just after ‘Don’t Ask, Don’t Tell’ ended, and I’ve learned to speak plainly, hold the line, and back up what I say.
I’ve also learned how to listen — and how to bring people with very different views to the table and actually make progress.” Gallant said that record, rather than his sexual orientation, is what should define his candidacy.
“I think who I am as a person is an asset,” he told the Blade when asked if his sexuality has played any role in his decision to run.
“I’m not running because I’m gay.
I’m running, and,’Oh, I’m gay.’ It’s part of my identity.
It’s just a part of who I am.
But it’s not necessarily on the table because that shouldn’t make a difference on how I’m going to respond in Congress or how I’m going to run into a burning building.
I don’t think those things necessarily matter so much.” For Gallant, the campaign is ultimately about something broader than identity politics.
He said he is running because he wants to restore Americans’ confidence in a government that he says has become less about its people and more about the wealthy leaders in charge.
“I’ve always said that part of the reason why I am running for Congress is because I want to restore faith in our government,” he said.
“We’re all drastically exhausted with what’s going on in this world, and particularly with Donald Trump being in office, [his] type of politics, and the way he works, essentially.
When Biden got into office after Trump won, it was like the release of a pressure cooker… the pressure cooker is just even worse now this second time around.” He also explained that his experience working inside the federal government has shaped how he believes Washington should approach its problems.
Gallant pointed specifically to the Trump administration’s efforts to reduce the size of government and the role of the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), arguing that cutting government spending should not mean indiscriminate cuts.
“I understand that we as a nation need to save money, or at least fix the budget or balance it.
We are getting further and further in debt … The budget is always a huge issue and something that we definitely 100% need to fix.
But we need to do it with experts and, you know, attack these issues with a scalpel, not a chainsaw.” The chainsaw Gallant mentioned is a clear reference to controversial Trump supporter Elon Musk, who announced he would be heading the highly politicized DOGE agency, where he, the first-ever trillionaire, brandished a shiny chainsaw symbolic of them cutting government spending with no regard for the impact on everyday Americans.
That philosophy, of scalpel over chainsaw, is also part of Gallant’s argument to voters who may be more accustomed to voting Republican.
He does not present himself as a traditional politician, but rather as someone whose career outside politics could be an asset in Congress.
“I’m not a politician.
This is my first foray into politics, and I think that’s what our politics need.
We need fresh new faces, not career politicians.
Someone who has brand new ideas, not tainted by politics.
So I would say, give me the opportunity to prove myself, because, you know, until I actually get there and I can actually do a job, we won’t know.” Gallant also shared signs that the political landscape on Long Island may be shifting.
He pointed to gains Democrats made across the district last November and said economic concerns could further erode Republican support.
“Last November, in this district we [Democrats] gained 13 and 14 points across the district ..And this district lost by 10 points last year.
So even just in one year, you can tell that there’s a difference.” The swapping of support from the Republican side to Democratic reflects the real concern voters have about the economy — impacted by the war in Iran, tariffs, and growing inflation.
“People are feeling it here in their pockets.
The cost of living here on Long Island is already high, and things are just getting worse.
There’s a lot of promises that were made with this administration that weren’t kept.” Gallant said that frustration has directly translated into support from some voters who previously backed President Donald Trump.
“I know a lot of people who have voted for Donald Trump that have my lawn sign on their front lawn today.
If that’s the change that they’re looking for, I’m going to provide it.” But Gallant’s campaign has been complicated by a very different kind of political issue: the publication of photos from his past that have become a national story.
The photos, which include pictures of Gallant wearing various fetish gear for Mr.
S Leather — including a harness and a collar — were shared with the New York Post .
Gallant has consistently rejected the idea that he should be ashamed of the images or that they undermine his qualifications for public office since their Sept.
13 publication.
Instead, he has argued that his record of service should be considered alongside a private chapter of his life from #ago.
“I mean, look, I’m totally not ashamed of who I am as a person.
Those pictures were taken in my private life.
I can still run into a burning building faster than most people.
I can still fly a helicopter.
And those are from #ago, right?
I built an entire life based around my commitment to the military and the Army National Guard.” Rather than allowing the controversy to define him, Gallant said he hopes it can become part of a broader conversation about privacy and the consequences of living in a digital age — and indicated that this sort of scenario would become something semi-regular for those who grew up with technology.
“The reality is that we live in a digital age.This is what our generation is going to be stuck with.
I laugh all the time because this is the reason why Congress and the Senate are about to die in their chairs.
They didn’t have cameras and pictures and photos like that.” “If I have to be the person to wave the banner now to fight and advocate for not only our community but anybody that could be put in this situation, that realizes now that we’re living in a digital age and this is what the future is going to be.
It’s, you know, everything’s left online now.” That argument extends beyond his own campaign.
Gallant said he worries that the prospect of having a digital footprint scrutinized could discourage highly qualified and passionate potential public servants from seeking office.
“I understand that getting into public life means that the rest of your life becomes public.
I get it, I do.
But I also feel bad because there’s a lot of good people out there wanting to do a job in the public world, and they’re just afraid of what their digital footprint might have on them or might—what might exist because we’ve done things when we’re 12 or 15, or 20, or 30, or whatever.” “I think living in this digital world, I would love to advocate for people to say, ‘How do we prevent some of these things from coming out into the public view so people can still maintain some kind of a private life?’” For an openly gay candidate, the issue also carries a particular significance.
Gallant said his experience has made him more interested in advocating for other LGBTQ people who may hesitate to enter public life because of what could eventually surface online.
His own LGBTQ policy priorities are relatively focused.
He said adding further protections for same-sex marriage in federal law would be one of the issues he would want to pursue if elected, particularly as questions about the Supreme Court’s future approach to marriage equality continue to loom.
“My partner, who’s my fiancé, is currently deployed.
We got engaged before he left in December because if the question came up to the Supreme Court– whether gay marriage was going to be a thing– we had to question whether, when he got back from deployment after Christmas this year, whether we were actually going to be able to legally get married.” “I mean, these are questions that shouldn’t even be questions anymore.
So if we can put something like [codifying same-sex marriage] into law, where it’s just not a Supreme Court decision and it can get overturned, like Roe v.
Wade, then let’s do it.” Gallant’s opponent, Rep.
Nick LaLota (R-N.Y.), is the two-term incumbent and has a mixed record on LGBTQ rights.
LaLota has been vocal in his opposition to overturning Obergefell v.
Hodges , the Supreme Court case that established the constitutional right to same-sex marriage, but he has also been outspoken in his opposition to transgender rights.
He co-sponsored and voted for H.R.
28, the Protection of Women and Girls in Sports Act, which would restrict participation in women’s sports based on sex assigned at birth rather than gender identity.
Gallant, on the other hand, has few official positions on LGBTQ issues but assured the Blade that, if elected, he would become “an advocate, particularly for the LGBTQ community.” He also said he believes same-sex marriage should be codified into law and that nondiscrimination protections should be expanded.
“I would definitely be pushing no discrimination against anyone for any reason — and that should not have any place in any workplace — especially on the federal government level.” For Gallant, however, LGBTQ rights are only one part of a broader platform centered on public service, affordability and restoring trust in government.
He repeatedly returned to the idea that a member of Congress should be accessible to the people they represent — something he believes his experience holding town halls and working directly with members of his community demonstrates.
“I always find that a record of service and showing up every day at work and in our communities is what’s going to be key and important.
And I think that’s what a representative needs to do.
As a representative you’re supposed to represent and work with the people that elect you to office.
And so that’s what I’m going to make sure I start today.” Link to article This Campaign Needs You This race will be decided by the people who show up early.
