Iowa Attorney General Brenna Bird has joined 19 other state attorneys general in asking the federal government to crack down on counterfeit weight-loss drugs made with ingredients from Chinese warehouses linked to fentanyl production.
The coalition sent a letter to Secretary of State Marco Rubio raising concerns about knock-off GLP-1 drugs.
These medications use Chinese-sourced active pharmaceutical ingredients, including semaglutide, tirzepatide and retatrutide.
"As GLP-1s become more popular, knock-off versions from China are making their way into our market," Bird said.
"The ingredients for these drugs can come from sources that have produced fentanyl, putting Iowans at risk.
I'm joining this coalition to ask Secretary of State Rubio to crack down on the security loopholes allowing these dangerous ingredients into the American market."
The attorneys general say these counterfeit drugs bypass the nation's safeguards and pose serious risks to American consumers.
The lack of safety measures means there's no guarantee the drugs are what they claim to be, and some manufacturers have ties to Chinese fentanyl production.
The letter points to a Chinese company that allegedly abused a trusted relationship with the U.S. to hide the unapproved source of an injectable drug entering the market.
An on-site inspection caught the breach, not border safeguards.
The attorneys general say this follows the same pattern as the Heparin crisis nearly 20 years ago that killed or harmed hundreds of Americans.
Recent reports show Chinese manufacturers associated with fentanyl supply chains are finding a new U.S. market in peptides, including cosmetic and weight-loss drugs.
The states question whether entities connected to fentanyl precursor markets moving into peptides could mean GLP-1 ingredients and related peptides are being introduced into the American compounding market.
The coalition asked to be provided with any information to protect their constituents.
They want to know what federal agencies know about foreign GLP-1 and peptide supply chains and their ties to entities involved in fentanyl production, what warning signs state attorneys general should watch for, and how state and federal partners can better share information before this market gets further out of control.
