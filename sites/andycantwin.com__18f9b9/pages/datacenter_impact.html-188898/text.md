The first Stokes application proposed allowing 70 decibels at the property line, and neighbors pushed back hard.
The conditions refiled in July moved the numbers, and the commissioners adopted them with the rezoning on September 14.
Next to land zoned non-residential it's still 70.
But next to homes, the cap is now 60 by day and 50 at night, with an extra cap on low-frequency hum and a 5-decibel penalty if the noise carries a tone.
Showing up moved a number.
A RURAL NIGHT, TYPICAL ~30 dB(A) · a whisper ALLOWED AT NIGHT, NEXT TO HOMES 50 dB(A) · 10 pm to 7 am · a refrigerator's hum ALLOWED BY DAY, NEXT TO HOMES 60 dB(A) · 7 am to 10 pm · normal conversation ALLOWED NEXT TO NON-RESIDENTIAL LAND 70 dB(A) · around the clock · a vacuum cleaner running measured at the exterior property line, sustained 15 minutes or more, from the zoning conditions adopted September 14, 2026 · binding on the project as approved Those bars look close together.
They aren't, because decibels multiply instead of adding.
Every 10 dB means ten times the sound energy.
Plot the same four numbers as actual sound energy and the picture changes completely.
30 40 50 60 70 30 dB · 1× 50 dB · 100× 60 dB · 1,000× 70 dB · 10,000× the sound energy dB(A) AT THE PROPERTY LINE the same four caps, plotted as sound energy relative to a 30 dB rural night · the decibel scale is logarithmic, so equal-looking steps multiply: every 10 dB is 10× the energy That's why the difference between 50 and 70 at a property line is not "a bit louder." And it's why the tightened residential caps were worth fighting for: on the energy scale, 50 is one one-hundredth of 70.

How much fuel?
The honest answer is the problem: the filings don't say.
At the July public information meeting, neighbors asked about the quantity, capacity, and fuel types of potential generators, and about the safety of fuel storage.
The applicant's own written report lists those questions among the topics discussed.
The filed documents put numbers on none of it.
To be fair, the industry frames on-site generation as a bridge while grid connections catch up, not a forever plan.
But Memphis shows what the bridge era looks like when nobody writes the rules down first, and the question a rezoning should answer is how long a bridge is allowed to be, and how big.
"Thermal drone imagery captured by Floodlight in late January shows some of the 15 permitted turbines operating at xAI's Colossus 1." · photo: Evan Simon / Floodlight · this is what "backup" hardware looks like when it runs And here's what the conditions do say about fuel.
The same July document that caps noise to the decibel, in about three pages of definitions and testing protocols, handles fuel in a list of permitted accessory uses introduced "including, without limitation": "fuel and water storage," four words, with no quantity, no cap, and no standard attached anywhere in the conditions.
Fire codes and state permits would bolt numbers on later, but the zoning the county actually votes on sets no limit of its own.
The adopted conditions do put rules around this hardware: generators at least 300 feet from the property boundary, testing limited to weekday business hours with noise attenuation in place.
They also ban open-loop cooling and any direct withdrawal from streams, rivers, wells, or aquifers.

Whether those conditions hold is a question about enforcement, which loops back to the stalled bill and to what the state should actually require .
