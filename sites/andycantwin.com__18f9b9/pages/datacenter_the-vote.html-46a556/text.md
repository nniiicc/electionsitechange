Updated September 15, 2026 · Stokes approved Project Delta 3 to 2 on September 14 3-2 Stop 4 · Stokes County · Project Delta Turned down by its own board, and approved anyway Project Delta · Stokes In January 2026, Stokes commissioners voted 3 to 2 to rezone nearly 1,850 acres of residential-agricultural land along the Dan River for Project Delta, and passed a text amendment opening more than a dozen other rural sites to data centers.
Their own planning board had recommended denial a month before.
The developer hadn't named a tenant, hadn't presented a detailed site plan, and hadn't explained where the promised jobs and tax revenue would come from.
Hundreds of residents showed up to oppose it.
The commissioners approved it anyway.
Then they took it back.
In March, thirteen residents and four cultural and environmental nonprofits sued.
On April 14 the board voided both January decisions and the land reverted to its old zoning, because the public notice for the January hearing hadn't met state law.
The defect is worth saying out loud: the notices went out too early .
North Carolina sets a window, the county mailed ahead of it, and there's no remedy for that except doing the entire thing over.
Months of hearings, lawyers, and packed rooms, undone by a mailing date.
There's a piece of this that deserves more than a zoning argument.
The site includes land tied to the Saura people and the Hairston Plantation, where descendants of enslaved people still maintain cemeteries nearby.
The January rezoning went through without a meaningful assessment of the impact on those places.
Ahead of the September vote, the developer said it would carry out an archaeological review and preserve land that turns out to be historically significant.
That's worth holding them to, and worth checking against the conditions the county actually adopted.
So the developer, a Charlotte firm called Engineered Land Solutions, started over.
It refiled on July 20, with substantially tighter conditions than the first time.
Next to homes, noise is capped at 60 decibels by day and 50 at night, with a low-frequency cap on top.
Generators stay 300 feet from the boundary.
Open-loop cooling and direct water withdrawals from streams, wells, and the river are banned.
Compliance gets measured by an independent, board-certified sound engineer.
And this didn't happen by magic: back in June, with the second application still taking shape, the developer was already telling FOX8 it was "looking to even go tighter on our noise limits." None of that was in the first draft.
Full rooms put it there.
The county's own experts kept saying the same thing.
In July, with the second application pending, the Stokes planning board voted 6 to 1 to recommend a six-month pause on data center approvals, saying the county needed time to study the risks residents kept raising.
Within days the commissioners rejected the pause, 3 to 2, and adopted countywide data center rules instead.
Then on August 13, looking at the refiled application itself, the planning board recommended denial again, 5 to 2.
That's twice the people the county appointed to study zoning have told the commissioners no on this project.
The money argument picked up a second opinion too.
Engineered Land Solutions has pitched Project Delta as worth $20 to $40 million a year in tax revenue, plus 250 to 500 permanent jobs at full buildout.
In early September, Michael Hicks, an economist at Shenandoah University, published an analysis funded by the Southern Environmental Law Center putting it at $20 to $40 million over ten years .
Same numbers, different denominator, a factor of ten apart.
The disagreement is about size: Hicks modeled a $1 billion investment, ELS says the campus tops $7 billion at full buildout, and Hicks called that full-buildout projection "not consistent with the observation of data center deployments around the country." ELS answered that the report never explains why the project wouldn't get there.
Nobody outside that argument has to settle it to notice what happened next.
The commissioners voted with both numbers sitting on the table.
Monday, September 14 · approved, 3 to 2 The Stokes County commissioners approved the rezoning, 3 to 2, the same margin as January.
It followed a public hearing the week before with more than three hours of comment, and a last round of speakers who were, to a person, opposed.
Commissioners voting yes pointed to the tax revenue and what it could pay for, the school system included.
The board settled a conflict-of-interest question first.
Two advocacy groups raised one commissioner's holdings in a rebar company that could end up supplying the project.
The county attorney gave her opinion that there was no legal conflict, and the board voted 3 to 1 against requiring a recusal.
What the vote settles: the zoning.
What it doesn't: the conditions still have to be enforced by somebody, the groups that sued in March have said further legal action is on the table, and the application the commissioners approved still doesn't state how much power the campus would draw.
Stop 1 is that hole.
None of the state rules that put a county in this position changed on September 14 either.

I want to be straight about what this is.
Showing up did not stop the rezoning.
It did change it.
The noise caps, the water bans, and the generator setbacks are in the record because people spent the better part of a year in those chairs, and conditions attached to a zoning order bind in a way a promise at a podium doesn't.
That matters.
It also isn't what anybody was there to win.
The part that room could never fix was the part that was never theirs to fix.
Five county commissioners were left to negotiate alone with a multibillion-dollar project, under state rules that make this kind of revenue nearly impossible to turn down, and no rule anywhere required the applicant to write down the one number that decides what it costs everyone else.

Application F-1669, filed by the Drox Group.
The plans showed three two-story buildings totaling about 1.12 million square feet, up to 80 feet tall, on a roughly 144-acre site the 2016 area plan had marked for mixed use, not industry.
And here's the detail that tells you how the rules work: the parcel sits just outside municipal limits, so the town it would tower over gets no vote.
The decision belonged entirely to the Forsyth County commissioners.
The town's elected council has been on both sides of it: Resolution 901 formally opposed the rezoning, then Resolution 912, passed June 22, withdrew that opposition and expressed support.
Either way, a town resolution is advice.
The vote belongs to the county.
Forsyth's planning board studied it and voted 7 to 2 to recommend denial.
This isn't a partisan observation, just a simple one: the county asked its own experts, and its experts said no.
On July 30, the commissioners agreed with them.
One more thing the public documents conspicuously never said: the project's power draw in megawatts.
That blank mattered, because the protections in SB 730 switch on at 100 megawatts.
The missing number is the difference between covered and not covered.
Thursday, July 30 · denied, 5 to 2 The Forsyth County commissioners voted 5 to 2 to deny the Iron Spur rezoning.
It took six months, more than 400 emails, and dozens of people at the microphone to get there.
"I don't know that we make the decision that this is something that they ought to have and we just cram it down everybody's throat," Commissioner Gray Wilson said.
Commissioners were clear they aren't opposed to data centers in general, and that the question is where one belongs.
What the vote settles: this rezoning, on this site.
What it doesn't: the developer can come back, here or somewhere else, and Stokes approved Delta six weeks later.
None of the state rules that make counties hungry for projects like this changed on July 30.

The pressure that put both projects in front of those boards is still sitting there, and it wasn't built in either county.
If you read the property tax guide , you already know the shape of it.
The state left property tax as the county's one real revenue tool, exempted this industry's equipment from the taxes counties could have collected, and killed the school funding case.
It's now moving to cap that last tool as well.
HB 1089 put a levy limit amendment on your November ballot, and it orders a cap without saying how tight the cap will be.
Approving big capital projects is one of the only growth paths Raleigh left counties.
What Stokes did in September is what that setup was built to produce.
Blaming one board's judgment misses the size of it.
