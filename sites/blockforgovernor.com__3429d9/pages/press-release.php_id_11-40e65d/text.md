Barrington, Rhode Island —
BARRINGTON, RI — Independent candidate for Governor Ken Block today questioned the financial due diligence performed by the Rhode Island state government before the General Assembly unanimously (with 12 votes not recorded) approved $18 million in taxpayer money to help rescue CharterCARE Health of Rhode Island ("CharterCARE"), a healthcare system that includes Roger Williams Medical Center and Our Lady of Fatima Hospital.
"Roger Williams and Fatima are important hospitals, and I understand why state leaders wanted desperately to save them," said Block.
"But desperation is not due diligence."
On Friday, seven months after the General Assembly approved the rescue bill that enabled healthcare nonprofit Centurion Foundation to take control of CharterCARE, Rhode Island Attorney General Peter Neronha began publicly questioning the hospital system's financial viability.
"Before the General Assembly put $18 million of our money behind this deal, who independently verified Centurion's numbers and determined that its plan would actually work?" Block asked.
"The answer appears to be nobody."
The warning signs were there before the vote.
The state's own financial consultant found that Centurion had not performed the customary financial due diligence expected in a major acquisition and that forward-looking financial estimates used in the transaction had not been independently tested.
CharterCARE was losing roughly $45 million annually.
Centurion's turnaround case relied on approximately $75 million in projected annual savings and improvements to erase those losses and support the new operation.
However, the public record reveals no evidence that anyone independently validated that critical $75 million assumption before legislators risked taxpayer money.
The bond market sent another warning.
CharterCARE's proposed debt had been rated BB-, below investment grade, and private investors ultimately required the state to provide an $18 million debt-service reserve before the financing could close.
The final financing carried rates as high as 12.5%.
In effect, CharterCARE's bailout was financed at junk-bond rates.
At the February 3 House Finance hearing, Centurion's CEO also declined to provide lawmakers with the bond term sheet detailing the debt-service and repayment schedule.
One legislator recognized the problem.
When McKee administration officials deferred questions about Centurion's financing back to Centurion, Rep.
Raymond Hull objected, saying, "I don't like that answer."
Hull said administration officials themselves should be "drilling them down in the interest of the people we're here to represent."
"This isn't hindsight," said Block.
"The state's own experts had already identified the risks.
The bond market was screaming risk.
Legislators weren't given all the financial information they requested.
And nobody appears to have independently verified the numbers that supposedly made the whole deal work."
The legislation was introduced on January 29.
It passed the General Assembly with unanimous support.
Governor McKee signed it on February 11.
"Rhode Island government once again threw a Hail Mary pass with taxpayer money and hoped everything would work out," Block added.
"Six months later, the Attorney General is questioning whether CharterCARE can remain financially viable.
If this deal fails, taxpayers, CharterCARE employees, and patients pay the price.
Nobody gets to say the warning signs weren't there."
