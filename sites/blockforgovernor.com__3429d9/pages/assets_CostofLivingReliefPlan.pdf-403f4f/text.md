COST OF LIVING RELIEF PLAN Government shouldn't be allowed to hide the cost of government My plan targets hidden and regressive taxes, fees, and government-imposed costs that are driving the affordability crisis in Rhode Island Learn more: www.kenblock.com My "Cost of Living Relief Plan" is aimed at reducing the taxes, fees, surcharges and other government-imposed costs buried in the everyday bills Rhode Islanders pay.
Rhode Islanders are being nickel-and-dimed to death, and too often government makes it difficult for us to even see what we're paying.
That's not just an affordability problem.
It's a transparency problem.
And there's another problem with the way we collect this money.
Many of these taxes and fees are regressive.
They fall on necessities like electricity, cell phones, insurance, and transportation, and they hit people who are struggling to make ends meet much harder than people who aren't.
A pair of bills introduced in the General Assembly this year provides a striking example of the transparency problem.
Senate Bill 2020 and its House companion, House Bill 7465, proposed a new 0.25% assessment on property-and-casualty insurance 1/4 premiums to fund fire-service training.
The legislation provided that the assessment “shall not be passed onto policyholders as a specific line-item surcharge.” Think about that.
The government was proposing a new cost on insurance and specifically preventing it from appearing as a separate surcharge on your bill.
If the government imposes a cost that Rhode Islanders ultimately pay, people have a right to know about it.
Thankfully, these bills didn’t pass.
Electric bills provide an even bigger example.
The state's own analysis says state programs and taxes account for approximately 24% of the average residential electric bill.
But even that doesn't tell the whole story.
Municipal taxes and other government-imposed costs are embedded elsewhere in utility rates and aren't identified to customers as taxes.
When those costs are included, government-driven costs approach 40% of a typical electric bill.
And these hidden costs aren't progressive.
The electric company doesn't ask how much money you make before collecting them.
A family struggling to pay the rent has to pay them just like a wealthy household does.
The difference is that the family struggling to get by feels every dollar.
Rhode Island cannot control inflation, interest rates or the national economy.
But it can control the taxes, fees and mandates imposed by state and local government.
Government shouldn't be allowed to hide the cost of government.
If the General Assembly wants to impose a tax or fee, disclose it.
If government wants to spend money on a program, put it in the budget.
Debate it publicly.
Debate it annually.
Make legislators vote for it.
And let Rhode Islanders see what they're paying.
And while we're making these costs visible, let's ask a second question: why are we financing so much of our government with regressive taxes on things everyone needs?
That's backwards.
Rhode Island has an affordability crisis.
Government should stop making it worse.
Block said the five proposals would be the beginning of a broader review of the taxes, fees, surcharges and mandates embedded in the cost of living in Rhode Island.
Rhode Island government can't fix every cause of the affordability crisis.
But it can stop adding unnecessary and regressive costs to the things people need every day.
Make the costs visible.
Make the government justify them.
Make them fair.
And get the costs down.” 2/4 Block's "Cost of Living Relief Plan" includes the following policies: 1.
Reduce government-imposed costs on electric bills My administration would target a significant reduction in residential and business electric bills by eliminating unnecessary government-imposed costs and moving appropriate and necessary taxation from electric bills to other, fairer mechanisms available to the state.
Our electric bills have become hidden tax bill.
Electricity is a necessity.
We shouldn't use everyone's electric bill as a backdoor way to raise revenue, especially when those charges bear little relationship to someone's ability to pay.
If the state needs the revenue, let's raise it transparently and fairly.
2.
Prohibit auto insurers from using credit-based insurance scores to set rates Rhode Island allows auto insurers to use credit-based insurance scores when determining rates.
My administration would work with the General Assembly to prohibit the practice.
Your auto insurance should be based on how you drive.
We should not penalize a good driver because they struggle financially.
In fact, charging someone more because they're already having trouble making ends meet just makes it harder for them to get ahead.
3.
Reduce taxes and fees on cell-phone bills According to the Tax Foundation, Rhode Island has the highest state and local wireless tax and fee burden in New England at 15.75%, compared with 10.75% in Massachusetts and 8.42% in Connecticut.
Rhode Island applies its 7% sales tax to cell-phone service, imposes a separate 5% telecommunications gross-receipts tax, and adds a monthly E-911 surcharge.
These taxes and fees increase the cost of providing wireless service and ultimately contribute to what consumers pay.
My administration would work to significantly reduce the taxes and fees imposed on wireless service.
Where the revenue is necessary to fund state services, I would seek to replace it through fairer and more transparent sources of revenue.
3/4 “A cell phone isn't a luxury anymore.
It's a necessity.
Yet we pile taxes and fees onto everyone's cell-phone bill without regard to their ability to pay.
If government needs the money, it should raise it in a fair and transparent way — not hide regressive taxes in the bills for things everyone needs.
4.
Stop the return of truck tolls and oppose passenger-vehicle tolls My administration would request a full stop on the planned return of Rhode Island's truck-tolling program and oppose new tolls on passenger vehicles.
Groceries…gasoline…almost everything we buy has to get to Rhode Island somehow, and much of it arrives by truck.
The truck toll doesn't stop with the trucking company.
It becomes another cost of bringing goods into Rhode Island and ultimately another cost paid by consumers.
Truck tolls are another tax you pay — you just don't see it.
5.
Eliminate RIPTA fares for one year and measure the results RIPTA currently collects roughly $9 million a year in passenger fares.My administration would implement a one-year fare-free trial, replacing that revenue with savings identified elsewhere in the state budget, and measure the effect on ridership and transportation costs.
Finding $9 million in a $15 billion state budget to fully subsidize RIPTA fares should be like finding a dime in your seat cushion.
If we can't find savings of that magnitude and redirect them toward something that could meaningfully lower transportation costs for Rhode Islanders, we're not looking hard enough. [END] 4/4
