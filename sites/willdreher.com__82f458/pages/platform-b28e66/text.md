Improving Statewide Education Performance The Puget Sound region (and the 46th district!) boasts a world class research university and one of the highest concentrations of well-educated parents in the country.
With all of the resources, here at home and across Washington, we should be a top-five state for K-12 education.
Yet the statewide performance of our public school system has lagged as average or below average—most recently, 31st in the nation—for decades.
Nearly 68% of fourth graders in this State were not proficient in reading according to a 2024 assessment, a new high.
40% read below grade level.
70% of 8th graders are not proficient in math.
As a graduate of public schools and a former public elementary school teacher, I firmly believe that fully funding our public schools is not merely Washington’s paramount constitutional duty, it is an investment in our future.
But it does not fully explain why our state lags behind other states that have done more with less money.
The problem is that we have not supported our teachers and followed rigorous, science-backed approaches to early literacy and math.
One report found that of 18 policies important to supporting early literacy—which many states had fully adopted years ago—Washington state had, until recently, only adopted 7.
While we passed a partial literacy reform bill this past session, we did so only after 43 other states had acted, and the bill permits districts to avoid updating their reading curricula for many more years.
That’s not good enough; every year that another grade of students falls behind is an urgent crisis.
I am committed to addressing it with commensurate urgency.
What we need are the things that have been proven to work in other states.
I would: Work with schools to accelerate curriculum revision and eliminate outdated literacy instructional practices.
Fund reading and math specialists and additional educator professional development, neither of which is reflected in current spending priorities.
Pursue additional annual universal reading assessments and parental notification of results, along with early interventions for struggling readers like individualized reading plans.
Fund summer reading camps and other evidence-based interventions.
We can’t just impose mandates on teachers and ask them to figure it out.
We need to provide them specialist support, training, and the tools required for our students to succeed.
Fully funding education, and passing a public education overhaul bill to make our state’s public education system the envy of the country, would be one of my top focuses in Olympia.
Public Safety Aurora Avenue.
Aurora Avenue is reportedly the second-busiest child sex trafficking location in the country, and gunfire is routine.
It’s been a problem for decades, and Seattle clearly cannot solve it alone, yet the incumbent has not sponsored a single Aurora-related law enforcement bill in 15 years.
I’m a former federal prosecutor and have prosecuted Aurora sex traffickers.
The problem has always been a lack of sustained outreach and enforcement resources, leading to few sex buyers (out of thousands) or traffickers being caught.
Increasing the likelihood of apprehension, not increasing sentences, deters crime, which is why relying solely on tweaks to sentencing laws won’t work.
The solution is a state-funded sex trafficking task force—with trauma-informed officers, detectives, prosecutors, and outreach workers.
It would quadruple the number of experienced personnel focused on Aurora : deterring buyers, building investigations that rescue children and put traffickers behind bars, and providing critical services to sex workers.
We can dramatically reduce child sex trafficking within four years; it’s a moral imperative.
As your representative, I will not abandon the neighborhoods around Aurora to sex trafficking and gun violence, and I will not idly tolerate child sex trafficking in my district.
Other public-safety solutions.
While we have made significant strides in recent years in combatting violent crime, FBI statistics show that across several recent years Washington and Seattle had among the highest crime rates in the country.
Many of us have seen those impacts in our community: stolen cars, garage break-ins, domestic violence.
Crime disproportionately impacts low-income, under-resourced, and marginalized communities.
Effectively reducing it is an economic justice issue.
Every one of us, not just the well off, has the right to be safe from violence, drug exposure, and serious property crime.
I’m the only candidate in this race who has spent six years working on criminal justice and public safety issues, as a Rule 3:03 public defender and federal prosecutor.
Studies consistently show that reactively lengthening the punishment for minor crimes is not the answer, and that certain offenders can be effectively diverted without the cost and recidivism that accompanies incarceration.
That is why, as a prosecutor, I initiated and led a novel federal pre-trial deferred prosecution for a non-violent offender struggling with mental health issues.
But the same research consistently shows that what does deter violent and property crime is increasing the likelihood an offender gets caught, and that is where we have work to do.
Moreover, we have yet to fully implement innovative public-health approaches to reducing gun violence that progressive cities like Baltimore (via its “Safe Streets” program) and Chicago have piloted to great success.
In the legislature, I will actually work to address local public safety issues, by: Working to increase cities’ flexibility to rapidly expand alternative response teams like Seattle’s CARE Department for mental health incidents, welfare checks, and other non-violent incidents, freeing officers to focus on violent and serious property crime.
Funding and supporting evidence-based gun-violence reduction strategies like those that Baltimore and other cities have pioneered to great effect, which combine public-health interventions with accountability and a focus on gun trafficking networks.
Combating child trafficking and sexual abuse by increasing funding for the some of our most successful and lowest cost interventions, particularly Washington State Patrol’s Missing and Exploited Children Task Force and the Washington Internet Crimes against Children Task Force.
Working hand-in-hand with my former colleagues, Washington Attorney General Nick Brown and Seattle City Attorney Erika Evans, on solutions aimed at addressing the root causes of crime.
Affordable Housing for All Seattle is by some measures the 8th most expensive city to live in in the world, and Washington state is the 5th least affordable state in the country with respect to housing.
Economists and experts agree that the best way to lower the cost of housing is to increase the supply of housing at all levels: market housing, affordable housing, and low-income housing.
And environmentalists know that urban density is how you prevent suburban and exurban sprawl, keep wildland wild, and protect wildlife corridors.
But we also need to ensure the people who live here, including renters, can afford to wait until that new housing is built.
W e have made progress in recent years on issues like zoning, but still have work to do.
Another critical component of the solution is permitting reform.
You can have a years-long permit process and design review, or you can have faster and cheap housing built.
You can’t have both.
Permitting delays (not permitting) alone add, by some estimates, over $50,000 to the cost of building a home in King County.
While acting in good faith, my opponent has at times reportedly led the opposition to what I believe are common-sense housing density reforms in our state.
For instance, in 2021, Rep.
Pollet made changes to a bill–intended to streamline the process for DADUs and ADUs–that would have arguably made “ it harder to build an ADU on a single-family lot, not easier .” That led groups like the AARP and environmentalists to urge that the amended bill be vetoed, which is what occurred.
With your support I will: Work with legislative colleagues, housing providers, and city leaders to reduce red tape and hurdles that slow down and raise costs for housing we need, such as by requiring pre-permitted, template housing designs to accelerate and make cheaper the kind of housing we want.
Continue pushing for state-level policies (like support for social housing) that increase density and improve affordability in every community, not just Seattle.
Support rent stabilization laws and just-cause eviction standards that help renters from being priced out while housing supply is built.
Continue to fund supportive and transitional housing for those experiencing homelessness.
Expand transit-oriented housing development and other targeted density programs that maximize housing opportunities along growing light rail and transit corridors.
Help work on land tax solutions that incentivize owners to build on vacant lots, not sit on vacant lots as investments.
Seattle will only become more expensive if our neighbors–who provide the essential services we need to keep local–cannot afford to live here.
We can increase housing supply in this state in a way that leads to mixed-income, diverse neighborhoods, with greater walkability and, because of greater density, more successful small businesses.
A Tax Code for Working Families Washington has one of the most regressive tax codes in the nation.
But it’s not just that our wealthiest pay one of the lowest rates in the country; our working poor and seniors on fixed incomes pay one of the highest effective tax rates.
Indeed, families in the bottom 20 percent pay an effective tax rate over three times what those in the top 1 percent pay.
Almost nobody believes that tax system makes sense.
We can fix this by seeking new progressive revenues, which we can use to both fund new programs and reduce the regressive taxes currently hitting the poorest and those on fixed incomes the hardest.
I am one of the only state candidates proposing an AI tax that can both address budget shortfalls and make it more expensive for companies to automate jobs and replace workers.
My opponent does discuss this idea now, but only after I introduced the idea.
I support lifting the Advanced Computing Surcharge cap , which currently protects our largest technology companies from paying taxes.
I support a comprehensive corporate income tax to replace Washington’s Business and Occupation (B&O) tax on gross receipts .
We are one of only three states to have B&O tax rather than a tax on corporate profits, but our B&O tax is poorly designed and overtaxes low-margin businesses.
While I would prefer a corporate income tax to an employee compensation payroll excise tax like the Well Washington Fund , the latter may be necessary in the interim until a corporate income tax can be enacted.
I support the Millionaire’s Income Tax , and I will fight any attempts to repeal it.
Climate, the Environment, and Transit I am the son of an environmental lawyer, and have passionately defended the environment throughout my career.
While we’ve made great strides in certain areas, in others, we are falling further behind: Renewable energy.
While the state legislature has set aggressive targets for renewable energy and decarbonization, our state is dead last in actually adding renewable energy (e.g., wind and solar projects) to the grid over the last decade.
Why?
Because “Washington leaders paid little attention to” the nuts and bolts of delivery clean energy: transmission planning, renewable siting and permitting, interconnection, all of which are required to get clean energy to our homes.
Indeed, “ [o]f the 469 large renewable projects that applied to connect to [our region’s] grid since 2015, only one has reached approval .” A former state senator rightly called this an “ embarrassing ” mistake by our legislators, including Rep.
Pollet, who missed this issue for a decade.
That is where I have expertise, and years of experience working on transmission and clean energy issues.
For example, I was part of a small legal team that beat the oil and gas industry in lawsuits across the country to establish the legal foundation of state clean energy subsidies (like renewable energy credits) within the United States.
Our legal wins are the reason Washington is still able to subsidize clean energy at all.
But I also worked on transmission planning issues, and understand the wholesale electricity markets and the complex interplay between federal and state authority over transmission.
I’ll bring that lacking expertise on these issues to a legislature that has missed it over the last decade.
