Let's Talk about Housing: Construction Defects Someone who has read all of our newsletters asked me a very simple question this week: “Why are you so focused on Construction Defects Liability Reform?” I’ve talked about my work on the legislation, but I could almost write a book already on what I’ve learned about this issue since December.
There are so many facets of this conversation that it can be hard in a simple conversation to separate all of the pieces - and I certainly won’t try to do that in a newsletter.
What has lit my passion about this area are four major issues with how things work today: 1) homeowners currently have very restrictive avenues for construction defects relief, 2) many home defects never get corrected (even after satisfactory settlements - leaving defective properties in inventory), 3) the actual responsible parties (builders, contractors, etc) aren’t held accountable for correcting their own mistakes, 4) we have a known issue in Colorado where multi-family ownable construction projects are basically uninsurable Our current regulations have created an industry that “seems” consumer focused, but it doesn’t always work that way.
As an example, I’ll share a (somewhat long) story about “John” who bought a house with a chimney that wasn’t properly attached to his new home.
John contacted the builder who told John to contact their warranty company.
Several people were sent to look at the issue and make a plan, and John’s experience was that the process was moving too slow despite the builder’s promise to fix the issue.
John received a card in the mail from an attorney’s office that said something to the effect of “we can help you resolve issues with your builder”, so John called the number on the card.
After consulting with the attorney, John was told that these claims were common and that he had very limited time to take action, and that the legal services wouldn’t cost John anything out of pocket since they would deduct any fees from any settlements or judgements.
John signed an agreement and the attorneys went to work on his behalf.
The first letter the attorney sent was to the builder advising the builder to cease all communication with their client.
This is all completely normal, but any progress the builder was making would now be subject to their work with the attorney.
We’re now off to the races in a legal dispute between lawyers and insurance companies while John waits and the builder may or may not have any further involvement.
Here’s where John’s story gets much more interesting: John’s settlement offer from the builder’s insurance was significantly less than the estimate to attach the fireplace and chimney.
With the proposed settlement minus the attorney’s fees, John was going to have to spend another +/-$40,000 on his brand new home, which he was unwilling to do, so John chose to reject the settlement and seek relief through district court.
A year passes and John gets a job promotion out of state and sells the home (with full disclosure of the chimney issue and that there is a current construction defects claim).
"Jane" buys the home and now owns the home with the defect.
John’s claim finally reaches trial (23 months later) and ultimately results in a more favorable settlement.
Jane, however, has no claim to this settlement - she owns the home with the defect, John keeps the settlement minus 30% for legal fees.
2 years later the home still has no use of the fireplace and the chimney has some “temporary fixes” but the issue isn’t corrected.
My next example I’ll keep a little shorter, but discuss an issue with a 8 unit townhome property that shares a common space, has an HOA for maintenance, etc.
The HOA noticed issues with the sidewalks cracking and buckling and were immediately dissatisfied when the contractor that installed them said “I’m not fixing it”.
They contacted an attorney who came to meet with a representative of the HOA and recommended that they file a suit against the contractor.
The 4 HOA board members agreed and filed suit (on behalf of all of the homeowners).
The homeowner that contacted my office about this was NOT one of the four board members and was unaware that he was now party to this lawsuit - and that his property was encumbered collectively until the suit is resolved.
He was livid that he didn’t understand that he wouldn’t be able to sell his condo until all of this was over.
His (almost exact) words to me were “I would have paid for the sidewalk myself before I’d let them decide to not let me sell my home.” This suit didn’t relate to anything that caused major issues for the townhomes, but the process is the same.
My view about how we need to fix this is 1) create pathways for homeowners to resolve these issues with the contractors that made the mistake, 2) fix any related issues without further delays or needing to file new claims, and 3) leave homeowners with the ability to reject work unless defects are corrected satisfactorily.
To me, defects should work almost like we would treat a defective car: if you buy a Honda and it’s defective, you have to take it back to Honda to correct it - at their expense and to full satisfaction.
You don’t get to take your Honda to the Ford Dealership, and you don’t sue Honda in a multi-year lawsuit for causing a defect in the first place.
If we really want “builders to build better” then we need to hold them directly accountable for these fixes.
If they don’t fix it correctly, homeowners should retain the ability to sue - but it shouldn’t be our first step.
Bills That I Have Nonstop Attention On Construction Defects Liability Reform ( SB24-106 ) - will continue on to the Senate Floor next week before coming to run it’s process through the House.
As I mentioned above this bill gives homeowners the option of requiring the contractor that caused a construction defect a way to remedy the defect without first going to litigation.
It aims to hold the builder that caused the defect accountable vs. sometimes long battles between the homeowner's lawyer and insurance companies, sometimes resulting in settlements that don’t leave enough money to cure a defect.
We are aiming to make sure the defects get corrected as well as eliminate some of the hidden surprises that can come into play in lawsuits and really have this legislation be about “if it’s broken, we should make the builder fix it.” Airports ( HB24-1235 ) - This bill is in Appropriations.

JYACAP - We got the bill draft for JYACAP this week.
This bill hopes to reduce a very serious bottleneck for youth who are convicted as adults in our system by removing the need for the governor's signature from parole board decisions.
This measure was originally put in place when there were doubts about how Juvenile Parole would function and is no longer necessary - the change streamlines the process.
Even the Governor thinks that Juvenile Parole can be managed without his oversight.
