VOTE - November 3rd 2026 Early Voting Begins October 22, 2026 Home About Ayanna Volunteer Blog More Home About Ayanna Volunteer Blog Home About Ayanna Volunteer Blog My Blog All Posts Delaware’s Healthcare Challenges Go Beyond the Cost of Care September 28, 2026 | News, What I'm Hearing As I continue talking with healthcare professionals, residents, families and people throughout Delaware, one thing is becoming increasingly clear: our healthcare challenges are not just about the cost of care.
They are also about capacity, workforce, access and what happens to patients when they no longer need to be in a hospital but have nowhere appropriate to go.
One issue I’m hearing about is patients who are medically ready to leave the hospital but cannot be safely discharged because they need guardianship, long-term care, supportive housing or another appropriate placement.
That matters.
A hospital bed is one of the most expensive places in our healthcare system to provide care.
When someone no longer requires acute hospital care but remains in that bed because there is nowhere appropriate for them to go, it creates a ripple effect throughout the entire system.
The patient still deserves care and dignity.
But the hospital may not be the right setting for that care.
Meanwhile, another Delawarean may be sitting in an emergency department waiting for a bed because they are seriously ill and need to be admitted.
This is not simply a hospital problem.
It is a systems problem.
Delaware has recognized this issue before.
State law established a process for healthcare institutions dealing with non-acute patients who no longer require hospital-level care and may need a guardian before they can be transferred to a less restrictive setting.
But a legal process alone does not create a place for someone to go.
We have to ask a bigger question: Does Delaware have enough appropriate facilities, long-term care options, supportive housing, behavioral health resources and community-based services for people who cannot safely return home?
And if we don't, what is that shortage costing taxpayers?
The Cost Problem Is Already Here This conversation becomes even more urgent when we look at Delaware's overall healthcare spending.
Spotlight Delaware recently reported that state health officials are warning that Delaware's current healthcare spending trajectory is "unsustainable." During a recent Delaware Health Care Commission meeting, Secretary of Health and Social Services Christen Linke Young put the situation plainly: "The wheels are going to come off.
There simply isn't the money." That should get everyone's attention.
The same discussion highlighted rising healthcare costs, workforce shortages and pressure on Medicaid.
State Medicaid Director Andrew Wilson pointed out that increasing healthcare expenses ultimately create greater demands on taxpayers — money that could otherwise support priorities such as education and infrastructure.
At the same time, Delawareans buying insurance through the Affordable Care Act marketplace are facing higher premiums, and the number of insurers participating in Delaware's individual market has declined.
So we have several problems happening at once: Healthcare is becoming more expensive.
Delaware's population is growing and aging.
Hospitals and healthcare providers continue to face workforce pressures.
Some patients cannot transition out of hospitals because appropriate placements or decision-making arrangements are not immediately available.
Taxpayers are ultimately helping shoulder an increasingly expensive system.
We cannot continue addressing each of these issues in isolation.
We Need More Capacity Outside the Hospital If a person no longer needs acute hospital care but cannot safely live independently, we need somewhere appropriate for that person to go.
That means Delaware needs to examine whether we have enough skilled nursing capacity, assisted living options, behavioral health facilities, rehabilitation services, supportive housing, home- and community-based services, and other forms of transitional and long-term care.
We also need to look at how quickly guardianship and placement cases move through the system.
Delaware's Office of the Public Guardian itself notes that guardianship proceedings take time and that the office does not perform post-hospital placement planning.
That responsibility remains with the facility.
This demonstrates just how complicated the transition can become when a patient has no family member or other person able to assume responsibility.
And we have to acknowledge the realities facing families and caregivers, too.
Caring for an aging parent, an adult with significant disabilities or someone with complex medical or behavioral health needs can be overwhelming.
Families may be dealing with financial limitations, housing challenges, employment responsibilities or simply a level of care they are not medically equipped to provide.
The answer cannot be to blame patients, families, hospitals or healthcare workers.
The answer has to be building a system that works better.
Follow the Dollars — and the Outcomes If Delaware is spending more and more on healthcare, we should be asking what taxpayers and patients are receiving in return.
Are we investing enough in prevention?
Are we building enough capacity outside our hospitals?
Are people able to see primary care providers before their conditions become emergencies?
Do we have enough nurses, physicians, specialists and other healthcare professionals?
Are we helping seniors age safely in their communities?
Are we creating enough appropriate places for patients who require long-term assistance?
And are we spending healthcare dollars in ways that actually produce better health outcomes?
These aren't questions for one hospital system or one state agency.
They require healthcare providers, insurers, state government, long-term care providers, community organizations and families to be part of the conversation.
Delaware Has to Plan for What Is Coming Delaware is changing.
Our population is growing.
Our residents are getting older.
Healthcare is becoming more expensive.
And the demand for services is not going away.
We cannot wait until every hospital is overwhelmed, insurance becomes unaffordable or taxpayers simply cannot absorb another increase.
We need a comprehensive healthcare strategy that looks beyond what happens inside the hospital.
That means investing in the healthcare workforce, expanding appropriate long-term and community-based care, addressing guardianship and placement bottlenecks, strengthening primary and preventive care, and making sure we are using taxpayer dollars efficiently.
Because ultimately, this conversation is about more than healthcare spending.
It is about whether Delaware is building a healthcare system capable of taking care of its people — in the right setting, at the right time and at a cost we can sustain.
And from what I'm hearing, this is a conversation Delaware needs to have now.
