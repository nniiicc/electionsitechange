The Bennington Banner, July 8
Empathy is not a threat to our community—it is one of its
greatest strengths, and government should help ensure that no one is
left without support.
Tom addresses a recent postcard attacking Senator Seth Bongartz,"Dark Money", Citizens United and
states his position on corporate and PAC donations.
Tom explains why he is running and what he hopes to accomplish.
The goal is to make government
more responsive, and his guiding principles are to listen to voters, use their input as a guide, and keep
constituents informed.
Tom proposes "Pledges & Bridges," a community-based microloan fund for people facing
a temporary financial crisis, using small contributions with flexible repayable.
The idea is to
interrupt small financial crises before they become critical.
Tom explores the Battenkill Valley Community Health Center in Arlington as a model for
health care in Vermont, and argues that Medicare for All would result in reduced overall
health care spending in Vermont and nationally.
The primary results showed a close two-candidate Democratic race and suggests the focus
for the November election should be voter turnout and encouraging voters to support
both Democratic candidates.
Tom argues that Vermont could use a state-owned bank to keep more financial returns within the state,
expand lending capacity, support local financial institutions, and respond more quickly to emergencies—but
acknowledges that establishing one would require significant upfront work and careful, politically independent
governance.
Healthcare costs don’t stop at the doctor’s office—they drive up school budgets, municipal costs,
business expenses, taxes, and the prices Vermonters pay every day.
Tom argues for pursuing universal
healthcare, such as Medicare for All, while also expanding community-based care and access
to health services.
Tom notes that the rapid expansion of AI data centers could drive up electricity costs
and environmental impacts, urging Vermont to require renewable energy for new facilities and proceed
cautiously with AI development.
Tom points out that health care is different from ordinary consumer goods and services:
when you are sick or
injured, you cannot simply wait for prices to fall or shop around for a better deal.
Meaningful reform will
require thoughtful government planning, responsible regulation, and sustained investment to expand access
while keeping costs under control.
Renewable energy—especially solar, wind, and geothermal—is now cheaper and faster to deploy
than fossil-fuel generation, even when battery storage is included.
Vermont can expand solar without
sacrificing farmland or scenic landscapes by prioritizing rooftops, parking lots, canals,
rail corridors, floating solar, and agrivoltaics.
Tom introduces his top priorities: healthcare affordability and access, energy costs,
and education/property taxes.
High healthcare and energy costs ripple through the broader economy,
while education costs are complicated by healthcare expenses, staffing needs, and Vermont’s
declining student population.
He argues for local input and state support rather than top-down
mandates, and asks residents of Bennington and Pownal to share their priorities so he can
better represent them.
Tom focuses on Act 181, saying that the parts supporting infill development in cities and
towns are reasonable, while questioning provisions such as the “road rule” and the added burdens on
some rural landowners.
While Vermont needs to balance environmental protection with economic
growth, housing, and jobs, his main concern is how poorly the provisions of the law were
communicated to constiutents.
