Don't Blame Young Coloradans for Socialism.
Give Them Their Future Back.
By Buddy Jericho
I recently returned from traveling across Colorado’s Western Slope with gubernatorial candidate
Victor Marx and his lieutenant governor running mate, George Markert.
Along the way, I spent
time talking with families, ranchers, small-business owners, workers and young people.
The data
suggest that feeling is widespread.
A recent statewide survey of registered Colorado voters found that roughly one in ten now
identifies politically as socialist.
Another Colorado poll found that 80% of voters believe the
American Dream is harder to attain today than it was a generation ago.
A Colorado Health
Foundation survey found that 76% of Coloradans worry about whether they will be able to afford
to continue living in this state.
Those numbers deserve more than a partisan reaction.
It is easy to look at the growing appeal of socialist ideas, particularly among younger Americans,
and dismiss it as entitlement or a lack of understanding of free enterprise.
That explanation is too simple.
Consider a 27-year-old teacher, deputy sheriff, mechanic or nurse in Colorado.
That person may
be working full time, paying rent, buying groceries, carrying insurance and trying to save for the
future.
Then rent rises.
Groceries cost more.
Insurance costs more.
A starter home that once seemed
attainable begins to feel distant.
Eventually, a reasonable question emerges:
What exactly am I working toward?
That question matters.
The danger is not that young Coloradans suddenly stopped believing in America.
It is that too
many may no longer believe America’s promises are available to them.
That does not make socialism the answer.
But it helps explain why arguments for a
fundamentally different economic system can find an audience.
Colorado has become a difficult place for many working families to build a life.
Housing costs
have climbed sharply.
Everyday expenses continue to strain household budgets.
Starting a family
can feel financially daunting.
For younger Coloradans, milestones that previous generations
considered ordinary increasingly can feel out of reach.
There is also a question of public trust.
Consider the I-70 Floyd Hill project.
In 2018, Colorado voters rejected Proposition 110, a statewide tax increase whose proposed
transportation project list included substantial funding for Floyd Hill improvements.
The need for safer infrastructure was real.
But voters rejected that particular funding approach.
Three years later, the legislature adopted a transportation package relying in part on new and
expanded fees, and Gov.
Jared Polis signed it at Floyd Hill.
The project subsequently moved
forward through a combination of state and federal funding.
Voters never cast a direct ballot on whether Floyd Hill itself should be improved, and that
distinction matters.
But so does the broader question of whether people believe government
respects the message they send at the ballot box.
When citizens believe government simply finds another mechanism after voters reject one
approach, confidence can erode.
Public safety adds another dimension.
Colorado recorded meaningful declines in violent crime and property crime in 2025.
Those
improvements should be acknowledged, and law enforcement deserves credit for progress.
At the same time, Coloradans spent years dealing with concerns involving vehicle theft, fentanyl,
homelessness, disorder and neighborhood safety.
Those experiences do not disappear
immediately when statewide statistics begin improving.
This combination of economic pressure, public-safety concerns and declining trust helps explain
why Victor Marx frequently talks about three priorities:
Affordability.
Safety.
Accountability.
Those three words do not explain every challenge facing Colorado.
They are a framework for
understanding many of the concerns we hear across the state.
Affordability asks whether working families can still build a life here.
Safety asks whether people feel secure in the communities they call home.
Accountability asks whether citizens believe their government listens to them and uses public
resources responsibly.
The growing appeal of socialism should not become an excuse to insult younger Coloradans.
It should prompt a more serious question about why confidence has been lost.
Young people do not need to be told everything is fine when their experience tells them
otherwise.
They need reason to believe that work still leads somewhere.
They need to believe homeownership remains possible, raising a family in Colorado remains
realistic, and effort, responsibility and persistence can still produce a better life.
If we want young Coloradans to believe in the American Dream, we cannot simply tell them it
still exists.
We have to build a state where hard work can still lead to a home, a family, security and a future
they can call their own.
