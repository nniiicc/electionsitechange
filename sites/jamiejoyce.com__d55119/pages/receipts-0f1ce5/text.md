Fact-checking Lateefah Simon's claims and billboard In short: the public record does not show that Lateefah Simon delivered $833 million in federal funding to the East Bay.
It shows her delivering $11.2 million.
"I've delivered $833 million in federal funding to the East Bay" Clear Channel billboard, downtown Oakland.
"Paid for by official funds authorized by the House of Representatives." Date posted: not established.
Rep.
Lateefah Simon's office has stated a running total of federal money it has "secured," "delivered" or "brought home" to California's 12th District at every recorded town hall since May 29, 2025, in a series of press releases, on the office website and on a billboard.
This page sets out what we collected, how we tried to match her figures to federal award records, what each dollar we could trace turned out to be, and where the public record runs out.
It is a review of public records, not an audit: only her office, which has never published an award list, or the agencies, can settle what the $833 million consists of.
Sources are linked where they are used, and every one is listed at the bottom of the page.

The billboard is not the end of it.
Since she first gave the $833 million figure at a town hall on July 28, 2026, her office's website now says "$1.15B+ announced in grants for the district." Nothing on the site itemizes that number, and it is about $630 million more than everything her office has ever itemized in writing.
Every finding below concerns the $833 million; the larger figure rests on even less.
That leaves $821.8M , 98.7% of the billboard figure, that the record does not show her delivering.
Of that, $639.6M could be plausibly identified by matching government records with her claims in press releases and at town halls.
It is real federal money that came to the district, or to the state and regional agencies that serve it.
We are more confident of some of these matches than others: for $219.2M of it her office named the program and the recipient; for $150.2M it named the agency and the amount and we found the records; for $258.9M it gave only a category total and we inferred which records it must mean; and $11.3M is an award her office never stated at all, counted because it went to an organization she did name (the Method section shows which is which).
But all of it looks like it came through programs that pay a fixed amount to the same recipients whoever holds the seat, or through competitions the recipients entered and won on their own.
Her office announced this money; the agencies decided it.
We found no record of her office causing any of it.
In two categories the records that fit her description add up to more than the figure she gave; the "Not itemized by her office" section explains this and gives the more conservative count.
The remaining $182.2M was never itemized: for most of it, nothing her office has said in any release or at any town hall describes it by program, recipient or amount, so there is nothing to match a government record to; for the rest (a year-end research total and a few small lines), her words were not specific enough to identify an award.
We looked and could not find what it is.
We have some ideas, set out under "Not itemized by her office" below, and we have asked her for an audit of every number.
To get this far we worked through 1,121 source documents and records, listed at the bottom of this page: 19 of her office's press releases and 11 town hall recordings, quoted verbatim; 845 federal award pages; the enacted appropriations tables; her bills, amendments and roll-call votes; the court orders behind every restoration; and the news, agency and local-government documents that bear on each claim.
On top of those listed sources we pulled, as bulk data, every grant and direct payment the government records as performed in the district from January 2025 to October 2026 (7,359 transactions on 2,981 awards, $2.59 billion), every SBIR and STTR research contract and grant to district firms over the same period (134 transactions), and the full export of the Grant Witness tracker, which lists the 108 district grants frozen, cut or suspended in 2025 and 2026.
We cannot be certain of any of this without that audit.

How federal money reaches a district, in plain terms Most people assume that when a member of Congress announces federal money, the member brought it.
Usually that is not how it works.
Federal money reaches a district in five main ways, and only one of them involves the member choosing where it goes.

Announcing a federal grant to a local recipient is an ordinary part of a congressional office's work, and nothing on this page faults it.
The claims under review are the ones in which the office says it "secured," "delivered" or "brought home" the money, and the billboard.
Two releases that only announce or welcome an award (April 3 and June 4, 2025) are not listed on this page; they appear in the source list at the bottom.
The billboard itself is paid for from the Members' Representational Allowance, the public appropriation that funds each House office (2 U.S.C.
5341), which is why it carries the line "Paid for by official funds authorized by the House of Representatives." House rules place official mass communications under the House Communications Standards Commission, a panel of House members.
Whether and how this billboard was reviewed is not public, and nothing on this page assumes it was or was not.
Her figures are not always consistent with each other.
At the Oakland town hall of February 19, 2026 she gave $383 million for the running total and, later the same evening (53:12), said that "last year we brought back... almost over a quarter of a billion dollars," against her office's $344 million for 2025.
At the first four recorded town halls (March 21, April 24, April 25 and May 27, 2025) no running total is stated.
The running total falls once: $870 million on August 26, 2026, six days after "almost $900 million." How we matched her figures to the record Fix her words.
Every funding sentence in a release or transcript was copied verbatim with its date and URL.
Verbs were logged separately ("announced," "welcomed," "secured," "delivered," "brought home"), because announcing a grant and delivering one are different claims.
Find the record.
For each line, search USASpending.gov by recipient, program and date window; where no single award page exists (block-grant allocations, FTA apportionments, enacted earmark tables), use the agency's published table.
Record the award ID, amount, date signed, period of performance and place of performance as the API returned them.
Count one amount per line.
The amount counted is the obligation in the claim window (the annual installment for a continuation award, not the multi-year total), so that nothing is counted twice across her cumulative figures.
Classify by mechanism, using the government's own label.
USASpending's assistance type (formula grant, project grant, direct payment, cooperative agreement), the program's statute, and the award's first date decide the category.
A continuation on an award first competed in 2018 is a renewal, not a win.
Test against the full district universe.
Pull every award and transaction with a CA-12 place of performance for the period and ask, for each of her lines, whether a different record fits as well or better.
Where more than one fits, say so.
Check for disruption.
Cross every matched award, and every district award, against the Grant Witness tracker, agency cancellation lists, court dockets and obligation timing, to see whether anything she counted was frozen, cut or restored, and by whom.
Re-verify live on a stated date, and grade confidence.
Figures were re-checked against their primary records on September 14 and October 7, 2026.
Confidence grades High: directly supported by a primary government record or her office's own words, re-checked live.
Medium-High: a primary record checked once that could not be re-fetched on the second date, or a record plus one inference the text states.
Medium: supported but resting on an inference or a single source.
Low: not supported, or supported only by the absence of evidence.
The Limits section lists the items rated below High on source grounds; the match-confidence tiers below cover the inference risk.
Uniqueness grades A match is Unique when her line names an amount and recipient that one record, or one obvious pair of records, fits.
It is Either/or, clarification requested when more than one record, or more than one set of records, fits her words, and the release gives no list to settle it.
In every either/or case on this page, whichever record is meant, the money is formula, renewal, entitlement or competitive, never money she designated, so the conclusion is the same; what differs is which award page to cite.

If her office provides the audit: what to look out for Rep.
Simon has agreed, more than once and on video, to provide an accounting of the $833 million.
If one arrives, it should be read against the same standard this page uses.
A dollar was delivered by her when a document shows her office choosing the amount or the recipient, or taking an action that a government record says decided the award.
Everything else is something short of that.
These are the ways an accounting can look like proof without being proof.
Watch for Vague verbs "Fought for," "pushed for," "stood shoulder to shoulder with," "worked to bring home." None of these names an action.
Each one should be followed by the question: what did the office do, on what date, and where is the document?
If the answer is a letter, it is a letter.
If there is no answer, the verb is doing the work the evidence should be doing.
Watch for Anecdotes in place of an accounting A few examples of things the office really did (a lab visit, a letter about a cancelled hub, the fifteen designated projects) can be offered as if they stood for the whole.
They do not.
The fifteen projects are 1.3% of the figure.
An accounting has to cover the other 98.7%, dollar for dollar, or say plainly that it cannot.
Watch for A list of grants A list of awards that happens to total $833 million proves that $833 million reached the district.
That was never in dispute.
It says nothing about what her office did to bring any of it.
Formula money, renewals and competitions the recipients won would all appear on such a list, and all of them would have arrived with another name on the office door.
Watch for Motion mistaken for action Sending a letter is not delivering the money.
Visiting a lab is not delivering the money.
Co-signing a statement after a court has already restored a grant is not delivering the money.
Voting for a bill with hundreds of items in it, or against one and then announcing the projects it carried, is not delivering the money.
These are the ordinary motions of a congressional office.
They may be worth something; they are not the thing the billboard claims.
Watch for Partial involvement counted as the whole Being one of 33 signers on a letter, one of 100 members on a bipartisan statement, or one of many officials who attended an announcement is involvement.
It is not grounds to say "I delivered it." The test is whether the money would have arrived without her, and for every traced dollar on this page outside the fifteen projects, the record says it would have.
Watch for Appeal to authority If she says a House ethics or communications committee approved the billboard, that means a panel of House members reviewed whether official funds could pay for it under the House's own rules.
It does not mean the contents of the billboard are true.
Nothing on the record shows that any committee of Congress checked whether she delivered $833 million; this page did, against the public record, and the record does not show it.
Approval of the ad is not verification of the claim.

An accounting in that form could be checked line by line against this page in a day, and this page would be updated to match it.
