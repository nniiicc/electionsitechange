People who use the Bulb, and some who once lived there, have described specific things they want it to be, and no long-term plan has been adopted.

Trash washing onto the Albany Marsh, cleaned up by volunteers and not measured under the regional stormwater permit Albany · Albany Marsh, Cerrito Creek and the Albany Mudflats · raised since April 2026 East Bay Trash Pandas, a volunteer cleanup group, held its first cleanup at the Albany Marsh , beside the mudflats, on 25 April 2026 and wrote up the day this way: "A truly fantastic day cleaning a truly enormous amount of trash out of the Albany Marsh with Sophie, Elisa, Brendan, Sylvia, Scott, Tara, and Andy!" East Bay Trash Pandas, cleanup write-up on the group's cleanups page, 25 April 2026 Seven named volunteers took part, and by the group's account they removed a shopping cart and a floating mattress.
Eighteen returned on Coastal Cleanup Day, the yearly volunteer shoreline cleanup, on 19 September 2026.
In a post dated 15 May 2026, the group wrote that "The site we cleaned last time is still pretty spotless, but the rest of the marsh could use another cleanup or two." The group's wetlands page says: "Unfortunately, these miraculous marshes are now covered in tons of trash washed up out of the Bay." It adds: "We suspect most of the garbage is local litter swept out to sea by stormwater runoff, likely from adjoining City of Richmond and CalTrans land." That is the group's suspicion, not a measurement.
It fits two state cease and desist orders, which require a party to stop breaking a rule: Order R2-2025-0032 for Richmond and Order R2-2019-0007 for Caltrans, the state highway agency.
Cities here operate under a regional stormwater permit, state rules requiring them to reduce the trash washing from storm drains into creeks and the Bay.
What we have tracked suggests it does not measure the trash that reaches this shoreline.
The regional Receiving Water Trash Monitoring Plan , version 1.0, dated 31 July 2024, picks six sites where trash reaching a creek or the Bay is measured.
None is on Cerrito Creek, Codornices Creek or the Albany shoreline.
The mudflats are a 160-acre state ecological reserve inside McLaughlin Eastshore State Park , and in the documents we have tracked no regulator names them as a receiving water, the body of water storm drains ultimately empty into.
Cerrito Creek carries runoff from Berkeley, Kensington, Albany, El Cerrito and Richmond across two county lines to the Bay , beside the mudflats, and Albany runs roughly 11 miles of storm drains to the Bay.
Upstream, El Cerrito's FY2021-22 annual report lists 164 full trash capture systems, devices that stop trash entering a storm drain, and is the only document we reviewed naming the SF Bay Cerrito Creek Watershed as a receiving water.
Berkeley reported 73.1% trash load reduction in April 2023 , the share of its trash stopped before reaching waterways.
Richmond has the hardest job.
The permit required 100% trash load reduction by 30 June 2025, and Richmond reported 66%, the lowest in the region.
On 10 December 2025 the San Francisco Bay Regional Water Quality Control Board, which writes the permit, adopted Order R2-2025-0032, extending Richmond's deadline five years to 30 June 2030, longer than Alameda County's (2027) or Vallejo's (2028).
Richmond reported zero new full capture devices and zero On-Land Visual Trash Assessments, which are street inspections for trash, citing a staff shortage.
Caltrans remains under Order R2-2019-0007, which covers the I-80 and I-580 corridors, and I-80 runs along the shoreline in front of the reserve.
We have not found a trash obligation assigned to the Albany segment, no capture-device count or list of outfalls (the pipes where storm drains empty) published by Albany, and no trash hot spot, the permit's label for an area with especially heavy trash, designated for Cerrito Creek.
Creek work here, such as the $826,000 Codornices Creek Phase 4 project , carries no trash credit for the mudflats.
Where things stand, as far as we have tracked: volunteers are doing the picking up, and a cleanup removes only what has already washed in.
Founder John Kirkham told ABC7 in 2024 about the group's Grizzly Peak Boulevard work, "I would love to put our crew out of business." Worth tracking Movement would look like an annual report naming the Albany Mudflats as a receiving water, a monitoring site added on Cerrito Creek or the Albany shoreline under Provision C.8.e (the permit's monitoring section), or a trash hot-spot designation under Provision C.10 (its trash section).
A member of Congress can ask EPA Region 9 whether the mudflats or Cerrito Creek are on California's list of impaired waters under section 303(d) of the Clean Water Act and, if not, why not, and can ask the Federal Highway Administration and Caltrans District 4 what trash obligation attaches to I-80 here.
We have not found a sign either question has been put.
Adding a monitoring site or hot spot is a decision for the Regional Water Board, not EPA.
Which steps come first is a call for the cities and residents beside this shoreline, and a congressional office would work with them rather than arrive with a list.

The restoration was a $12.4M program whose Phase I cost $4.1M , paid for from Measures WW and CC, two funding measures that voters approved for the park district.
The district stated that the Albany Bulb, a former landfill on the shoreline, and the center of the Neck, the strip of shoreline land beside the Bulb, are owned by the City of Albany and were not part of the plan.
The Neck lies north of McLaughlin at the foot of Buchanan Street .
Who takes over the Bulb has been open for decades.
Transfer of the Bulb, meaning its ownership passing from the city to California State Parks and the park district, was still under negotiation in April 2025 .
Golden Gate Bird Alliance and Citizens for East Shore Parks , two conservation groups, were still campaigning through 2025 to see it completed.
We have not found later word on the transfer, and none on capping the landfill, which means covering it with a protective layer.
The Bulb was built in 1963 from contracted construction-debris disposal , meaning building rubble was hauled there under contract, and it is largely concrete and rebar, the steel bars inside reinforced concrete.
Litigation closed it in 1983 .
It is uncapped, with debris exposed at the water's edge, and its exposed face adjoins the Albany Mudflats .
The site is inspected by CalRecycle, the state's recycling and waste agency, and by the Alameda County Local Enforcement Agency, the county office that oversees landfills.
East Bay Trash Pandas' cleanups of the marsh beside it are covered in the entry on trash reaching the Albany Marsh.
The one regulator-directed investigation we identified addressed a different problem from the visible one.
Water quality regulators directed Albany in January 2024 to investigate historic Stauffer Chemical alum mud disposal at the site.
Alum mud is an aluminum-processing waste.
The Love the Bulb website, which carries updates through September 2025, reports an initial investigation found no radioactivity associated with alum mud disposal, and the Bulb remains open.
We could not confirm whether that is the final finding.
It concerns radioactivity and says nothing about the plastic, styrofoam and construction debris at the edge.
Some things remain unclear.
We have not found a study measuring how much of the uncapped edge is eroding into the mudflats, so it appears nobody has measured it, and until somebody does, the agencies that would take the land cannot put a number on what it would cost them.
We have not confirmed whether the January 2024 direction was a formal order under Water Code section 13267, the state law that lets water quality regulators require a party to investigate and report.
We have not found public opposition to the transfer.
Taking on a cost nobody can estimate is a hard position for any agency.
Worth tracking Movement would look like someone commissioning a study of how much plastic, styrofoam and construction debris is entering the mudflats and at what rate, the receiving agencies agreeing terms for managing the land, or a public update on the transfer, which we have not found since April 2025.
It is also worth asking CalRecycle and the county agency whether their inspections already hold erosion data, because if they do, the study is smaller than it looks.
Ownership and conveyance are local and state matters: Albany owns the Bulb, State Parks and the park district are the receiving agencies, and CalRecycle and the county agency oversee the landfill.
There is one federal opening, and it is money for the study nobody has commissioned.
EPA brownfields assessment grants fund this kind of characterization of an uncapped debris site, and a congressional office can help Albany apply.
Congress cannot compel a city to convey land to a state park.
The Bulb is not a Superfund site, meaning it is not on the federal list of highly contaminated sites that qualify for the cleanup program of that name, and the initial investigation did not find the radioactivity that might have opened that door.
Which steps come first is a call for Albany, the receiving agencies and residents, and a congressional office would work with them rather than arrive with a list.
If you know this shoreline, tell us what you would put first.

Jimbow (James Lee Bailey), who lived at the Albany Bulb from 1997 for 17 years, in the KALW radio story "Going Home Again to the Albany Bulb" by reporter Alastair Boone, 12 May 2025 The city removed the roughly 70-person encampment at the Albany Bulb in 2014.
The clearance ended in a lawsuit settlement that paid most residents $3,000 each , along with a 12-month stay-away order.
A decade later, a May 2025 KALW report found no current city or park district redevelopment plans for the site.
Nobody has decided what the Bulb is for.
The question matters now because planning for a regional park at Golden Gate Fields, the horse racing track property directly next door, is on the way.
Community planning for it is targeted for 2027 through 2029.
What the Bulb's users want is unusually specific for a place with no plan.
Love the Bulb, a volunteer group, continues to care for the site and runs Bulbfest , a festival held there.
The group also presses for a portable environmental and arts classroom, equipment storage , education zones and a skatepark.
The skatepark would go under the raised roadways of I-80 and Buchanan Street.
Artists, dog walkers, park users and formerly and currently unhoused residents use the site as it is, informal and unlandscaped.
The case they make is that the informality is the appeal, not a flaw to be fixed.
Amber Whitson, who lived at the Bulb from 2006 to 2014, told KALW: The variety of plants and stuff out here, it really shows that nature really will find a way.
Always, nature will find a way, if you just gotta give it a little bit of room.
Amber Whitson, former Albany Bulb resident (2006-2014), in the KALW radio story "Going Home Again to the Albany Bulb," 12 May 2025 Others see the same site differently, and their concerns are real too.
Residents worried about noise, litter and safety point to gatherings held without a permit.
The East Bay Regional Park District police , the officers of the regional agency that manages parks along the shoreline, handle enforcement there, and they warned of a planned unpermitted beach takeover at the Bulb , a gathering on the beach that was planned without a permit.
One long-running question has been answered.
The Water Board, the state agency that protects water quality, prompted a 20-month investigation into whether waste from the Stauffer Chemical company, known as alum mud, might have been dumped at the site.
The investigation concluded in September 2025 that no associated radioactivity was found and recommended no access restrictions, meaning no limits on where people can go.
That finding covers radioactivity and nothing else.
The condition of the construction debris at the site, which has not been covered with a protective cap, is a separate issue: "The uncapped Albany Bulb landfill: eroding debris beside the mudflats, transfer unfinished." Worth tracking The Bulb has no adopted long-term plan, and nothing has been scheduled that would produce one.
Movement would look like the city or the park district announcing a planning process, especially once community planning for Golden Gate Fields gets under way next door.
Whether Love the Bulb's proposals for the classroom, the storage and the skatepark get a hearing would be the next marker.
The skatepark idea sits under the I-80 and Buchanan Street structures, which stand on land the state controls for its roads (called state right-of-way), so it would involve the state as well as the city.

Cleanup offsets in the Bay trash permit Cleanup offsets and the trash permit.
Volunteers clean the Albany shoreline regularly and deserve credit.
Under the Bay Area's stormwater permit, which requires cities to keep trash from washing into creeks and the Bay, a cleanup offset lets shoreline cleanups stand in for part of the trash a city must stop upstream.
The permit normally limits offsets to 10%, but Cease and Desist Order R2-2025-0034 lets Vallejo offset 14%, and Richmond's order also directs cleanup offsets.
San Francisco Baykeeper filed a written comment that changed none of the December 2025 orders .
Worth watching: whether anyone publishes a tally for the 2025 Albany Bulb cleanup.

Together, those places span the whole city of 20,000 residents, and 1.5 positions (one part-time and one full-time worker) cover all of it.
The ARPA money did real work in Albany.
It extended Project HOPE through June 2026, after which the program goes back to pre-pandemic funding.
Albany received $4,711,702 in ARPA funds, and $3.75M had been spent by October 2025 on Project HOPE, rental assistance to 77 households averaging $8,200, Friendship Club subsidies, a grocery program and 98 small-business grants.
The council has said it wants to keep the Albany CARES navigation program, which served 668 clients in 2024 at a general fund cost of roughly $350,000 a year, and to keep rental assistance.
It reallocated $50,000 to extend rental aid through 2026 .
On 18 August 2026 the city held a Homeowners and Tenants Resource Fair with about 20 city departments and local agencies.
The fair took the place of the ordinances the council had declined to adopt (the tenant protection rules the council chose not to pass), and it offered residents help with rent review, ECHO Housing (a housing counseling organization), and retrofit and rebate programs.
So far, Albany has not put in place a general fund line to carry Albany CARES, rental assistance and Project HOPE past the ARPA deadline, or more than 1.5 outreach positions for the whole city.
There is also the question of renewing the Project HOPE contract.
The case for renewal is that it keeps an established outreach and navigation program running without a break, that a specialized provider may do the work better than a service built in-house from scratch, and that unhoused residents rely on continuity in the relationships outreach workers build.
The case against is that contracting out rather than staffing in-house limits the city's direct oversight, and that multi-year contracts can lock in a funding level after needs or performance change.
The renewal paperwork lists no cost figures and no outcome measures, and no discussion was recorded, only the decision.
Worth tracking The date to watch is December 2026, when all ARPA money must be spent.
The signs of movement would be a general fund line of roughly $350,000 a year for Albany CARES and rental assistance, and outreach staffing above 1.5 positions.
If neither appears before ARPA runs out, the program falls back to pre-pandemic funding, and the same two people carry the whole city.
The federal lever that fits is help with existing grant programs.
The U.S.
Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) funds street outreach and housing navigation of the kind Project HOPE and Albany CARES provide.
It does so through its Emergency Solutions Grant program and its Continuum of Care program.
The Continuum of Care is a local network of agencies that coordinates homeless services and applies for that money, and the money flows through the Alameda County Continuum of Care.
At roughly $350,000 a year, the gap is small enough that those accounts are a plausible fit.
A congressional office can press HUD and the county on that application.

Continuum of Care and Emergency Solutions money is run by the county and awarded competitively, so it is not a guaranteed amount for Albany.
Whether Albany pays for the program from its general fund after December 2026 is the council's decision.

The November 2026 ballot measures The November 2026 ballot.
Albany's 3 November 2026 ballot carries six measures.
Measures P, Q, R and S restructure existing taxes: P (street paving and storm drains, about $1.4M a year), Q (emergency medical and fire protection, about $2.5M), R (business licenses, about $600,000) and S (property transfers, about $1M).

Albany's budget gap and pension debt The city budget and pension debt.
The two-year budget adopted on 2 June 2025 draws $1.15M from reserves to balance FY2026/27.
It also names the city's unfunded pension liability, the gap between what it owes workers through CalPERS (the state pension system) and what it has set aside, at $45.2M as of 30 June 2023.
Annual payments on it were $3.3M in FY24/25 and are projected to peak at $5.1M in 2031.
Audited figures presented in January 2026 show the general fund balance fell $1.63M to $10.05M.
