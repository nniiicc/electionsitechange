Shoreline adaptation on an island: $290M needed, $26.45M secured
On 3 and 4 January 2026, king tides, which are unusually high tides, flooded Crab Cove, paths on Bay Farm Island, Alameda Point and the marinas.
"This is a preview of the future," Zoe Siegel, an advocate with Greenbelt Alliance, told the Alameda Post in an interview on 4 January 2026.
She urged nature-based solutions, meaning protection that works with features such as beaches and marshes.
The tide gauge that the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) runs at station 9414750 recorded 9.192 feet during that event, against 7.906 feet in January 2024.
The tides were reported as 2.5 feet above normal and the highest since 1998.
Bay Farm, East End and marina residents watched that flooding and have been asking where the protection is.
Residents facing repeat flooding have watched the city add $3.4M, through a contract amendment, to its work with a landscape-architecture firm while the plan is still being studied, and they want to see construction.
The city has been working on an answer, and what it has funded so far is design rather than construction.
Council accepted $2.64M in State Coastal Conservancy money (the Conservancy is a California state agency that funds coastal projects) to take Bay Farm Island from 30% to 60% design, meaning to move the engineering plans from 30% complete to 60% complete, with construction targeted for 2030.
It also applied for a $1.6M Bay Restoration Authority grant (the Authority is a regional agency that funds Bay shoreline projects) for South Shore concept design, meaning early design work that lays out the basic idea of a project.
The design would cover Elsie Roemer Bird Sanctuary, eelgrass expansion (restoring underwater grass beds in the bay) and beach nourishment (adding sand to a beach).
Requests for about $1.78M in federal money for the Alameda Point stormwater and slope-stabilization project (work to manage rain runoff and to keep the shoreline ground from sliding) came from Rep.
Simon, Sen.
Padilla and Sen.
Schiff.
They were still pending as of August 2026.
The scale is what makes this hard.
Alameda has identified $290 million of near-term shoreline adaptation need, meaning work to protect the shoreline from rising water, and holds $26.45 million against it, about 9% of the total.
Along the way, roughly $54 million in federal support that had been awarded to the city was rescinded, meaning it was taken back.
The city's sustainability manager, Danielle Mieler, confirmed the loss of the federal money and that the city has shifted to seeking state and regional money instead.
A December 2025 workshop set out four projects, with these costs: the Estuary at $120M, Bay Farm Island at $75M, South Shore at $75M and Alameda Point at $20M.
The citywide adaptation plan, which is meant to set out how the city will protect its whole shoreline from rising water, is due in fall 2027.
The objection residents raise is not that the risk is invented.
It is that money is going to consulting firms for design work while the plan is still being studied and construction has not begun.
So far the money has paid for design rather than building, and construction on Bay Farm Island is a 2030 target.
Both positions can be held in good faith.
Worth tracking
Three things are worth watching.
First, whether the citywide adaptation plan is finished on schedule in fall 2027.
Second, whether Congress approves the $1.78M federal request for the Alameda Point stormwater and slope-stabilization project, which was still pending as of August 2026.
Third, whether Bay Farm Island reaches 60% design (60% of the engineering plans complete) on the way to its 2030 construction target.
Money committed to actual construction would show the issue moving.

A separate contamination problem on the same site is further along.
Chevron planned to excavate soil to seven feet in targeted areas at Alameda Point in spring 2026, a job expected to take one to two months.
The aim is to remove tar waste from a refinery it operated between 1879 and 1903 (the Alameda Post dates it to the 1880s).
The work is under a 2021 order of the San Francisco Bay Regional Water Quality Control Board, the state agency that oversees cleanups that could affect water.
The plan for the cleanup, called a corrective action plan, is still under review.
That work has a lead agency, an order and a schedule.
The radium dispute has none of the three.
The cost of the deadlock falls on a party that is not one of the two agencies in dispute.
Every year Building 5 cannot be conveyed is a year the city cannot lease or redevelop it.

That bears directly on how the city will pay for Alameda Point's $900M in infrastructure work.
Neither side of the dispute is described as wanting the land left contaminated.
Each is applying its own rules, and the state health department's background-matching approach is the more protective one.
Some residents might read any risk-based number as a weakening of the standard, which makes this a hard problem to settle.
Worth tracking

On 21 July 2026 the mayor and vice mayor moved to eliminate the elected City Auditor and City Treasurer, offices created in 1937 that cost about $84,000 a year in benefits.
No vote was called.
Two charter measures, changes to the city's governing document, go to voters in November 2026: Measure M, with 21 provisions, and Measure N, on city leases and property sales.
The city is also negotiating only with Neptune Beach Surf Club Development Partners for a surf pool on public parkland, and SurfLoch, a competing developer, challenged the exclusive talks.
Whether the lease terms become public is worth watching.
School district budget and reserves
The school district budget.
Alameda Unified School District staff reported on 11 August 2026 that the 2026-27 year had opened $6.83 million better than budget, helped by a cost-of-living adjustment of 4.31% and an $8 million one-time grant.
The longer view differs: the district projects its reserves falling from $8.88 million to $170,000 by 2028-29, and a four-year deficit of up to $14 million, with no permanent fix yet.
The board unanimously approved labor agreements for 2025-28, and bond-funded building work continues.
Whether later budget updates move the reserve projection is worth watching.
AUSD curriculum and personnel decisions made over community objection
School board decisions parents questioned.
On 26 May 2026 the Alameda Unified School District board voted 4-0 to approve a $1.4 million English language arts curriculum, after parents and some teachers said it relies on excerpts rather than full books.
On 23 June 2026 the board approved personnel actions 4-0, and families asked why baseball coach Ken Arnerich Sr. and three assistants were not renewed; personnel law limits what the district can say.
Trustee Jennifer Williams resigned effective 12 August 2026, and the board opened an appointment process instead of a special election.
Worth watching: whether staff add the promised novels, and the November vote for two full-term seats.
The Wadi Foquin sister-city agreement: 50 speakers, an organized letter campaign, no decision
The Wadi Foquin sister-city proposal.
A sister city is a city abroad, here a village in the West Bank, that a city forms a formal partnership with.
On 18 March 2025 the Alameda City Council heard 50 speakers, 37 of them in support, and had 292 pages of letters, about 75% of them opposed.
The council then voted 4-1 to extend the process for more community engagement.
The agreement has been neither adopted nor withdrawn, and nothing is known to be scheduled.
Worth watching: whether it returns to a council agenda, or whether the city adopts a stated standard for sister-city relationships that touch an international conflict.
Home Electrification Fairs and the upfront cost they do not cover
Home electrification fairs.
On 3 May 2025 the City of Alameda and Alameda Municipal Power (AMP), the city's electric utility, held a Home Electrification Fair, where council attended and homeowners could get rebate details for replacing gas appliances with electric ones.
The Alameda Post reported that the upfront cost can be high and that AMP prices run about 45% lower than Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E).
Beyond AMP's own rebates, no local program helps with that cost, and the federal tax credits residents were pointed to ended for equipment put to use after 2025.
Worth watching: local money for the upfront cost, from the city or AMP.
