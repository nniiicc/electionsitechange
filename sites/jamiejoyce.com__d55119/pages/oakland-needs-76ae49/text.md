The status open is the city's own label, and it does not say whether anyone has visited.
The city is putting real money into paving.
It has been committing more than $50 million a year in bond proceeds to the streets that are overdue for repaving, and the Oaklandside catalogued the worst-rated streets in December 2025 from the city's own pavement condition data.
The bond sale is where the schedule slipped.
CBS reported that the finance department did not sell Measure U bonds in the fall of 2024, which left a funding gap of possibly as much as $55 million.
In May 2025, KTVU reported that the city's transportation director expected to pave only 20 miles of road in 2025 because of the delayed sale, compared with the 50 or 60 miles he had hoped to pave each year.
The same report said Oakland paid $27 million in pothole lawsuit settlements from 2018 to 2023, the most of any major Bay Area city.
The city's side is that the money is committed and the constraints are real.
Drainage, sewer work required by a consent decree (a court-approved agreement) and street lighting all have claims on the same construction budget, and that trade-off is hard.
Where things stand, as far as we have tracked: paving continues at a reduced scale, we have not located a revised schedule with mileage targets, and about two in five pothole requests remain open.
Worth tracking Three things would show movement: a revised paving schedule tied to the bond proceeds, with annual mileage targets; 2026 mileage compared with the 50 to 60 miles hoped for; and a falling share of pothole requests still open, now 43%.
Oakland's paving is paid for by bonds approved through ballot measures (Measure U and Measure KK) and by the city's capital plan, its multi-year list of construction spending.
Federal help comes in two kinds.
Surface Transportation Block Grant money is a formula program, divided among regions by a set formula, and it reaches Oakland through the Metropolitan Transportation Commission (MTC), the Bay Area's regional transportation planning agency.
Its rules largely limit it to arterials and collectors (larger streets that carry traffic between neighborhoods), so it generally cannot be spent on residential streets.
A direct appropriation request, which is how a member of Congress asks Congress to fund a specific local project, does not carry those rules, and residential paving has been funded that way.
Competitive programs such as RAISE (Rebuilding American Infrastructure with Sustainability and Equity grants) and Safe Streets and Roads for All (a street safety grant program) can also fund rebuilding a corridor with new pavement.
A request could cover part of the backlog or more, depending on its cost, which the city would need to supply.

Federal help comes in two kinds.
Sidewalk repair for low-income owners is an eligible use of the Community Development Block Grant (CDBG), a federal grant cities receive by formula for housing and neighborhood improvements.
The Department of Housing and Urban Development's Section 108 program lets a city borrow against its future CDBG funds, which could pay for a large share of repairs up front.
Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a federal civil rights law, requires a city to keep a transition plan, a written schedule for making curb ramps and other pedestrian facilities usable (28 CFR 35.150, part of the federal regulations).
A congressional office can ask the city for that plan and ask the Justice Department whether it is current,.
The limits are specific: CDBG is divided by population and poverty, not by the size of a repair backlog, and the ADA covers curb ramps at crossings rather than every damaged panel.
A direct appropriation request, in which a member of Congress asks Congress to fund one named project, is not sized by a formula and could cover part or all of the cost of a set of repairs, depending on the number.

Oakland's municipal code also sets time limits for putting a street back after digging.
As we understand Section 12.12.112 of the code , whoever holds the permit to dig must backfill and compact the excavation within 72 hours of starting work, place the pavement base material within 72 hours after that, and finish the trench paving within 72 hours after the base is in.
The city publishes an application for a utility excavation permit for companies that dig in streets.
Some trenches are work in progress, and the data cannot show whether a trench behind a request was still within those limits.
The census also does not say whether the city, a utility or a contractor dug it.
Where things stand, hedged to what we can see: requests about trench paving and lids are mostly open, while requests about missing manhole covers are mostly closed.
That may mean the city has a quick way to close one kind of hazard and a slower path for the others, but the data does not give a reason.
Worth tracking What would show movement: the open share of trench paving requests (87%) and lid requests (61%) falling, the requests from October 2024 being closed, and the share of downed-wire requests not yet closed (97%) dropping.
A published count of trenches that were restored within the 72-hour limits would also help residents see how the rule works in practice.
Most of this belongs to the city's permit rules and to the state, where the California Public Utilities Commission oversees electric, gas and phone utilities, so we do not see an obvious federal lever in the trench rules themselves.
The office could still ask the city and the utilities for public answers about specific open requests, and could bring them together with residents.

For residents, that shows up as dark blocks and dead signals.
At East 12th Street and 16th Avenue in the San Antonio district, a stolen signal was replaced with stop signs in May 2024, which is a cut in service rather than a repair.

In April 2026, cable cuts near Skyline Boulevard and Keller Avenue left homes in the hills without internet three times in one week , and AT&T offered a $20,000 reward for information.
The West Oakland Neighborhood Crime Prevention Council has asked for solar lights along a dark stretch of Mandela Parkway .
The city is working on it.

It is also a hard problem.
Electrical repair competes with sidewalks, sanitation and parks for the same public works budget.

Airport sponsors accept FAA grant assurances, and Assurance 22(i) permits a sponsor to exclude a category of aircraft activity only where that is necessary for safe operation or to serve the needs of civil aviation.
An objection to a cargo's destination is neither.

An exception at (b)(3) lets an airport owner set certain rules in its role as the owner, but the courts have limited that exception to matters of noise and access.
