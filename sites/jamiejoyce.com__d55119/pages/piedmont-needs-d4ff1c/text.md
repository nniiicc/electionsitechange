Piedmont Play School's eviction from 401 Hampton Road and the recreation space squeeze behind it
Piedmont Play School is a cooperative preschool that parents help run, and it has operated for 77 years.
It has used the city-owned building at 401 Hampton Road under a 25-year agreement with the city.
That agreement expired in June 2025.
In September 2025 the city announced that it would not renew it.
More than 130 Piedmont families signed a letter opposing the decision and asking for a public hearing.
On 3 November 2025 the city put out a statement explaining itself, and this is the reason it gave:
increasingly severe space constraints due to aging infrastructure,
City of Piedmont, official statement on the Piedmont Play School decision, 3 November 2025
The city's reasoning is documented, and the space problem behind it appears to be real.
The Recreation Commission's April 2026 annual report is the commission's first comprehensive report in over a decade.
It records growing demand for recreation programs and limits on the facilities that house them, and the city says it currently rents private space for its own programs.
The city's statement on the decision cites severe space constraints and aging infrastructure.
A growing list of programs and too few rooms to hold them is a hard position for any city, and Piedmont is a small one.
The city offered the school three things: free use of the building through December 2025, a $37 hourly rate to use the building from January to 15 June 2026, and a twelve-month transition while the school's time at the building comes to an end.
The decision was handled in closed session, which is a meeting held out of public view.
No public hearing was granted beforehand, and none has been held since.
That is what the families are responding to.
Letters to the Piedmont Exedra, a local news site, called the move a breach of community trust.
Others took the opposite view, and a letter from a former student argued that the site is not sacred.
The families ask a specific question that the city has not answered.
Which other city space, including 358 Hillside Avenue, is underused, and how does its use compare with that of 401 Hampton Road?
The complaint about broken trust is about how the decision was made more than about whether the Recreation Department needs the room.
Worth tracking
The key deadline is the end of the twelve-month transition.
Within it, three things are worth watching.
The first is whether the city publishes usage figures showing how much its facilities are used, including 358 Hillside Avenue.
The second is whether it holds the public hearing the families asked for.
The third is whether it says if an alternative site is on offer and on what terms.
If none of those happens, the matter is stuck.
There is no federal role in the decision itself.
The office could still ask the city questions on the record if residents want that, and it would work with the city rather than arrive with a list.
Which of the three things above should come first is a call for the city and its residents, and if you have a view, we want to hear it.
The use agreement, the decision not to renew it, the closed-session handling and the refusal of a hearing belong to the City Council and the Recreation Department.
They are governed by California's Brown Act, the state open-meeting law that sets rules for when a public body may meet in private.
The federal child care programs, the Child Care and Development Block Grant and Head Start, pay for child care slots and subsidies.
They do not pay for building disputes.

Severe e-bike head injuries among Piedmont children, and the push for licensing standards
Dr.
James Crawford-Jakubiak, a pediatrician at Children's Hospital Oakland, spoke to the Piedmont City Council on August 17, 2026 about e-bikes, meaning electric bicycles.
The bikes he described weigh 50 to 60 pounds, can reach 20 to 28 mph, and are ridden by increasingly young riders.
What he sees at the hospital, he told the council, is "a dramatic increase in severe head injuries... every week".
His testimony is the only evidence of a trend so far, and it comes from the clinician who receives the patients.
Piedmont has not adopted any local rule on e-bikes.
The city has begun to respond, and its response so far is education rather than enforcement.
Police Chief Frederick Shavies presented an education-before-enforcement approach in August 2026, in which the city teaches riders and families about safe use before it penalizes anyone.
The city would coordinate with the Piedmont Unified School District (PUSD) and parent groups.
The chief described the risk this way: "The concern arises when young or inexperienced riders are operating a vehicle that is capable of significant speed." Vice Mayor Conna McCarthy expressed interest in licensing standards, meaning a set of requirements for riding e-bikes.
The City Administrator, the city's top appointed manager, called it the beginning of a conversation.
Two bills are also pending in the state legislature.
The education program has not yet been delivered.
The concerns about moving quickly to licensing are practical ones.
Younger riders use e-bikes to get to school.
Licensing would be difficult to enforce.
And any fines would be paid by families.
The case for something more than education rests on the injuries the pediatrician described.
Dr.
Crawford-Jakubiak says the injuries arrive at the hospital every week.
The question for the city is whether to act before its conversation about licensing has finished.
Worth tracking
Two local markers matter.
One is whether the education program with PUSD and parent groups is actually delivered.
The other is whether the council takes up rules on e-bike speed, on modifications that make e-bikes faster, or on licensing standards, which are the decisions that are entirely Piedmont's.
The two pending state bills will likely be decided before any federal action could take effect, so what happens to them matters too.
There is a federal lever, but it is slow.
The Consumer Product Safety Commission, the federal agency that oversees the safety of consumer products, has legal authority over e-bikes and can write federal rules on battery safety.
It is the only federal body that could set a speed classification standard, a national category system that sorts e-bikes by how fast they go.
The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, which regulates motor vehicles, cannot set one, because its authority does not extend to bicycles.
A federal speed class would define what a 50-to-60-pound bike capable of 20 to 28 mph may be sold as, and what a modification kit, an add-on that changes how a bike performs, may lawfully do to it.
A congressional office could press the commission on whether it will set one and could back legislation directing it to.
That is authorizing work, which changes what an agency is directed to do, rather than an appropriation, which is a decision about spending.
A federal standard is unlikely to arrive soon enough to affect the current discussion in Piedmont.
Commission rulemaking, the formal process of writing a federal rule, runs for years, and the state bills will likely be decided first.
The priority call belongs to Piedmont and its residents.
We would work with the city rather than arrive with a list, and we would like to hear what you would put first: education, speed rules, limits on modifications, licensing, or something else.

We Oaklanders have been kept in the dark throughout the process and we are affected more than anyone.
This is not safe!
Jane Pingatore Holland, an Oakland resident near Moraga Canyon, in a letter to the editor of the Piedmont Exedra, 26 September 2025
Holland is not alone in that concern.
Neighbors on Moraga Avenue and at Blair Park, joined by residents of adjacent Oakland, say the street carries 11,000 vehicles a day at speeds of 40 mph and is the only way out of Moraga Canyon.
They say evacuating residents and emergency vehicles would share the same two lanes in a wildfire.
They also say that problems with sight distance, meaning how far ahead a driver can see in order to stop safely, were flagged in a 2010 assessment by Caltrans, the California Department of Transportation, and have never been resolved.
