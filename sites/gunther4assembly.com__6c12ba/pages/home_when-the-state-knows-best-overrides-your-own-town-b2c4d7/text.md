When “The State Knows Best” Overrides Your Own Town
By John Gunther, Candidate for NYS Assembly, District 114
Something is happening in Ticonderoga worth every one of us paying attention to, not because of which party is involved, but because of a much simpler question: should an unelected state office be able to override what your own town decides?
Citizens of Ti are scared to death about a proposed 50 MW lithium-ion battery storage facility, situated on 12 acres within the town. 56 large shipping containers of batteries within 3,000 feet of their hospital, elementary school and Elderwood assisted living facility.
What’s Currently Happening
Ticonderoga’s town board unanimously passed a moratorium on battery energy storage systems (BESS) this year, after a public hearing drew so many concerned residents { by the town clerk’s own estimate, more than 250 people} that it had to be moved to the high school auditorium to fit everyone.
The current moratorium expires this November.
The Town of Ti has created a task force to study the health and safety issues.
I attended a Sept. 23 presentation by the panel which was well attended albeit the absence of NY Assemblyman Matt Simpson and State Senator Dan Stec.
Residents continue to raise real, specific concerns: fire risk from lithium-ion batteries, which can’t be extinguished with water and have to be allowed to burn out; groundwater contamination; a buzzing noise from the inverters; property values plummeting near the proposed site.
The task force recommended extending the town moratorium while they complete their study, then hold a town referendum, yes or no vote on the project.
I strongly suspect the vote will be a clear NO as there is absolutely no benefits to the town, only liabilities.
I see the fight ending up in court and, as history shows us, the side with the deeper pockets usually prevails.
Here’s the part that should concern every resident of this 114th Assembly District, regardless of how you feel about renewable energy itself: it may not matter what Ticonderoga decides.
ORES
A town can say no to a battery plant today.
A bill in Albany would take that away.
New York’s Office of Renewable Energy Siting (ORES) was created in 2019 to fast-track the permitting process for renewable energy projects of 25 megawatts or larger, regardless of local moratoriums or zoning laws.
A local NO vote, a unanimous town board decision, hundreds of concerned residents at a packed hearing, none of this may matter.
Pending legislation (S5506 / A8378) would move standalone battery storage under ORES.
That would give the state the power to override local moratoriums.
Battery-industry advocates are backing it.
This isn’t just a Ticonderoga problem.
New York currently leads the nation with more than 90 active local moratoriums on battery storage projects, spread across 35 counties.
Towns across our district and the wider Adirondack region including Northampton, St.
Armand, Crown Point, Hadley and Dresden are having versions of this same fight right now.
Brookfield and a Possible Conflict of Interest
I want to be clear about something: I support renewable energy.
Getting off fossil fuels matters, and New York needs real investment in clean power.
But supporting renewable energy and supporting due process are not in conflict.
In fact, I’d argue you can’t have durable, lasting clean energy infrastructure without real buy-in from the communities hosting it.
Brookfield Renewable is the company working with ORES to secure permits to install and operate the battery installations in Ticonderoga and in the Town of Dresden, Washington County.
The company operates 74 hydropower facilities and 3 wind farms across New York.
The battery storage business is a new venture, new revenue source for them.
For sure, it will be a big profit center.
At the Ti task force meeting on Sept. 23, the head electrical engineer on the committee presented a pro forma chart of these potential windfalls.
Brookfield would buy power from National Grid when electricity is cheapest, middle of the night around $27/unit, store the electricity in batteries and resell it back to National Grid during peak demand at maybe $76/unit.
This will happen 365 days per year.
So then the obvious question, is BESS really a green technology or just a cash cow for their investors.
I asked my science advisor, Dr.
Mihai Beretea, chemist and mathematician what he thought about BESS, “these battery storage solutions are a bridge to nowhere.”
I also find it concerning that the acting executive director of ORES, Jason Zehr, is a former employee of Brookfield.
I haven’t seen evidence of specific wrongdoing, and people are allowed to have a career in an industry before regulating it.
But when an agency with this much override power over local communities is being run, even temporarily, by someone who came from the industry it regulates, that’s a fair thing for the public to ask questions about.
This is not an accusation, but a legitimate transparency issue.
Where I Stand
I don’t think this is about Republicans versus Democrats.
In fact, ORES was created under a Democratic majority, and some of the most vocal pushback against its override authority right now is coming from Senate Republicans.
I think that tells you this issue cuts across party lines, the way local control usually does.
If elected, here’s what I’d support: Real transparency requirements for ORES: A public dashboard of pending projects, mandatory consultation with municipalities before applications are even deemed complete, and written justification whenever the agency overrides a local law.
A meaningful role for the towns themselves.
Not just a comment period that gets summarized and set aside, but real weight given to a community’s own decision when it’s made through its own legitimate local process.
Honest, non-defensive answers about ORES’s own leadership and process, especially while it operates without a permanent director.
None of this is about stopping clean energy.
It’s about making sure the way we get there doesn’t run straight over the people who actually live where these projects get built.
Ticonderoga deserves a real answer to a real question, and so does every other town in this district facing the same fight.
Lastly, and I don’t want to make this a partisan issue, but where is Matt Simpson, our incumbent Assemblyman for the 114th District?
It seems to me; the first responsibility of an elected official is to protect their constituents.
This is clearly dereliction of duty.
If you’re not going to fight for your voters, then it’s time for you to get out of the way!
