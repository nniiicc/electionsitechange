Meet John News Endorsements Volunteer Voter Information Donate by Mail Contribute Home / Why I Support the New York Health Act 18 Sep Friday, 8:07 AM · 2026 Why I Support the New York Health Act Why I Support the New York Health Act By John Gunther, Candidate for NYS Assembly, District 114 Here’s the short version: Under the New York Health Act, most families would pay less than they do now once premiums, deductibles, copays and surprise bills disappear.
On top of that, county and school property taxes would come down, because local governments would stop footing the bill for employee health insurance and the current NYS mandated share for Medicaid.
Small business, start-ups and farmers will greatly benefit by not having to compete with large corporations for talented people who can afford to offer health insurance as part of their employment package.
That's the case I will lay out here with supporting documentation.
Previously, I wrote to you about why I support the New York Health Act, as someone who spent 49 years running a manufacturing business here in the North Country, watching health insurance go from affordable to out of reach for most small and mid-sized businesses.
That email raised a fair question I will answer here: How would this actually work, and what would it mean for the North Country specifically?
How It Would Actually Be Funded Here's the bottom line.
What you pay today in premiums, deductibles, copays, and surprise bills would go down.
The New York Health Act is not funded through these costs, the ones families feel most acutely today.
Instead, it's funded three ways: A progressive graduated payroll tax; paid at least 80% by employers and no more than 20% by employees (100% for the self-employed).
The first $25,000 of an individual's income and $50,000 for Medicare recipients is tax exempt.
A progressive graduated tax on non-wage income; capital gains, dividends, and interest.
This means investment income is taxed on the same ability-to-pay principle as ordinary income from wages.
The plan redirects the money NYS state currently spends on state and local Medicaid back into the New York Health Act.
Pending a Congressional waiver, all Federal Medicare and Medicaid dollars received will instead flow into the NYHA general fund.
I won't pretend this is a small tax increase - it isn't, and no one should tell you otherwise.
The real comparison is what you already pay in premiums, deductibles, copays, and surprise bills versus what you'd pay through this progressive tax instead.
When researchers at the RAND Corporation modeled the plan for the New York State Health Foundation, they projected that roughly 90% of New Yorkers would come out ahead financially once premiums and out-of-pocket costs disappeared.
Right now, all these costs function like a flat tax that hits middle and lower-income families the hardest.
That said, I think it's fair to note this is a projection, not a guarantee.
The Piece Most People Miss: Property Tax Relief And here's the part that doesn't come up enough: the New York Health Act could meaningfully lower property taxes too for our counties and small towns we live in here in the North Country.
A May 2026 analysis by Results for Development and the League of Women Voters of New York State looked at something most single-payer debates skip entirely.
What happens to county, town and school district budgets under the NYHA?
The answer is striking.
Local governments currently spend enormous sums covering health insurance for public employees; teachers, police officers, town highway crews, county workers.
New York State mandates that counties pay a large share of Medicaid costs.
New York's counties collectively pay about $7.6 billion a year into Medicaid, the largest local Medicaid burden of any state in the country.
That's money raised largely through our property taxes.
The study looked at what would happen if that spending were replaced by the NYHA's payroll tax instead using real, audited 2025 budget data.
In Suffolk County, the math showed a 92% reduction in health insurance related costs, savings equal to 18.3% of the entire county budget, or roughly $497 per resident.
The Town of Islip would save 13.7% of its budget.
The local school district saved 12.1% or $890 per person.
In total, residents of that one school district could see combined savings of roughly $1,437 per person when county, town, and school district effects are stacked together.
This wasn't a one-town fluke.
The researchers repeated the analysis across 13 counties, 17 municipalities and 22 school districts spanning every region of the state.
They found the same pattern every time; an average 90-95% reduction in health insurance costs at the county level, and 80-85% at the municipal and school-district levels.
This averaged a 12-14% reduction to the overall total local budgets.
Why does the savings run so deep for local governments specifically?
The study found the following.
Public employee health plans tend to be more generous and costlier than the average private plan.
Local governments, unlike most private employers, are usually on the hook for retiree health coverage.
For every teacher, town clerk, and county worker in Warren, Washington, and Essex Counties, NYHA costs would be less than what's being paid today.
None of the counties in this specific study were in our district.
I want to be clear about that.
The size and cost structure of a place like Suffolk County isn't identical to Essex, Warren or Washington Counties.
But the underlying mechanics; the county Medicaid share, the rising cost of public employee health plans, the property tax pressure applies here just as much as anywhere else in the state.
If anything, small rural towns and counties feel this pressure more acutely, because health costs make up a larger share of a smaller overall budget.
That means school boards will be freed up to invest in classrooms instead of health premiums.
County budgets with room to fix roads and bridges instead of just keeping pace with insurance inflation.
And, potentially, real relief on property tax bills that hit small businesses, family farms, and fixed-income seniors here harder than almost anywhere else in the state.
Didn’t they try this in Vermont?
If you've followed this debate at all, someone has probably mentioned Vermont to you.
In 2014, Vermont abandoned its own single-payer plan, Green Mountain Care, before it ever launched.
It's become the go-to example opponents raise whenever single-payer comes up.
I think it's worth examining.
Here's what actually happened: Vermont's plan didn't collapse because single-payer is impossible.
It collapsed because of a string of specific, avoidable planning failures.
Federal funding came in far below what the state had projected.
Savings projections were over estimated.
A recession hit at the worst possible time and a benefit package that got more generous over time without adjusting the financing plan.
Vermont's governor pulled the plug when the math caused by these mistakes showed the plan to be too disruptive to the state's economy.
This a story about bad planning, not proof that single payer health plan can't work.
But here’s why New York is in a fundamentally different position than Vermont.
It's not simply that New York has more people.
It's that New York has a dramatically larger and more diverse tax base to draw from.
New York's state economy is roughly $2.47 trillion.
If New York were its own country, that would rank as one of the ten to twelve largest economies on Earth, ahead of Canada, Russia, and South Korea.
Vermont's entire economy is about $46 billion, comparable to a small country like Bolivia.
New York's economy is roughly 50 times the size of Vermont's.
This difference matters for how a plan like this holds up under pressure.
A progressive tax on payroll and investment income works best when there's a large, deep, diversified base of income to draw from, including a concentration of high-income earners and personal investment income.
New York has what Vermont simply does not.
When a state the size of Vermont hits an unexpected shortfall like a federal waiver that comes in $160 million under projection, a gap that has to be closed by a comparatively small population and a comparatively small tax base, is exactly what forced Vermont to raise its tax rate estimates so high it made the plan untenable.
New York absorbing a proportional shortfall would face a far smaller relative hit, spread across a vastly larger and wealthier base.
None of this means New York's version is risk free, and I'd never tell you otherwise.
But the specific failures that doomed Vermont; Badly overestimated federal funding, a benefit expansion with no matching revenue plan, a recession hitting at the worst possible moment are lessons New York can learn from.
New York starts from a fundamentally stronger financial position to absorb the kind of surprises that sank Vermont's plan in the first place.
Where I Stand I don't think this is a simple issue, and I don't think anyone should pretend it is.
It's a genuinely large change, it deserves real scrutiny, and the people asking hard questions about cost and transition aren't wrong to ask them.
Some of my fellow Democrats think that the Federal Government should fix our current very broken system as do I.
However, based on what I have seen over the last 25 years, I’ll be long gone before they do.
New York State, the Empire State is in a unique position to be the first to tear down the collapsing house and build anew.
If it does this, California will follow and then, the rest of the nation.
I've spent five decades watching the current system fail small businesses, family farms and working people in the North Country in slow motion.
I believe New York has the chance to get this right and when elected, I intend to make sure the New York Health Act gets sponsored, debated and voted for on the floor of the New York State Assembly.
If you'd like to go deeper on any of this - the funding model or the property tax analysis - I'm glad to point you to the sources.
