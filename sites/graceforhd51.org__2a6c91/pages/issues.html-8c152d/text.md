Real-World Context Holloman is home to the 49th Wing, training F-16 and MQ-9 Reaper pilots (including allied Spanish, British, and Italian aircrews) and shares water rights and regional infrastructure with the City of Alamogordo.
Alamogordo and Holloman won the 2025 Altus Community Support Award for the second time in three years, recognized in part for the Alamogordo Thrive Guide connecting incoming military families to housing, childcare, healthcare, and spouse-employment help, and for securing $34.2 million for a new Holloman Elementary School and $59.6 million for Holloman Middle School.
New military-adjacent housing — including the Sendero Alamo Apartments — is underway to address a real housing shortage for enlisted families; 2026 Basic Allowance for Housing rates rose nearly 12% for the Alamogordo Military Housing Area, reflecting how tight the local rental market has become.
Spouse employment remains one of the hardest parts of a Holloman assignment, given Alamogordo's small civilian job market.
PFAS groundwater contamination has been documented in and around the base since 2016 — a known concern for both base personnel and neighboring well users.
MainGate United, the Alamogordo Chamber of Commerce, the Otero County Commission, the City, and Alamogordo Public Schools have formally adopted the "Five and Thrive" framework and "Project R.I.S.E." to coordinate community support for the base.
Grace's Policy Commitments I will work across party lines to protect Holloman's mission and advocate at every opportunity — budget hearings, capital outlay requests, and constituent casework — for the base's long-term viability and growth.
I will champion continued state support for military-family housing, childcare access, and spouse-employment programs, building on what the Thrive Guide and base partners have already started.
I will push for state and federal accountability on the PFAS contamination near Holloman, including testing support and remediation funding for affected residents and well owners off-base.
I will support veteran-owned small business development and workforce pipelines that let military spouses and transitioning veterans build careers in Otero County instead of leaving the area.
I will continue Alamogordo's tradition of greeting Honor Flight veterans home on New York Avenue, and I will fight for the benefits, healthcare access, and recognition every veteran in this district has earned.
02 Seniors, Caregivers & Disability Services Grace lived this pillar before she ran for office — as a former CNA at Casa Arena and a family caregiver, not as an outside observer.
Real-World Context Otero County operates senior centers in Alamogordo, Tularosa, and three Sacramento Mountains sites — a sprawling rural geography that makes transportation and home-delivered services harder to sustain than in a single city.
The New Mexico Health Care Authority maintains a local office serving Otero County aging and disability services, alongside a county-published Otero County Resource Guide listing dozens of senior, caregiver, and disability service contacts.
Rural New Mexico communities, including Otero County, face a well-documented shortage of in-home care workers and certified nursing assistants — the same workforce gap Grace worked inside of directly.
Disability advocacy in New Mexico increasingly intersects with the child-welfare and aging systems alike, with advocates pushing for better tracking and protection of benefits owed to vulnerable New Mexicans, including children aging out of foster care.
Grace's Policy Commitments I will fight for state investment in home- and community-based care, so seniors in Alamogordo, Tularosa, and the Sacramento Mountains communities can age in place with dignity instead of being forced to leave the district for care.
I will support pay and training investments for CNAs and direct-care workers — I've done this job, and I know retention starts with respect and a living wage.
I will work to expand transportation options linking rural Otero County seniors to medical appointments, senior centers, and pharmacies.
I will be a consistent voice for disability rights at the Roundhouse, including protecting benefits owed to disabled New Mexicans and ensuring state agencies are transparent stewards of those funds.
I will support caregiver respite programs so family caregivers — like I once was — aren't forced to choose between caregiving and their own financial stability.
03 Children, Families & CYFD Reform This is personal for Grace, and it is also one of the most urgent statewide accountability issues in the Legislature right now.
Real-World Context New Mexico's Kevin S. v.
CYFD settlement (originally filed 2018) continues to drive court-enforced reform; a new Stipulated Remedial Order (adopted March 2026) set fresh benchmarks on caseworker caseloads, foster-family recruitment, timely wellness checks, and handling of critical incidents.
The 2025 Legislature created an independent Office of the Child Advocate (House Bill 5) inside the Department of Justice, with authority to investigate CYFD, take complaints, and subpoena records.
Reporting through early 2026 documented foster youth cycling between CYFD offices and youth homeless-shelter "couch stays" due to a continuing shortage of appropriate placements — even after a gubernatorial executive order barred overnight office stays.
CYFD's FY2026 budget request included roughly $7 million to fund 101 additional positions tied to Kevin S. commitments; the department reported a net gain of 132 caseworkers by the end of 2025 but acknowledged that's still short of what caseloads require.
Alamogordo Public Schools alone has identified roughly 130 students as homeless — a direct line between housing instability and the child-welfare pressures CYFD is failing to keep up with locally.
Local providers in Otero County (Love INC, COPE, the Counseling Center, 100% Otero) already partner informally with CYFD, juvenile probation, and the DA's victim advocates — infrastructure the state should be funding, not bypassing.
Grace's Policy Commitments I will support full, sustained funding for CYFD caseworker positions and caseload-reduction targets — not one-time appropriations that lapse the next budget cycle.
I will hold CYFD accountable to the Office of the Child Advocate and to the Kevin S. remedial orders, and I will push for rural-county-specific reporting so Otero County families aren't an afterthought to Albuquerque-centered data.
I will champion expanded support for kinship and foster families in Otero County, including faster licensing and reimbursement, so local children can stay close to home, school, and family instead of being placed hours away.
I will support trauma-informed care standards and partnerships between CYFD and trusted local nonprofits already doing this work — Kids Inc., Love INC, the Counseling Center, 100% Otero — rather than reinventing services that already exist here.
I will push for state resources to address student homelessness identified by Alamogordo Public Schools, recognizing that family stability and child welfare are the same fight.
04 Homelessness, Mental Health, COPE & Law Enforcement Grace relied on COPE and the Otero County Sheriff's Office when she needed them most.
She wants every resident in crisis to have that same lifeline.
The Alamo City Dream Center In June 2026, the Alamogordo City Commission voted 6-0 to back the Alamo City Dream Center, a project championed by Mayor Sharon McDonald and conceived by Pastor Anthony Torres and his wife Sasha Torres of Mountain View Church — Grace's own church home for more than a decade.
The Dream Center builds on a decade of the church's hands-on work, including an annual cold-weather emergency shelter, free funeral services for families in need since 2020, and sheltering 329 families (1,523 people, with 625 staying overnight) during the Ruidoso fires.
The facility is designed as permanent, year-round infrastructure rather than a short-term fix: showers, laundry, and storm-shelter space; a soup kitchen serving free community meals Monday through Friday; and room to grow into education, case management, and addiction-recovery support — all addressing homelessness, food insecurity, dignity, and disaster readiness under one roof.
For Grace, this is exactly what compassionate policy should look like: not a one-time program, but a lasting place that meets people where COPE, 100% Otero, and her own life experience have shown her the need is greatest.
Real-World Context The Center of Protective Environment (COPE) is Otero County's core domestic-violence and victim-services provider, working alongside the DA's Office Victim Advocates, CYFD, and Juvenile Probation.
The Alamogordo Police Department's Mobile Crisis Response Team (MCRT) pairs officers with behavioral-health professionals to respond directly to residents in mental health crisis — a co-responder model that keeps people out of jail and into care when appropriate.
100% Otero, a county-wide coalition, has organized "Day of Hope" events (modeled on Veteran Stand Down events) bringing together dozens of partners — COPE, Love INC, the Counseling Center, Income Support, Roadrunner Food Bank, the Otero County Hunger Coalition, and HELP NM — to deliver services directly to people experiencing homelessness or housing insecurity.
The county's Harm Reduction Program (NM Department of Health–Otero) provides syringe services and overdose-prevention supplies, addressing substance use as a public-health issue rather than a purely criminal one.
Alamogordo Public Schools' identification of roughly 130 homeless students underscores that homelessness here isn't confined to a visible street population — it includes working families and children.
Grace's Policy Commitments I will support sustained state funding for co-responder models like the Alamogordo Police Department's Mobile Crisis Response Team, so mental health crises are met with care, not just custody.
I will back continued and expanded funding for COPE and victim-services providers, recognizing that survivors need both emergency shelter and a clear path to long-term stability.
I will support coordinated, "no wrong door" homelessness response — the kind 100% Otero and its partners already model — and push for state grants that fund this kind of cross-agency coordination rather than single-agency silos.
I will support Mountain View Church in its quest to build and open the Dream Center, which will provide resources to the homeless and at-risk communities through shelter, a food kitchen, education, case management, and addiction-recovery resources.
I will champion harm-reduction and behavioral-health investment in rural counties like Otero, where treatment capacity lags far behind need.
I will stand with law enforcement — backing fair pay, training, and resources for the Otero County Sheriff's Office and Alamogordo PD — while also investing in the social-service partnerships that prevent crises before they require a badge.
05 Economic Growth — Small Business, Workforce & Education Otero County's economy lives and dies with the military, tourism, and a handful of anchor employers — and the data shows real strain.
Real-World Context Otero County's unemployment rate has run notably above the statewide average — recent reporting put it at roughly 5.1%, versus about 4.2% statewide — a gap that matters in a district this dependent on one employer base.
The local civilian job market is genuinely limited for military spouses, a recurring theme in base-family guidance for incoming Holloman personnel.
NMSU-Alamogordo, the local two-year community college, is a key workforce pipeline for healthcare, trades, and military-adjacent careers, but rural community colleges statewide face chronic enrollment and funding pressure.
Tourism — White Sands National Park, the New Mexico Museum of Space History, the Lincoln National Forest, and the Sacramento Mountains — is a real but seasonal economic driver that needs supporting infrastructure (roads, water, broadband) to grow.
Small businesses along White Sands Boulevard and in Alamogordo's MainStreet corridor have weathered both the U.S.
54 relief-route shift in customer traffic patterns (built 1998–2001) and, more recently, sharp water/sewer rate increases tied to the City's infrastructure rebuild.
Grace's Policy Commitments I will support targeted state workforce-development funding for NMSU-Alamogordo, aligned to healthcare, skilled trades, and aerospace/defense-adjacent careers tied to Holloman.
I will push for small-business support — grants, low-interest loans, and regulatory relief — specifically for Otero County's MainStreet and tourism-corridor businesses, many of which are still absorbing rate increases tied to the City's water infrastructure overhaul.
I will advocate for military-spouse workforce and licensing-portability programs, so a spouse with an out-of-state professional license isn't forced to start over when stationed at Holloman.
I will support continued investment in tourism infrastructure — roads, signage, water, and broadband — that lets White Sands, the Space History Museum, and the Sacramento Mountains keep drawing visitor dollars into local businesses.
I will treat economic development and infrastructure as one conversation, not two — because in Otero County, a failing water line or an unpaved road is also a small-business problem.
06 Roads, Water & Infrastructure Every other priority in this platform — Holloman's future, small business, child welfare, senior care — depends on roads that hold up and water that runs reliably.
Otero County's infrastructure has been deferred for decades, and the bill has now arrived.
As your state representative, this is where Grace intends to spend real political capital.
The Local Picture Grace is Building On For the last four and a half years — under Mayor Susan Payne and now Mayor Sharon McDonald — the City of Alamogordo has been playing catch-up on the basics: roads, water mains, and sewer lines neglected for nearly two decades before that.
City leadership has made the hard, often unpopular calls (rate increases, bond commitments, a $160 million reinvestment plan) to start closing that gap.
Grace sees this not as a city problem to leave at the City's doorstep, but as a partnership opportunity: Otero County and Alamogordo have done the hard local work of admitting the scope of the problem and starting to fix it — now it's the state's turn to bring real dollars and real expertise to the table alongside them.
Water: The Most Urgent Fight in HD51 Alamogordo is unusual among New Mexico cities in relying mainly on surface water — collected from the La Luz/ Fresnel and Alamo Canyons in the Sacramento Mountains (about 3,969 acre-feet/year in combined rights) and piped roughly 90 miles from Bonito Lake, where the City and Holloman AFB share water rights (about 1,449 acre-feet/year each).
The City lost access to Bonito Lake in 2012 after the Little Bear Fire sent burn-scar debris and flooding into the reservoir — a stark example of how a single wildfire can knock out a city's water supply for years.
After nearly a decade without a rate increase, the Alamogordo City Commission approved roughly $160 million in water and sewer reinvestment, citing decades of deferred maintenance, rising main breaks, and deteriorating storage tanks.
A 6,000-gallon monthly bill rose from about $48 to about $71 — a roughly 46% increase that hit a community already squeezed by inflation and lower wages.
Active and upcoming projects include the New York Avenue underground water/sewer replacement in the City's core business corridor, the Oregon Street waterline and road project (Scenic Drive to 10th Street now, 10th to 1st planned for 2026), and a $12 million Walker Road project addressing water mains that are roughly 70 years old and have been neglected that long.
The City's 40-Year Water Development Plan (2015–2055) centers on three pillars: aggressive conservation (per-capita use already cut from 260 to 165 gallons/day), major pipe and storage infrastructure investment, and brackish-groundwater desalination through the Alamogordo Regional Water Supply Project (ARWSP) — sited across the street from the federal Brackish Groundwater National Desalination Research Facility in Alamogordo.
The desalination plant remains inactive pending completion of a multi-mile transmission pipeline; a $12 million pipeline phase was set to begin construction in 2026, with tens of millions more in brackish infrastructure still needed and unfunded for later phases.
Local officials, including the acting city manager, have acknowledged the original plant siting was a planning misstep made before current leadership — exactly the kind of "decades of neglect" pattern this platform is built to confront, not relitigate.
The New Mexico Municipal League has named water infrastructure investment a top priority for the 2026 legislative session, citing the Rio Rancho water crisis as a warning other cities, including Alamogordo, are racing to avoid.
Roads: A Military Highway and a County That Maintains Its Own U.S.
Highway 54, widened from two to four lanes with a relief-route bypass (built 1998–2001, roughly $23–36 million), functions as a military corridor connecting Fort Bliss in El Paso to Holloman AFB — and the bypass measurably reduced congestion and improved safety on White Sands Boulevard.
Otero County's Road Department (7085 U.S.
Hwy 54/70) maintains county roads independently of NMDOT and the City, a separate funding and maintenance burden in a large, rural county.
NMDOT funds projects through a mix of the state road fund (fuel tax revenue, generally maintenance) and federal funds (generally construction), guided by the State Transportation Improvement Program (STIP) and regional planning bodies — Otero County works through its Regional Transportation Planning Organization (RTPO) for many of these requests.
Federal programs like the Highway Safety Improvement Program (~$22 million/year statewide) and the Transportation Alternatives Program fund safety improvements and bike/pedestrian infrastructure that rural districts like HD51 often under-access compared to metro areas — usually because smaller jurisdictions lack the staff capacity to apply.
The Lever a State Legislator Actually Has: Capital Outlay Reform New Mexico funds most local infrastructure — water, sewer, and roads — through capital outlay, the so-called "Christmas Tree Bill," where each of the 112 legislators and the governor get an individual "member share" to direct to projects in their own district.
Reform advocates (and Governing magazine) have repeatedly called this system one of the most dysfunctional in the country.
As of FY2024, an estimated $5.9 billion in capital outlay funds sat unspent statewide — the result of piecemeal funding, weak eligibility vetting, and projects funded years before localities were ready to spend the money.
In one recent session, local governments requested $3.9 billion in capital outlay; the Legislature had under $600 million available.
Other funding streams matter just as much for HD51: the Water Trust Board, the Colonias Infrastructure Fund (created in 2010 for exactly the kind of underserved southern New Mexico communities Otero County contains — water, wastewater, roads, drainage, and housing), and the NM Finance Authority's revolving loan funds.
The Colonias Infrastructure Board had awarded only about $52 million of roughly $81 million available for its 2025 cycle — meaning real money was sitting on the table for communities that qualify.
Legislators including Rep.
Derrick Lente and LFC analysts have pushed for stricter reauthorization rules and merit-based prioritization (echoing bills like HB 55, which passed 65-1 in the House in 2021 to make capital outlay appropriations transparent).
This reform fight is ongoing and directly shapes whether HD51 actually gets its fair share.
Grace's Policy Commitments I will champion more direct state funding for Alamogordo and Otero County — working hand-in-hand with City leadership, building on the foundation Mayor Payne and Mayor McDonald have already laid, to infuse state and federal dollars into the basics that were neglected for two decades: roads that are actually passable, and water and sewer lines that don't keep breaking under our streets.
I will bring state-level expertise and funding to expand on the City's desalination initiative — which has sat as a "supplemental resource awaiting full potential" for too long — and push to finally complete the well field connections and transmission pipeline that have repeatedly taken a back seat to other projects.
I will treat my capital outlay "member share" as a transparent, public commitment to HD51's highest-need water, road, and drainage projects — not a closed-door list — and I will publish my requests and their status every session.
I will actively pursue Water Trust Board and Colonias Infrastructure Fund dollars for Otero County water and wastewater projects, recognizing that millions in available colonias funding has gone unawarded in recent cycles while our infrastructure keeps aging.
I will support state-level capital outlay reform — merit-based prioritization, reauthorization deadlines, and better coordination with local infrastructure plans — so HD51 isn't competing in a "Christmas Tree" system that has left $5.9 billion sitting unspent statewide while our pipes break.
I will push for continued state support of Alamogordo's 40-Year Water Plan, including funding for the ARWSP desalination pipeline and brackish-water infrastructure, so the City isn't forced to choose between affordability today and water security in 2055.
I will advocate for ongoing, predictable funding — not one-time relief — for projects like Walker Road, Oregon Street, and New York Avenue, and for the County Road Department's rural maintenance needs, so working families don't keep absorbing the cost of 70-year-old infrastructure through sudden rate hikes.
I will pursue Highway Safety Improvement Program and Transportation Alternatives Program funding for HD51's roads and pedestrian/bike infrastructure, helping smaller jurisdictions like ours compete for federal dollars that often default to metro areas with more grant-writing capacity.
I will be a vocal Santa Fe advocate for protecting Holloman's water-rights partnership with the City — recognizing that Bonito Lake, surface-water reliability, and Holloman's mission are inseparable from Alamogordo's own water future.
