The 2020 file was retrieved via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine after being removed from the Comptroller's live rates page.
A 2016 dataset could not be located on the Comptroller's site or in web archives; the ten-year comparison therefore spans 2017-2025 (nine years of verified primary data), and this gap is disclosed rather than filled with an estimate.
For each year, every Texas city's reported taxable value and total tax rate was extracted, and a taxable-value-weighted average rate was calculated for (1) all Texas cities statewide, (2) all cities located in Dallas County, and (3) the nine HD109 cities (Cedar Hill, Lancaster, DeSoto, Glenn Heights, Hutchins, Wilmer, Combine, Ferris, and Seagoville).
Weighting by taxable value (rather than a simple unweighted average) ensures the comparison is not skewed by small cities with disproportionately high or low rates.
Findings HD109 cities' weighted-average city property tax rate fell from $0.7426 per $100 of taxable value in 2017 to $0.6212 per $100 in 2025, a decline of about 16.3% (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2017, 2025) .
Over the same period, the Texas statewide average fell from $0.5665 to $0.5316 (-6.2%), and Dallas County's average fell from $0.6882 to $0.6348 (-7.8%).
Despite HD109's steeper rate cuts, HD109 cities were still taxed 16.8% above the Texas statewide average in 2025, though this is down sharply from a 31.1% gap in 2017.
Compared to the rest of Dallas County, HD109 cities went from a 7.9% rate premium in 2017 to essentially rate parity by 2025 (about 2.1% below the county average), showing that HD109's largest tax-rate reductions — led by Lancaster (-30.9%) and Glenn Heights (-36.4%) between 2017 and 2025 — outpaced the rest of the county.
Individual city rates within HD109 vary widely: Seagoville carried the highest 2025 rate at $0.7207 per $100 , while Combine carried the lowest at $0.3500 per $100 — a difference of more than double.
This means the "average" HD109 rate masks meaningfully different tax burdens depending on which HD109 city a constituent lives in.
Limitations These figures cover city-portion tax rates only — they do not include school district, county, community college, or special district rates, which for most HD109 homeowners make up the majority of a total property tax bill.
A full effective-rate or total-bill comparison would require combining city, county, school district, and special district rates for a representative property, which was outside the scope of this rate-only comparison.
2016 data could not be located from the Comptroller or web archives and is excluded rather than estimated.
DeSoto figures reflect the full city, though only the eastern half of DeSoto falls within HD109; Ferris straddles the Dallas/Ellis county line, and this dataset captures Ferris as reported under the Dallas Central Appraisal District.
Dollar-impact estimates referenced elsewhere (e.g., tax on a $300,000 home) are illustrative calculations applying the city rate only to a round-number taxable value, not actual average tax bills.

For each taxing-unit type, a taxable-value-weighted average total tax rate was calculated statewide and for all units located in Dallas County.
The HD109 composite was built city-by-city: for each of the nine HD109 cities (Cedar Hill, Lancaster, DeSoto, Glenn Heights, Hutchins, Wilmer, Combine, Ferris, and Seagoville), the city's own weighted-average city rate was added to the total rate of its primary school district, the Dallas County rate, and any identified special district (MUD) rate whose name explicitly matches the city.
The nine city-level totals were then combined into a single HD109 composite using each city's own taxable value as the weight, so larger cities like DeSoto and Cedar Hill influence the composite more than smaller cities like Combine.

When the two identified HD109 special districts (Lancaster MUD #1 and Wilmer MUD #1) are added, HD109's all-in composite rises to 2.2337 per $100 .
On an illustrative $300,000 home, that translates to roughly $6,701 per year in HD109 versus $6,108 in Dallas County overall and $6,031 statewide.
School districts are consistently the dominant component: ISD rates make up 58% of HD109's core total, 54% of the statewide core total, and 55% of the Dallas County core total.
Within HD109, DeSoto (2.126 per $100) and Lancaster (2.988 per $100 including its MUD) carry the two heaviest all-in rates, while Combine (1.735) and Wilmer (1.857) carry the lightest.

DeSoto figures reflect the full city; only the eastern half of DeSoto technically falls within HD109.
The special district search was limited to units whose name explicitly references an HD109 city; smaller or unnamed special districts (e.g., generic municipal utility districts, emergency services districts, or road districts) serving these cities may not be captured, so the "total incl. special" figures should be read as a floor rather than a ceiling on the special-district layer.
All dollar-impact figures are illustrative calculations applying the estimated rate to a round $300,000 taxable value, not actual average tax bills for a real property.

03 Sales Tax Stability & Growth Under a Fixed Rate How Texas sales & use tax revenue grew for 33+ years without a single rate increase, 1991–2024 6.25% State Rate, Unchanged Since 1991 +472% Revenue Growth, FY1991–FY2024 $47.24B FY2024 Collections (All Funds) +19.4% FY2022 Growth, Post-COVID Rebound +0.2% FY2020 Change — COVID Year Held Flat, Not Negative Summary of Findings The rate never moved, but revenue kept growing.
Texas set its state sales and use tax rate at 6.25% in 1990, effective fiscal 1991, and the Legislature has not raised it since.
Over the following 33 years, collections rose from $8.26 billion to $47.24 billion — a 472% increase driven entirely by a growing population, rising incomes, and expanding consumer spending, not by any tax increase.
Even COVID-19 did not produce a real decline.
Fiscal 2020, the year the pandemic hit hardest, saw collections essentially hold flat (up 0.2% to $34.04 billion) rather than collapse.
Growth resumed immediately in fiscal 2021 (+5.6%) and accelerated sharply in fiscal 2022 (+19.4%, to $42.99 billion) as the economy reopened and consumer spending surged — the strongest single-year gain of the entire 33-year fixed-rate period.
A fixed-rate consumption tax captures economic growth automatically.
Because the tax is a percentage of what people actually spend, a bigger economy and a bigger population mean bigger dollar collections at the exact same rate — no vote, no rate hike, and no reassessment required.
This is the core mechanical argument for why a broad sales tax can fund government more predictably, and with more built-in room to eventually lower the rate, than a property tax system tied to fluctuating home valuations.

This report tracks what happened to actual dollar collections over that same period, using the Comptroller's own year-by-year net collections data, to show what a fixed-rate consumption tax does as the underlying economy grows.
Methodology All revenue figures come directly from the Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts' Sources of Revenue: A History of State Taxes and Fees, 1972-2024 report (January 2025 edition), specifically its Sales and Use Tax net collections table, which reports "All Funds" collections for every fiscal year from 1972 through 2024 (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
Fiscal years run September 1 through August 31.
Inflation adjustments use the U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), annual averages, rebasing every year's nominal dollar figure into constant fiscal 2024 dollars (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026) .
The revenue growth index in the third tab simply divides each year's nominal revenue by the fiscal 1991 baseline and multiplies by 100, so a reader can see the pace of growth in relative terms independent of dollar scale.
Findings Nominal sales tax collections grew from $8.26 billion in fiscal 1991 to $47.24 billion in fiscal 2024, a 472% increase , without a single change to the 6.25% state rate (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
Even after removing the effect of inflation, real collections still grew from the equivalent of $19.01 billion to $47.24 billion in constant 2024 dollars — meaning a large share of the growth reflects genuine economic and population expansion in Texas, not just rising prices (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025); (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026) .
The COVID-19 pandemic produced only a brief pause, not a collapse.
Fiscal 2020 collections rose a modest 0.2% to $34.04 billion even as the pandemic shut down large parts of the economy for months (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
Growth resumed the following year (+5.6% in fiscal 2021) and then accelerated sharply to +19.4% in fiscal 2022, the single largest annual increase of the entire 33-year fixed-rate period, as reopening, stimulus-driven spending, and inflation combined to push collections to $42.99 billion (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
By fiscal 2024, collections reached $47.24 billion, roughly 5.7 times the fiscal 1991 level in nominal terms.
Two prior downturns are visible in the data and provide useful context: the dot-com/early-2000s slowdown produced a mild dip in fiscal 2002-2003, and the 2008-2009 financial crisis produced a two-year decline (fiscal 2009 down 2.7%, fiscal 2010 down a further 6.6%) before growth resumed in fiscal 2011 (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
Both recoveries confirm the same pattern seen after COVID: a broad consumption tax dips only when actual consumer spending genuinely contracts, and rebounds automatically — without any legislative action — once spending recovers.
Why This Matters for a Sales-Tax-Based System Because a sales tax is levied as a percentage of what people actually spend, a bigger economy and a bigger population mechanically produce bigger dollar collections at the exact same rate.
No vote, no reassessment, and no rate increase were needed to grow Texas sales tax revenue nearly six-fold since 1991 — population growth, income growth, and business formation did that automatically (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
This is the central mechanical argument for why a jurisdiction relying on a broad sales tax, rather than a property tax tied to home valuations, can experience rising revenue purely from organic economic growth, and — if a taxing entity ever collects more than it needs to fund its services — has room to lower its rate over time rather than needing to raise it.
Limitations The 6.25% state rate has been fixed since 1991, but the tax base (what specifically is taxed) has seen minor legislative adjustments over the same period — including a temporary fireworks tax that was later repealed, changes to the Texas Emissions Reduction Plan (TERP) surcharge on off-road diesel equipment, a 2019 requirement that online marketplace providers collect the tax, and targeted exemptions added in 2023 for items such as maternity and baby hygiene products (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .
These base changes mean a small share of long-run revenue growth reflects adjustments to what is taxed, not solely economic and population growth, though the rate itself never changed.
Local sales taxes (city, county, transit, and special-district add-ons, capped at a combined 2 percentage points on top of the state rate) are excluded from this analysis, which covers only the state's 6.25% portion (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2025) .

The earliest year for which reliable, state-specific Texas data could be located was 1969 (income, from the 1970 Census) and 1970 (home value, from the 1970 Census of Housing); a complete continuous series back to 1966 itself does not exist in Census records, so 1966 is used only as the price-deflation base year via the Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025) .
In real, inflation-adjusted terms, Texas median household income grew modestly and unevenly — even declining during some periods — while Texas median home values grew far faster and more consistently, especially after 2010.
The result is that median home value went from roughly 1.4 times median household income in 1970 to roughly 3.7 times median household income in 2024, meaning the affordability gap between what a typical Texas household earns and what a typical Texas home costs has more than doubled in real terms over this period.
Data Sources & Methodology Income series.
Decennial Census benchmark values for Texas median household/family income were assembled directly from U.S.
Census Bureau decennial releases: $8,490 for 1969 (American Statistical Association Proceedings, 1972) , $16,469 for 1979 (United Press International, 1982) , $27,016 for 1989 and $39,927 for 1999 (Demographia, 2001) .
From 1984 forward, the annual Current Population Survey (CPS) Annual Social and Economic Supplement series for Texas — as compiled by the Federal Reserve Bank of St.
Louis (FRED) from Census Bureau data — provides year-by-year figures through 2024 (Federal Reserve Bank of St.
Louis, 2025) .
Home value series.
Decennial Census "Historical Census of Housing Tables" provide unadjusted (nominal) Texas median home values for 1970 ($12,000), 1980 ($39,100), 1990 ($59,600), and 2000 ($82,500) ("Median Home Values: Unadjusted," n.d.) .
The 2010 figure ($128,900) comes from American Community Survey estimates as compiled by the Texas Demographic Center (Texas Demographic Center, 2015) , and the 2020 figure ($187,200) and 2024 figure ($302,999) come from the Zillow Home Value Index for Texas as republished via FRED, cross-validated against Zillow's own current reporting (Zillow, 2026) .
Because decennial Census and ACS benchmarks for home value are only available roughly once per decade, annual-resolution movement between benchmark years was estimated using the Federal Housing Finance Agency's (FHFA) All-Transactions House Price Index for Texas, available annually from 1975 forward (Federal Housing Finance Agency, 2026) .
The FHFA index was scaled piecewise by the author so that it exactly matches the known Census/ACS dollar benchmarks at each decade point, with the index used only to approximate the shape of the path between those fixed points.
This is a calculation performed by the author, not a figure drawn directly from any single source, and is disclosed here per the requirement to identify inferential or calculated data distinctly from directly reported source data.
All nominal dollar figures were converted to constant 1966 dollars using annual average CPI-U index values from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025) , using the formula: real value = nominal value × (CPI-U in 1966 ÷ CPI-U in the observed year).
Findings: Decade Benchmarks Texas real median household income was essentially flat for the first 30 years of this period: it was actually lower in real terms in 2000 ($7,512 in 1966 dollars) than a household earning the 1970 median might expect after even modest real growth, and real income dipped notably in the 1980s before recovering.
By contrast, real median home value grew in every decade except the 1980s, when Texas experienced an oil-bust housing downturn that temporarily depressed home values even in nominal terms.
The home-value-to-income ratio rose from 1.41 in 1970 to 2.37 in 1980, eased slightly to 2.07 by 2000, then climbed steadily to 2.56 in 2010, 2.74 in 2020, and 3.72 by 2024 — the sharpest single jump of the entire series occurring in the four years between 2020 and 2024.
Findings: Annual Trend, 1984–2024 Using the annual FRED income series and the FHFA-interpolated home value series, real median household income in Texas grew from roughly $7,179 (1966 dollars) in 1984 to about $8,417 in 2024 — a real increase of only around 17 percent over 40 years.
Over the same period, real median home value in Texas rose from roughly $21,082 to $31,296 (1966 dollars) — a real increase of roughly 48 percent, with nearly all of that increase occurring after 2012 and especially after the COVID-19 pandemic-era housing boom of 2020–2022.
When both series are indexed to 1984 = 100, the real household income index stood at roughly 117 by 2024 (a 17 percent real gain), while the real home value index stood at roughly 148 (a 48 percent real gain) — visually confirming that Texas home prices have outpaced Texas household earning power in real terms, particularly after the 2008 financial crisis recovery and the 2020–2022 pandemic-era housing market surge.
Findings: The Affordability Ratio The home-value-to-income ratio — how many years of gross household income it would take to purchase the median home outright — fell from approximately 2.56 in 2010 to 2.74 in 2020, then jumped sharply to roughly 3.72 by 2024, an increase of nearly one full year of household income needed to purchase a median home in just four years.
This acceleration coincides with historically low mortgage rates and pandemic-driven housing demand of 2020–2022, combined with continued population growth into Texas that outpaced new housing supply, as reflected in the FHFA index's largest recorded single-year Texas appreciation rates occurring in 2021 and 2022 (Federal Housing Finance Agency, 2026) .
A ratio below roughly 3.0 is generally considered affordable by historical housing-finance conventions; ratios above 4.0 to 5.0 are typically associated with severe affordability strain, meaning Texas's 2024 ratio of 3.72 sits meaningfully above the traditional affordability threshold.
Limitations No single continuous, annually reported, state-specific Texas dataset exists covering median household income and median home value back to 1966; the decennial Census only measured state-level median income and home value once every ten years prior to the American Community Survey's launch in the 2000s.
The 1969, 1979, 1989, and 1999 income figures and the 1970, 1980, 1990, and 2000 home value figures are therefore official Census-year snapshots rather than a continuous annual series.
The annual home value figures for years between decennial Census/ACS benchmarks were calculated by the author by scaling the FHFA All-Transactions House Price Index for Texas to match the known dollar benchmarks at each decade point; these interpolated values should be understood as a reasonable estimate of the trend rather than an official reported statistic for any single year other than the anchor years themselves.
This is a deductive calculation using an official, methodologically sound index (FHFA) anchored to official Census/ACS dollar figures.

Limitations Both measures are estimates from CPS ASEC and are subject to sampling and nonsampling error.
A lower national poverty rate does not show that every geographic area or demographic group improved equally, and it does not mean families just above the threshold face no hardship.

Those limits are reasons to examine both OPM and SPM—not reasons to reverse the official historical series.

Period statistics calculations by author. ↓ Download CSV Read the Full Report ▾ What This Shows This report places official U.S. gasoline price data alongside the dates each president and each congressional majority held office.
It answers a narrow factual question — what did gasoline cost, and who held office at the time — and it does not attempt to measure how much any president or Congress caused prices to rise or fall.
Retail fuel prices also move with global crude oil markets, refinery capacity, taxes, seasonal fuel blends, wars, natural disasters, recessions, and demand shocks.
Data and Methodology Recent weekly series.
The January 2024 – September 2026 zoom uses the weekly U.S.
Regular All Formulations Retail Gasoline Price, reported in dollars per gallon including taxes, from the release of September 29, 2026, through the week of September 28, 2026 (U.S.
Energy Information Administration [EIA], 2026c) .
Weeks dated January 13, 2025 and earlier are grouped as Biden administration weeks; weeks dated January 20, 2025 and later are grouped as Trump administration weeks, matching the January 20, 2025 inauguration date (U.S.
House of Representatives, Office of the Historian, n.d.) .
Period means, medians, minimums, maximums, and first-to-last-week changes were calculated by the author from those 144 weekly observations.
Long-run monthly series.
Monthly gasoline prices are the BLS U.S. city average price for unleaded regular gasoline (series APU000074714), available from January 1978 through August 2026 (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026a) .
Monthly on-highway diesel prices, available from January 1995, and domestic crude oil first-purchase prices, available from January 1974, come from the EIA Monthly Energy Review (EIA, 2026b) ; (EIA, 2026a) .
Inflation-adjusted values convert each month to August 2026 dollars with the formula: nominal price × (August 2026 CPI-U ÷ CPI-U in the observation month), using the BLS Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (BLS, 2026b) .
Missing months were left blank and were not interpolated.
Political periods.
Presidential term dates come from the House Office of the Historian (U.S.
House of Representatives, Office of the Historian, n.d.) , and House and Senate party control by Congress comes from the U.S.
Senate party-division record and the same House source (U.S.
Senate, n.d.) ; (U.S.
House of Representatives, Office of the Historian, n.d.) .
In the monthly term groupings, the month of an inauguration is assigned to the incoming term (for example, January 2025 is grouped with the 2025–2026 term).
Congressional-control groups pool every month with the same combination of House and Senate majorities, even when those months are decades apart.
Findings: January 2024 – September 2026 Across the 55 weeks from January 1, 2024 through January 13, 2025, the national regular gasoline price averaged $3.294 per gallon, ranging from $3.668 (week of April 22, 2024) down to $3.006 (week of December 30, 2024).
The final week of that period, $3.043, was 1.5% below the first week of January 2024 ($3.089) (EIA, 2026c) .
Across the 89 weeks from January 20, 2025 through September 28, 2026, the price averaged $3.416 , with a low of $2.779 (week of January 12, 2026) and a high of $4.500 (week of May 11, 2026).
The September 28, 2026 price of $4.465 was 43.6% higher than the $3.109 recorded the week of January 20, 2025 (EIA, 2026c) .
Because the two periods cover different lengths of time and different seasons, their averages are descriptive comparisons, not measures of policy performance.
Findings: Long-Run History, 1978–2026 Measured in the dollars of the day, the highest annual average gasoline price in the series was $4.192 per gallon in 2022 , and the highest single month was June 2022 at $5.058 (BLS, 2026a) .
After adjusting for inflation to August 2026 dollars, the picture changes: the highest annual average was 2012 at $5.391 , the highest single month was July 2008 at $6.229, and the lowest annual average was 1998 at $2.291 (BLS, 2026a) ; (BLS, 2026b) .
By presidential term, the highest nominal monthly average belongs to the 2021–2025 Biden term ($3.563), followed by the 2025–2026 Trump term to date ($3.518) and the Obama terms ($2.974).
In August 2026 dollars, the ranking differs: the Obama terms averaged $4.349, the Carter months from 1978 averaged $4.219, and the Biden term averaged $4.038, while the Clinton terms had the lowest real average at $2.533 (BLS, 2026a) ; (BLS, 2026b) ; (U.S.
House of Representatives, Office of the Historian, n.d.) .
By decade, the 2000s showed the greatest relative volatility (coefficient of variation 0.327), while the 1990s were the most stable (0.075) (BLS, 2026a) .
Grouped by congressional control, months under divided control averaged $2.273 nominal and $4.022 in August 2026 dollars; Democratic unified control averaged $1.749 nominal and $3.555 real; and Republican unified control averaged $1.986 nominal and $3.124 real (BLS, 2026a) ; (BLS, 2026b) ; (U.S.
Senate, n.d.) .
These pooled groups mix different decades, presidents, and world events, so they describe timing only.
Limitations The weekly EIA series and the monthly BLS series are both official gasoline price measures, but they are different survey products, so a weekly EIA value should not be compared directly with a monthly BLS value for the same period (EIA, 2026c) ; (BLS, 2026a) .
Monthly gasoline data begin in January 1978, so terms and decades before that date have no gasoline observations in this analysis; the 1970s decade covers only 1978–1979, the Carter term covers only 1978–1980, and the 2020s and the 2025–2026 term are incomplete.
Annual averages for 2026 are excluded because the year is not complete.
None of the statistics here control for crude oil prices, inflation expectations, global events, or other factors, and none should be read as evidence that a particular officeholder caused a price change.

The Question and the Data Debates about Texas schools often assume that districts earn better grades when they spend more per student, pay teachers more, or reach a certain size.
Those assumptions can be tested.
The Texas Education Agency publishes an A–F grade for nearly every school system, along with each system’s revenue, attendance, student characteristics, and teacher pay.
This report puts those records side by side for the 2023–24 school year.
Five official TEA files were used.
They are the 2024 accountability ratings (TEA, n.d.-d) ; actual FY2024 revenue and spending reported by each system (TEA, n.d.-g) ; 2023–24 student and staff data from the Texas Academic Performance Reports (TAPR) (TEA, n.d.-c) ; refined attendance counts from the final 2023–24 Summary of Finances (TEA, n.d.-b) ; and TEA’s classification of each system by type (TEA, n.d.-e) .
All 1,246 school systems that appear in any of these files were matched by their six-digit TEA district number.
Of those, 1,187 had an A–F grade and complete funding, attendance, and teacher-pay data.
That group includes 1,016 traditional school districts and 171 open-enrollment charter systems.
The two groups are analyzed separately because they are funded and governed differently.
Definitions Used in This Report Overall score is the 0–100 score that determines a district’s letter grade (TEA, 2024) .
Operating revenue is the state, local, and federal money a system received to run its schools in FY2024, as reported to TEA (TEA, n.d.-g) .
Refined ADA (average daily attendance) is the attendance count TEA uses to calculate school funding (TEA, n.d.-f) .
This report divides revenue by refined ADA to measure “funding per student in attendance,” and uses refined ADA to measure district size.
Average teacher base salary is the district’s average base pay for teachers, as reported in TAPR (TEA, n.d.-c) .
To see whether the 2023–24 patterns are new or long-standing, the same measures were assembled for every school year from 2014–15 through 2023–24.
The sources were TEA’s district profile files for each year (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) , district revenue reported for FY2015 through FY2024 (TEA, n.d.-g) , and TEA’s statewide summary of district and campus ratings and scores (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) .
That produced 10,214 district-year records for traditional districts.
Dollar amounts from earlier years are converted to FY2024 dollars using the federal Consumer Price Index (BLS, 2026) , so a dollar in 2014–15 is compared with what it would buy in 2023–24.
Reading the 10-Year Comparisons The grading system changed several times during these ten years, and every over-time comparison has to account for that.
Districts were rated only Met Standard or Improvement Required from 2014–15 through 2016–17.
Letter grades began in 2017–18.
No ratings were issued for 2019–20 or 2020–21 because of the pandemic.
In 2021–22, districts that would have received a D or F were labeled Not Rated instead.
TEA then revised the scoring rules beginning in 2022–23 (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, 2024) .
Because scores before and after that change are not directly comparable, the over-time analysis uses each district’s percentile rank, its standing relative to other traditional districts in the same year, rather than its raw score.
Historical funding is measured per enrolled student, because fall enrollment is reported the same way in every year.
The results describe how these measures line up across districts in one school year.
They do not prove that any one factor causes a grade.
Section 7 compares districts with similar students, and also follows the same districts over time to see whether changes in funding, pay, or enrollment moved with changes in their standing.
2.
How Districts Were Graded Under the 2024 accountability system, a district’s overall score combines three areas: Student Achievement, School Progress, and Closing the Gaps.
The score is then converted to a letter grade (TEA, 2024) .
Among the 1,016 traditional districts in this analysis, 106 earned an A, 390 a B, 354 a C, 151 a D, and 15 an F.
Among the 171 charter systems, 26 earned an A, 53 a B, 38 a C, 30 a D, and 24 an F (TEA, n.d.-d) .
The median traditional district scored 79.
Grades Over Ten Years District grades have shifted sharply over the decade, partly because of changes in the rules themselves.
From 2014–15 through 2016–17, 96% to 97% of traditional districts were rated Met Standard.
In 2017–18, the first year of letter grades, 61% of districts that received a letter earned an A or B; TEA labeled another 194 traditional districts Met Standard that year instead of assigning a letter.
The share of A or B grades rose to 86% in 2018–19 and 91% in 2021–22, when D and F labels were not issued.
It then fell to 52% in 2022–23 under the revised rules and 49% in 2023–24.
The median overall score moved from 82 (2017–18) to 87 (2018–19), 87 (2021–22), 80 (2022–23), and 79 (2023–24) (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) .
Of the 1,011 traditional districts with a letter grade in both 2018–19 and 2023–24, 347 kept the same letter, 599 received a lower letter, and 65 received a higher one (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) .
Because the rules changed between those years, a lower letter does not by itself mean a district’s students learned less.
This is why the over-time sections compare districts by percentile rank.
3.
Per-Student Funding and Grades The median traditional district received $16,752 in operating revenue per student in attendance, or $15,170 per enrolled student (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) .
Across all 1,016 traditional districts, funding per student in attendance has almost no relationship to the overall score.
The correlation is 0.07, on a scale where 0 means no relationship and 1 means a perfect one.
Measuring per enrolled student instead gives 0.09 (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) .
Letter grades account for only about 2.0% of the differences in funding between districts.
Median funding was $18,098 for A districts, $16,514 for B, $16,393 for C, $17,432 for D, and $19,604 for the 15 F districts (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) .
The highest- and lowest-graded districts both had above-median funding.
That is a sign that something other than funding is driving the pattern.
For charter systems the pattern runs the other way: higher funding per student goes with lower scores (correlation −0.27).
One likely reason is that charters serving more economically disadvantaged students receive more formula aid and also tend to score lower (correlation with poverty −0.29) (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Grouping traditional districts into five equal-sized funding groups shows the same thing.
Of the lowest-funded group, 56% earned an A or B, compared with 39% of the second group and 49% of the highest-funded group (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) .
The lowest-funded group is mostly larger districts, with a median of 5,204 students in attendance.
The highest-funded group is mostly very small districts, with a median of 200.
State formulas send extra money to small districts and to districts with more students in poverty.
Funding levels therefore reflect who a district serves, which is why Section 7 compares districts with similar students.
Funding Over Ten Years After inflation, median operating revenue per enrolled student in traditional districts rose from $13,602 in 2014–15 to a high of $15,962 in 2021–22, then eased to $15,180 in 2023–24.
That is a real increase of 11.6% over the decade; in dollars of the day, the 2014–15 figure was $10,356 (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Part of the 2020–21 through 2022–23 rise was temporary federal pandemic aid.
Median federal revenue per enrolled student went from $1,466 in 2018–19 to $2,870 in 2021–22 and back to $1,825 in 2023–24, all in FY2024 dollars (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Through all of those changes, funding per student never lined up with grades across districts.
In each of the five letter-grade years, the correlation between funding per enrolled student and the overall score stayed between −0.04 to 0.09 (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) .
The over-time picture adds something the single year could not show, however.
When the same districts are followed from 2018–19 to 2023–24, those whose real funding per student grew the most (median +27%) changed their median percentile rank by +1.3 points.
Those whose funding fell the most (median −10%) changed theirs by −5.1 points (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Holding changes in teacher pay, enrollment, and student poverty constant, along with each district’s 2018–19 standing, a 10% increase in real funding per student from 2018–19 to 2023–24 went with about 1.5 percentile points of improvement in rank (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
The relationship is real but modest, and it is consistent with the single-year finding in Section 7.
4.
Teacher Pay and Grades The median traditional district paid an average teacher base salary of $56,547 (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Across districts, average teacher pay shows almost no relationship to the overall score: the correlation is 0.05 (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Median salary was $56,071 in A districts, $56,742 in B, $56,410 in C, $56,867 in D, and $52,558 in F districts.
Statistically, the salary differences across letter grades are not distinguishable from chance (p = 0.12).
Sorted into five pay groups, the lowest-paying fifth of districts (median $50,534) earned an A or B 50% of the time.
The highest-paying fifth (median $64,808) did so 51% of the time (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
The highest-paying districts are mostly large: their median size is 6,194 students in attendance, compared with 382 for the lowest-paying group.
They also receive less operating revenue per student, $14,632 compared with $19,315 (TEA, n.d.-c) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) .
Large districts can pay teachers more because their fixed costs are spread over more students.
Small districts receive more per student but spread it across fewer teachers at lower average salaries.
Teacher pay also varies with local labor markets.
Districts in and around large cities pay more than rural districts in the same part of the state.
When districts are compared only with others of the same TEA district type and in the same education service center region, a pay relationship appears (Section 7) (TEA, n.d.-e) .
Among charter systems, higher teacher pay goes with modestly higher scores (correlation 0.14), although that result falls just short of conventional statistical significance (p = 0.06) (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Teacher Pay Over Ten Years Over the decade, the median district’s average teacher base salary rose 25.9% in dollars of the day, from $44,927 in 2014–15 to $56,560 in 2023–24.
After inflation, however, it fell 4.1%, from $59,009 to $56,560 in FY2024 dollars.
Real pay peaked at $62,611 in 2019–20, then lost ground as prices rose faster than salaries (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Teacher pay’s link to grades across districts has weakened.
The correlation between average teacher salary and the overall score was 0.17 in 2017–18 and 0.17 in 2018–19, compared with 0.01 in 2022–23 and 0.05 in 2023–24 (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Over time, districts whose real average salary rose the most from 2018–19 to 2023–24 (median +6%) changed their median percentile rank by +0.8 points.
Those with the largest real declines (median −11%) changed theirs by −2.7 points.
The rank-based correlation is 0.10.
When salary change and funding change are considered together, the salary change adds no clear relationship of its own (p = 0.38) (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) .
Districts that raised pay tended to be the same districts whose funding grew.
Section 7 tests teacher pay within the same districts across all five letter-grade years.
5.
Enrollment and District Size Texas districts range from a few dozen students to more than 160,000 in attendance.
The median traditional district has about 915 (TEA, n.d.-b) .
Size has essentially no straight-line relationship with the overall score (correlation 0.01).
A rank-based measure, which is less influenced by the very largest districts, shows a slight tendency for larger districts to score lower (−0.11) (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) .
The size groups are not evenly matched on grades.
The smallest three-fifths of districts earned an A or B about half the time or more.
Mid-sized districts, with 1,348 to 3,966 students in attendance, had the lowest share of A and B grades (36%) and the highest share of D and F grades (21%).
The largest districts, with 3,969 or more, had 47% rated A or B (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) .
Size also shapes the other two measures in opposite directions.
The smallest districts had a median of $22,669 per student in attendance and $53,326 in average teacher pay.
The largest had $13,497 and $63,142 (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Among traditional districts, the median A district had 403 students in attendance and the median D district had 1,385.
The median F district had only 374 (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) .
Both the best and the worst grades are common among small districts.
That makes size a poor predictor of a district’s grade on its own.
Enrollment Over Ten Years Traditional districts enrolled 4.99 million students in fall 2014 and 5.09 million in fall 2023, an increase of 2.1%.
Enrollment peaked at 5.14 million in 2019–20 and dropped to 4.99 million during the pandemic year.
The median district stayed close to 1,000 students throughout, 977 in 2014–15 and 997 in 2023–24 (TEA, n.d.-g) .
District size never lined up with grades in any letter-grade year: correlations ranged from −0.03 to 0.08 (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) .
Growth did not line up with improvement either.
From 2018–19 to 2023–24, the fastest-growing fifth of districts (median +18%) changed their median percentile rank by −3.7 points, and the fastest-shrinking fifth (median −14%) by −2.8.
The rank-based correlation across all districts was −0.02 (p = 0.47), meaning no detectable relationship (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) .
6.
Student Need: The Factor That Lines Up With Grades Funding, teacher pay, and size each show little relationship with grades.
The share of a district’s students who are economically disadvantaged shows a strong one: a correlation of −0.49.
The share of students TEA identifies as at risk of dropping out shows a similar relationship (−0.44) (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
About 24% of the variation in districts’ poverty rates lines up with their letter grades, more than ten times the share for funding.
In the fifth of traditional districts with the fewest economically disadvantaged students, 83% earned an A or B and 2% earned a D or F.
In the fifth with the most, 28% earned an A or B and 38% earned a D or F.
Median scores fell from 86 to 74.
This happened even though the highest-poverty districts received more funding per student in attendance, a median of $17,765 compared with $14,703 (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) .
Student Need Over Ten Years The median traditional district’s share of economically disadvantaged students rose from 57.2% in 2014–15 to 61.9% in 2023–24 (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Its link to grades held steady.
In each of the five letter-grade years, poverty had the strongest correlation with the overall score of any factor examined, ranging from −0.50 to −0.38.
Funding, teacher pay, and size stayed near zero every year (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) .
Changes in poverty within a district, by contrast, did not track changes in its standing.
From 2018–19 to 2023–24, the rank-based correlation between a district’s change in economically disadvantaged share and its change in percentile rank was −0.03 (p = 0.37) (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Poverty separates districts from one another, but these modest year-to-year shifts within a district were not large enough to move its relative standing.
7.
Comparing Similar Districts Raw comparisons can hide relationships, because the state’s formulas give more money to districts with greater student need.
Those same districts tend to score lower.
A fairer question is this: among districts with similar students, do the ones with more funding, higher pay, or more students score differently?
Regression analysis answers that by estimating each factor’s relationship with the score while holding the others constant.
The models include each district’s shares of economically disadvantaged, emergent bilingual, and special education students, plus its size, average teacher pay, TEA district type, and education service center region.
Once student need is held constant, funding shows a positive but small relationship.
Among comparable traditional districts, 10% more operating revenue per student in attendance goes with about 0.34 points higher on the 100-point overall score (95% confidence range: 0.07 to 0.61 points).
Funding alone explains essentially none of the variation in scores (R² = 0.000).
Adding student need raises that to 0.27, which shows that need, not funding, does most of the explaining (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Teacher pay shows a relationship only when districts are compared with others of the same type and region.
On that basis, each additional $1,000 in average base salary goes with about 0.16 points higher (95% range: 0.05 to 0.26).
Without the type and region comparison, the estimate is 0.05 points and is not statistically significant (p = 0.30) (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) ; (TEA, n.d.-e) .
District size shows no relationship once the other factors are included: a district twice as large differs by about 0.06 points (p = 0.84).
By comparison, 10 more percentage points of economically disadvantaged students goes with about 2.7 points lower.
Key Finding Among districts serving similar students, the funding and teacher-pay relationships are real but small.
Raising funding per student by 10% is associated with roughly a third of a point on a 100-point scale.
A $1,000 raise in average teacher pay is associated with less than a fifth of a point.
When larger districts are given more weight in the model, the funding estimate is no longer statistically clear (p = 0.11), while the teacher-pay estimate remains (0.23 points, p = 0.016) (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-b) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Comparable Districts Over Ten Years The same comparison was repeated for each letter-grade year, using funding per enrolled student and fall enrollment, the measures available in every year.
In every year, more funding per student went with a modestly higher score among comparable districts.
A 10% increase was associated with between 0.27 and 0.54 points, and every estimate was statistically significant (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
Teacher pay was not statistically significant in any single year under this version of the model; its 2023–24 estimate is 0.06 points per $1,000 (p = 0.24).
That means the single-year teacher-pay result above depends on how size and funding are measured.
A stronger test follows each district against itself.
Using all five letter-grade years, the analysis asked whether a district’s standing rose in years when its own funding, pay, or enrollment changed, setting aside differences between districts that do not change over time.
On that basis, a 10% increase in a district’s real funding per student went with about 1.2 percentile points higher standing (p = 0.001).
Each additional $1,000 in real average teacher salary went with about 0.39 points (p = 0.014).
Ten percent enrollment growth went with about 1.0 point lower standing (p = 0.02).
Changes in poverty share were not significant within districts (p = 0.46) (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) ; (TEA, n.d.-g) ; (TEA, n.d.-h) ; (TEA, n.d.-c) .
What the 10-Year View Adds Across districts, money and pay barely line up with grades, because the districts that receive the most money often serve the most students in poverty.
Within the same district over time, increases in real funding and teacher pay go with small improvements in standing: about one percentile point for a 10% funding increase, and less than half a point for $1,000 in pay.
These are associations, measured over years when the grading rules changed.
They do not prove that the added dollars caused the improvement.
8.
Grades Inside Districts A district’s letter grade is a single summary of many campuses.
Among the 306 traditional districts with five or more letter-graded campuses in 2023–24, the typical gap between the highest- and lowest-scoring campus was 24 points.
In 38% of those districts (115 in all), at least one campus was rated A while another was rated D or F (TEA, n.d.-d) .
Campuses that share the same school board, budget, salary schedule, and district grade can produce very different results.
District-level averages for funding and pay cannot explain that difference.
Why This Matters for Parents A family choosing a school is choosing a campus, not a district average.
TEA publishes campus-level grades and scores for every rated campus.
Those results show more about a child’s school than the district’s overall letter grade or its spending per student (TEA, n.d.-d) .
Campus Gaps Over Time Wide campus gaps are not new.
Campuses first received letter grades in 2018–19.
That year, 31% of traditional districts with five or more letter-graded campuses had both an A campus and a D or F campus, with a typical score gap of 23 points.
The share was 38% in 2022–23 and 38% in 2023–24 (TEA, n.d.-a) ; (TEA, n.d.-d) .
The 2021–22 year is omitted because D and F labels were not issued.

What the Evidence Supports Across 1,187 Texas school systems in 2023–24, per-student funding, average teacher pay, and district size each had little relationship with a district’s A–F grade on their own.
Districts with the most money per student were not the highest rated.
Districts paying the highest average salaries were rated A or B about as often as those paying the least.
Districts of every size earn both high and low grades.
The measure that lines up most strongly with grades is how many of a district’s students come from economically disadvantaged families.
The 10-year record shows these are long-standing patterns, not features of a single year.
In every letter-grade year since 2017–18, student poverty lined up with grades far more than funding, pay, or size did.
Over the same decade, real funding per enrolled student in the median district rose 11.6%, while real average teacher pay fell 4.1% and enrollment grew 2.1%.
Following the same districts over time, rising real funding and pay went with small gains in standing, and enrollment growth with a slight decline.
Once similar districts are compared, more funding and higher teacher pay are associated with modestly better scores.
The size of those relationships matters for policy.
If these relationships held, across-the-board increases in funding or salaries would be expected to move district scores only slightly.
The large gaps between campuses within the same district suggest that results also depend on what happens at the campus and district level: how money is spent, how teachers are deployed, and how campuses are led.
These records cannot measure those factors directly.
That is consistent with policies that put decisions and accountability closer to the campus, and that give parents clear campus-level information.
It also argues against assuming that more money or higher pay by themselves will raise grades.
The evidence does not show which governance changes would work best, and it does not show that local control by itself would improve results.
Those questions require evidence across multiple years.
What This Analysis Cannot Establish The central comparison covers a single year (2023–24).
The 10-year extension spans changes to the grading rules, two unrated pandemic years, and a year without D and F labels, so it relies on percentile ranks rather than raw scores.
All results measure associations, not causes.
It uses district averages, which conceal differences between campuses and between teachers.
Revenue is total operating revenue, not money spent in classrooms; instruction spending is reported separately in the data tables and shows the same pattern.
Average teacher base salary reflects both pay scales and the experience of each district’s teachers.
Refined ADA runs about 91% of fall enrollment statewide, so amounts per student in attendance are higher than amounts per enrolled student.
The two should not be compared directly.
About This Analysis All figures are calculated from the original Texas Education Agency files listed in the References, without alteration to source values.
The central analysis covers the 2023–24 school year: 2024 accountability ratings, FY2024 actual finances, and 2023–24 attendance, student, and staff data.
The 10-year extension covers school years 2014–15 through 2023–24, with dollar amounts adjusted to FY2024 dollars.
Results describe associations between districts and do not by themselves establish cause.

General Fund operating revenue for Texas school districts and charter schools rose every year, from $34.35 billion in FY2009 to $60.08 billion in FY2025.
Per student, it rose from $7,808 to $11,934 before inflation (+52.8%), but only from $11,646 to $11,934 in FY2025 dollars (+2.5%).
Real per-student funding fell in 8 of those 16 years and was 8.4% below its FY2020 peak in FY2025 (TEA, 2025a) ; (TEA, 2025b) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Much of the largest state spending growth replaced local property taxes.
The state’s share of operating revenue fell from 49.4% in FY2009 to 40.9% in FY2023 as rising property values shifted the cost to local taxpayers.
House Bill 1 (2006), House Bill 3 (2019) and Senate Bill 2 (2023) each used state money to cut school tax rates, and the state share was back to 49.6% in FY2025 (TEA, 2025a) ; (LBB, 2008) ; (LBB, 2023) .
One session, 2011, cut formula funding by law.
The 2012–13 budget cut per-student entitlements by an average of 5.6% a year, and real operating spending per student fell 8.6% from FY2011 to FY2013.
The next Legislature reversed course with $4.5 billion more in General Revenue.
Texas Education Agency appropriations rose in every other two-year budget, from $33.60 billion in 2006–07 to $89.97 billion in 2026–27 (LBB, 2012) ; (LBB, 2014) ; (LBB, 2026) ; (TEA, 2025a) .
“Defunded” does not fit the record, and neither does “record investment.” Average teacher pay rose 31.8% in dollars from 2011–12 to 2024–25 but fell 5.8% after inflation, and the basic allotment lost 19.3% of its buying power from FY2020 to FY2025 before House Bill 2 (2025) raised it.
Statewide enrollment fell by 76,613 students in 2025–26, only the second decline since 1987–88 (TEA, 2026b) ; (BLS, 2026) ; (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2019) ; (TEA, 2025d) ; (TEA, 2026a) .

Funding per Student: More Dollars, Same Buying Power A common claim in Texas politics is that Republican-led Legislatures have “defunded” public schools.
The opposite claim, that funding has soared, is just as common.
Both sides usually cite numbers, and the numbers usually measure different things: total dollars or dollars per student, before or after inflation, money for daily operations or money that also includes bond debt and construction.
This report tests both claims against the state’s own records, using actual results that every Texas school district and charter school reports to the Texas Education Agency (TEA) (TEA, 2025a) .
Every law discussed here was passed by a Legislature with Republican majorities in both the House and the Senate.
Legislative Reference Library member records show those majorities in every session from 2006 through 2025 (Legislative Reference Library of Texas [LRL], n.d.) .
Funding and spending In this report, funding means General Fund operating revenue: local property taxes for maintenance and operations (M&O), state aid, federal aid and other local income received for day-to-day operations.
Spending means operating spending from all funds: payroll, contracted services, supplies and other operating costs.
Neither includes bond debt or construction.
Per student and real dollars Per-student figures divide by average daily attendance (ADA), the basis of most state formulas.
Statewide ADA grew from 4,399,018 in FY2009 to 5,034,410 in FY2025 (TEA, 2025b) .
Real dollars are adjusted with the U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index, averaged over the Texas fiscal year (September through August), and stated in FY2025 dollars (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
Did funding go down?
The Five Funding Tests tab above applies five tests to the statewide figures for every year from FY2009 to FY2025.
Before inflation, operating revenue per student grew from $7,808 in FY2009 to $11,934 in FY2025, an increase of +52.8%.
After inflation, the same measure went from $11,646 to $11,934 in FY2025 dollars, a change of +2.5% over 16 years (TEA, 2025a) ; (TEA, 2025b) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Real per-student funding fell in 8 of those years: 2010, 2011, 2012, 2017, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2025.
The path was not flat.
Real per-student funding fell about 7% from FY2009 to a low in FY2012, after the 2011 budget cuts.
It then climbed to a peak in FY2020, after House Bill 3 (2019).
From FY2020 to FY2025 it fell 8.4%, because the base per-student amount stayed fixed while prices rose (TEA, 2025a) ; (BLS, 2026) .
Operating spending followed the same pattern: $13,742 per student in FY2009 and $14,224 in FY2025, both in FY2025 dollars (+3.5%), with a low of $12,336 in FY2013.
One number causes more confusion than any other: the $93.26 billion TEA reports as total district revenue for FY2025.
It is often called school “spending.” In fact, it is a revenue total that also counts property taxes for bond debt, bond-related financing and the state’s direct payment to the Teacher Retirement System.
Money for daily operations was $70.03 billion, and operating spending was $71.61 billion (TEA, 2025a) .
2.
Who Pays: State Aid and Local Property Taxes The per-student total combines state aid and local property taxes, and the split between them shifted.
In FY2025 dollars, state General Fund aid per student fell from $5,750 in FY2009 to $4,911 in FY2023, a 14.6% decline.
Over the same years, local M&O taxes per student rose from $5,402 to a peak of $6,447 in FY2020 (TEA, 2025a) ; (TEA, 2025b) ; (BLS, 2026) .
As property values rose, local taxes covered more of the cost and the state covered less, so the state’s share fell from 49.4% to 40.9%.
The trend reversed in FY2024.
That year, Senate Bill 2 (88th Legislature, Second Called Session, 2023) cut school tax rates by another 10.7 cents and raised the school homestead exemption from $40,000 to $100,000.
The state replaced the lost local revenue, at an estimated two-year General Revenue cost of $12.7 billion (Legislative Budget Board [LBB], 2023) .
By FY2025, state aid per student was $5,918, local M&O taxes were $5,242, and the state share was back to 49.6% (TEA, 2025a) .
The per-student total did not rise.
What changed was who pays: state money took the place of local property taxes.
The Same Trade Has Happened Three Times In 2006, House Bill 1 cut school M&O tax rates to 88.67% of the 2005 rate in FY2007 and 66.67% in FY2008, with state aid replacing the lost revenue (LBB, 2006b) .
The LBB reported that $12.0 billion of the $12.7 billion increase in Texas Education Agency appropriations for 2008–09 paid for that property tax relief (LBB, 2008) .
House Bill 3 (2019) and Senate Bill 2 (2023) did the same.
Each time, a large share of new state money went to replacing local taxes rather than adding to the per-student total.

What Republican Legislatures Passed The Laws and Votes tab above lists the major school finance laws of the period.
For each, it shows the Legislative Budget Board’s estimate of the two-year effect on General Revenue at passage and the final vote in each chamber by party.
Vote totals match the certification printed in each enrolled bill.
Party counts come from the House and Senate Journals and Legislative Reference Library member records (Texas House of Representatives, 2006–2025) ; (Texas Senate, 2006–2025) ; (LRL, n.d.) .
The fiscal notes put large price tags on the major school finance bills: $3.92 billion over two years for House Bill 1 (2006), $11.60 billion for House Bill 3 (2019), $12.7 billion for Senate Bill 2 (2023) and $8.38 billion for House Bill 2 (2025) (LBB, 2006b) ; (LBB, 2019a) ; (LBB, 2023) ; (LBB, 2025) .
House Bill 21 (2017) created the Texas Commission on Public School Finance, whose work led to House Bill 3 (H.B.
21, 2017) .
House Bill 2 (2025) added a Teacher Retention Allotment, a Support Staff Retention Allotment of $45 per student, and a new Allotment for Basic Costs of $106 per enrolled student for costs such as transportation, insurance and utilities (LBB, 2025) .
Most of these laws passed with large bipartisan majorities.
The 2011 cuts were the main exception: no House Democrat voted for the final version of Senate Bill 1, and 12 House Republicans voted against it.
The 2011 budget and House Bill 21 (2017) also drew few Democratic votes.
The 2013 budget, which restored much of the 2011 reduction, drew most of its opposition from Republicans: 28 of the 29 House members voting no (Texas House of Representatives, 2006–2025) .
Total appropriations to the Texas Education Agency, as reported by the LBB, rose in every two-year budget from 2006–07 to 2026–27 except 2012–13.
The 2026–27 budget appropriates $89.97 billion, up from $33.60 billion in 2006–07 (LBB, 2006a) ; (LBB, 2012) ; (LBB, 2026) .
Reading the Basic Allotment Correctly The basic allotment is the base state formula amount per student, before adjustments for student needs and district size.
Its jump from $3,218 in FY2009 to $4,765 in FY2010 was not a 48% raise.
House Bill 3646 (2009) redefined the allotment so it applied at districts’ lower, compressed tax rates; the dollar figure changed because the definition changed (H.B.
3646, 2009) ; (LBB, 2009) .
Under a single definition, the basic allotment lost ground to inflation between increases.
It was $5,140 from FY2016 through FY2019, and $6,160 from FY2020 through FY2025.
From FY2020 to FY2025, its buying power fell 19.3% (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, 2019) ; (LBB, 2020) ; (BLS, 2026) .
House Bill 2 (2025) raised it to $6,215 for 2025–26 (TEA, 2025d) .
4.
The 2011 Cut in the Official Record The 2011 session is the clearest case of a school funding reduction written into law.
The Legislative Budget Board estimated the General Revenue shortfall for 2012–13 at $27 billion.
Facing it, the Legislature passed a budget (House Bill 1) and a school finance bill (Senate Bill 1, First Called Session) that cut formula funding for the 2012–13 biennium (LBB, 2012) .
The official record shows three things about this cut.
First, General Revenue spending on the agency did not fall.
Most of the all-funds decline came from the end of one-time federal stimulus money that had replaced state funds in 2010–11 (LBB, 2010) ; (LBB, 2012) .
Second, the formula reduction was measured against what current law would have required, not against the prior budget.
The LBB described it as cutting per-student entitlements by an average of 5.6% a year and reducing expected General Revenue growth by $4.0 billion (LBB, 2012) .
Third, districts felt it: operating spending per student fell 8.6% in real terms from FY2011 to FY2013 (TEA, 2025a) .
The next Legislature reversed course.
The 2014–15 budget raised Texas Education Agency appropriations by $3.7 billion (7.5%) over 2012–13 spending, including $4.5 billion more in General Revenue, and Senate Bill 758 restored the deferred payment (LBB, 2014) .
By FY2016, real operating revenue per student ($11,897) was above its FY2009 level ($11,646) (TEA, 2025a) .
Key Finding The 2011 session is the only one in this period whose school finance law cut per-student formula funding.
It was not the only period when real per-student funding fell.
That also happened in FY2017, in each year from FY2021 through FY2023, and in FY2025, with no formula cut, because the base allotment stayed fixed while prices rose.
5.
Teacher Pay and Retirement Average teacher pay rose every year in dollar terms but lost ground to inflation for most of the period.
TEA’s statewide reports show average teacher base pay rising from $48,375 in 2011–12 to $63,749 in 2024–25, a 31.8% increase before inflation.
After inflation, average pay fell 5.8% over those years.
In 2025–26, the first year of House Bill 2’s Teacher Retention Allotment, average base pay reached $68,001 (TEA, 2026b) ; (BLS, 2026) .
State law sets a pay floor, not salaries.
Under Education Code §21.402, “a school district must pay” each classroom teacher, full-time librarian, certified counselor and full-time nurse at least the state minimum for that employee’s experience (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 21.402, 2025) .
TEA puts it plainly: “In no instance may a school district pay less than the state base salary” (TEA, n.d.-a) .
The floor does not reach every school.
The list of Education Code provisions that bind open-enrollment charter schools does not include §21.402, so charter schools are not required to follow the state salary schedule (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 12.104, 2025) .
A traditional district designated a District of Innovation may also exempt itself from the schedule.
Neither the statute nor the Commissioner’s rule lists §21.402 among the provisions such a district may not waive (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 12A.004, 2025) ; (Tex.
Educ.
Agency, 19 Tex.
Admin.
Code § 102.1309, 2026) .
The same rule bars those districts from exempting themselves from Education Code Chapter 48, where House Bill 2 placed the Teacher Retention Allotment and its raise requirement (H.B.
2, 2025) .
Above the floor, the Legislature has at times told districts how to use new money.
House Bill 3 (2019) required districts to spend at least 30% of new per-student funding on raises for non-administrative staff, with three-quarters of that going to teachers, librarians, counselors and nurses (H.B.
3, 2019) .
House Bill 2 (2025) funds a Teacher Retention Allotment of $2,500 or $5,000 per eligible teacher in districts with more than 5,000 students, and $4,000 or $8,000 in smaller districts, depending on experience.
Districts must pass the allotment on as raises (H.B.
2, 2025) .
For teacher retirement, the state’s contribution was cut in 2011, with the combined state and district rate falling from 6.64% to 6.00% of payroll in FY2012 (TRS, 2025) .
Senate Bill 12 (2019) later raised the state rate from 6.8% to 8.25% by FY2024, the member rate from 7.7% to 8.25%, and the district rate to 2.0% by FY2025 (S.B.
12, 2019) .
6.
Enrollment, Attendance and School Closures Statewide enrollment fell by 76,613 students (1.4%) in 2025–26, to 5,467,642.
In TEA’s series going back to 1987–88, the only other decline was in 2020–21, the pandemic year (TEA, 2026a) .
Attendance has been slowing for longer: statewide ADA rose 14.4% from FY2009 to FY2025, but has been nearly flat since FY2018 (TEA, 2025b) .
Several large districts have voted to close campuses.
The School Closures tab above covers five of the largest that approved closures from 2023 to 2026, using each district’s own documents.
It is a sample, not a statewide count.
All five districts cited underused buildings or empty seats, and four cited declining enrollment.
From 2019 to 2025, fall enrollment in these districts fell by 9.5% to 16.8% (TEA, 2025a) .
Most closure decisions are made by locally elected school boards, and two cases here also involve the state.
Austin ISD’s closures were approved alongside 24 turnaround plans required under state accountability law (Austin ISD, 2025) .
Houston ISD’s closures were approved by a Board of Managers appointed by the Commissioner of Education, who suspended the elected board’s powers on June 1, 2023.
In 2025 the Commissioner extended the appointed board until June 1, 2027 (TEA, 2023) ; (TEA, 2025e) .
7.
What the Record Shows The records do not support the claim that Republican-led Legislatures defunded Texas public schools.
Operating revenue for schools rose every year from FY2009 to FY2025, from $34.35 billion to $60.08 billion.
Texas Education Agency appropriations rose in every two-year budget except 2012–13.
Measured per student and adjusted for inflation, FY2025 funding was slightly above FY2009 (+2.5%) (TEA, 2025a) ; (TEA, 2025b) ; (LBB, 2006a) ; (LBB, 2026) ; (BLS, 2026) .
The records also do not support the claim that school funding soared.
Real per-student funding fell in 8 of 16 years and is 8.4% below its FY2020 peak.
The 2011 cut was real and measurable in district spending.
Average teacher pay lost 5.8% to inflation from 2011–12 to 2024–25.
And much of the largest state spending growth went to replacing local property taxes rather than adding to the per-student total (TEA, 2025a) ; (TEA, 2026b) ; (LBB, 2008) ; (LBB, 2012) ; (LBB, 2023) .
Bottom Line Across 16 years, the state’s records show steady growth in dollars, roughly flat buying power per student, a state share that fell and was then restored, and one session, 2011, that cut formula funding by law.
“Defunded” does not describe that record.
Neither does “record investment” once inflation and enrollment growth are counted.
These records show what districts received and spent.
They do not, by themselves, show why funding changed or whether any amount was adequate.
Several limits apply: Inflation adjustment uses a national consumer price index.
School costs, especially salaries and insurance, may rise at different rates.
Per-student figures use average daily attendance, which dropped in FY2020 during pandemic closures and affects that year’s ratios.
Federal pandemic aid raised all-funds federal revenue in FY2021–FY2024.
The headline funding measure uses the General Fund to limit that effect.

It dropped from 23.4% in 2008 to 16.7% in 2024, while the U.S. rate fell from 14.6% to 8.2%.
The gap in percentage points was about the same at the end as at the start (8.8 vs.
8.5), while the ratio grew from 1.60× to 2.04× because the national rate fell to a smaller base (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Far more Texans have coverage than in 2008.
Texas’ population grew 29.2%, more than twice the national rate, and the number of insured Texans rose by about 7.4 million (+40.4%), while the uninsured count changed by only −7.4%.
Employer coverage held steady in Texas as it fell nationally, and Medicaid covered about the same share of Texans in 2024 as in 2008.
Texas has not adopted the optional ACA adult Medicaid expansion (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) (Texas Health and Human Services Commission [HHSC], 2024b) .
Coverage costs much more, and cost is the most common reason people give for going without it.
The average employer family premium roughly doubled (+99.5% nationally, +101.4% in Texas), faster than median household income (+65.3% U.S., +74.8% Texas) and general prices (+45.7%).
Hospital-service prices rose 104.0%.
In 2024, 61.9% of uninsured U.S. adults ages 18–64 named affordability as a reason (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025b) (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) (Cohen & Giri, 2026) .
Public health spending grew at every level, faster than prices, population, or income.
Federal health spending rose 180.6%, national health spending 119.9%, and Texas state health and human services spending 128.1%, from $29.7 billion in FY2008 to $67.7 billion in FY2024 and $73.4 billion in FY2025.
These data show measures moving together in time, not that one caused another, and whether a given uninsured rate is good or bad for a state is a judgment left to the reader (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) (Comptroller, 2012) .

Executive Summary Texas’ uninsured rate is often cited by itself: 16.7% of Texans had no health insurance in 2024, the highest share of any state and about twice the national rate of 8.2% (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Those figures are accurate.
But a rate is one number divided by another, and it doesn’t show what happened to the price of insurance, the price of care, the number of people who gained coverage, or the public money spent on health care over the same period.
This report puts those measures next to each other for 2008 through 2024 using only original government data.
The Short Version Coverage.
Texas’ uninsured rate fell from 23.4% to 16.7% (−6.7 points), while the U.S. rate fell from 14.6% to 8.2% (−6.4 points).
Over the same years Texas added 29.2% more residents, compared with 12.3% nationally.
The number of Texans with coverage rose by about 7.4 million (+40.4%), even as the number without coverage changed by only −7.4% (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Cost.
The average employer family premium roughly doubled, rising 99.5% nationally and 101.4% in Texas.
Median household income rose 65.3% nationally and 74.8% in Texas, and general consumer prices rose 45.7% (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025b) (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
Hospital-service prices rose 104.0% (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
Why people say they are uninsured.
In 2024, 61.9% of uninsured U.S. adults ages 18–64 said cost was a reason they had no coverage (Cohen & Giri, 2026) .
Spending.
Federal health spending rose 180.6%, total national health spending rose 119.9%, and Texas state health and human services spending rose 128.1%, from $29.7 billion in FY2008 to $67.7 billion in FY2024 and $73.4 billion in FY2025 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) (Comptroller, 2012) .
Taken together, the data show a Texas uninsured rate that fell substantially but remains well above the national rate.
That decline took place while the cost of buying coverage rose much faster than prices in general and faster than household income, and while public health spending at every level grew faster than both.
Texas covers a smaller share of its residents through Medicaid than the nation does, and it has not adopted the optional Medicaid expansion for adults (Texas Health and Human Services Commission [HHSC], 2024b) (KFF, 2026) .
Employer coverage in Texas held steady while it declined nationally (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
What This Report Does and Does Not Do This report reports what the official data show and some basic statistical facts about those data.
It does not claim that any law, program, or party caused any change shown here.
Where events such as the start of ACA coverage in 2014 are marked on a chart, the marker shows timing only.
Whether a given uninsured rate is “good” or “bad” for a state depends on what the reader values, including cost, choice, taxes, and public spending, and the report leaves that judgment to the reader.

Data and Methods Every figure in this report comes from an original government data file.
Each was downloaded, opened, and checked value by value in October 2026.
No news reports, advocacy summaries, or secondary compilations were used as data.
The full annual tables are in the tab stacks above, and every original file is linked in the References below.
Key Definitions and Choices Uninsured rate.
The share of the civilian noninstitutionalized population with no health coverage at the time of the survey.
The Census Bureau publishes a margin of error of ±0.2 percentage points for the Texas rate and ±0.1 points for the U.S. rate in every year shown (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
No 2020 data point.
Because of pandemic disruptions to data collection, the Census Bureau did not release standard 2020 one-year ACS estimates.
The charts show a gap for 2020 rather than filling it in (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Inflation adjustment.
“Constant 2024 dollars” means a value is multiplied by the ratio of the 2024 CPI-U annual average (313.69) to that year’s average.
Prices rose 45.7% from 2008 to 2024 (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
Texas fiscal years.
Texas state spending is reported for fiscal years ending August 31.
When these figures are adjusted with calendar-year CPI, the result is a close approximation, not an exact match.
Survey estimates.
Premium, income, and coverage figures are survey estimates with sampling error.
Small year-to-year changes, especially in state-level employer survey figures, may not be statistically meaningful.
3.
The Uninsured Rate From 2008 to 2024 the Texas uninsured rate fell from 23.4% to 16.7%, a drop of 6.7 percentage points, or 28.6% of the starting rate.
The national rate fell 6.4 points, from 14.6% to 8.2%, a relative drop of 43.8% (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Both rates fell most sharply between 2013 and 2016, the years in which the Affordable Care Act’s marketplace coverage and optional Medicaid expansion took effect on January 1, 2014 (Finegold & Gunja, 2014) .
Texas reached its lowest rate in the series, 16.4%, in 2023.
Two Ways to Measure the Gap How “far behind” Texas looks depends on how the gap is measured.
In percentage points , the gap between Texas and the nation was about the same at the end of the period as at the start: 8.8 points in 2008 and 8.5 points in 2024.
As a ratio , the gap grew from 1.60× to 2.04×, because the national rate fell to a much smaller base (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Both numbers are correct.
They answer different questions, and the ratio rises whenever the U.S. rate shrinks faster in relative terms, even if the absolute distance doesn’t change.
A Rate Is a Fraction: Population Growth Matters Texas grew much faster than the nation.
Its civilian noninstitutionalized population rose from 23.8 million to 30.8 million (+29.2%), compared with +12.3% for the U.S.
Over the same period the number of Texans with coverage rose from 18.3 million to 25.6 million (+40.4%).
The number without coverage went from 5.56 million to 5.15 million (−7.4%) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Nationally, the uninsured count fell 36.8%.
Texas made up 9.2% of the U.S. population in 2024 and 18.7% of its uninsured residents, up from 12.8% of the uninsured in 2008.
In 2024 Texas had the highest uninsured rate of any state.
The next highest were Georgia (12.0%), Oklahoma (11.5%), and Nevada (11.4%) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
4.
How Texans Are Covered Where people get their insurance helps explain the rate.
Between 2008 and 2024, the share of Texans with employer-based coverage was essentially flat (51.4% to 51.5%), while the national share fell from 58.7% to 54.6%.
Direct-purchase coverage, which includes marketplace plans, rose in Texas from 11.2% to 13.4% (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
The biggest difference is Medicaid.
The share of Texans covered by Medicaid was 14.8% in 2008 and 14.9% in 2024.
Nationally it rose from 15.1% to 20.5% (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Texas has not extended Medicaid eligibility to the optional adult group created by the Affordable Care Act (Texas Health and Human Services Commission [HHSC], 2024b) .
It is one of 10 states that have not adopted that expansion (KFF, 2026) .
5.
Why People Go Uninsured Federal survey data address why people go without coverage.
The CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics asks uninsured adults why they lack insurance.
In 2024, 20.9% of uninsured adults ages 18–64 named affordability as their only reason, and another 41.0% named affordability along with other reasons.
Together, 61.9% cited cost.
In 2019 the combined share was 73.9% (Cohen & Giri, 2026) .
Reading This Correctly These are national survey responses, not a Texas-specific estimate, and they describe what uninsured adults say, not a measured cause.
They are consistent with the cost data in the next two sections: the price of coverage is the most commonly reported barrier.
6.
The Price of Coverage Most Americans with private insurance get it through an employer.
The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality surveys private-sector employers every year and publishes results for every state.
In 2024 the average total premium for family coverage was $24,540 nationally and $24,106 in Texas, up from $12,298 and $11,967 in 2008.
That is an increase of 99.5% nationally and 101.4% in Texas (AHRQ, 2021) (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) .
Single-coverage premiums rose 93.5% nationally and 86.3% in Texas.
After adjusting for inflation, the 2008 U.S. family premium equals about $17,918 in 2024 dollars.
The actual 2024 premium was 37.0% higher than that in real terms.
For Texas the real increase was 38.3% (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
Premiums Compared with Household Income One way to see the size of this cost is to compare the average family premium with median household income.
This is a benchmark, not a measure of what any family pays, since employers pay most of the premium.
In 2008 the U.S. family premium equaled 24.4% of median household income, and in 2024 it equaled 29.5%.
In Texas the share rose from 25.7% to 29.7% (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025b) .
Most of the increase happened between 2008 and 2011.
Since then, premiums and median incomes have grown at roughly similar rates.
What Workers Pay Directly The worker’s own share of a family premium rose from $3,394 to $7,216 nationally (+112.6%).
In Texas it rose from $3,872 to $6,851 (+76.9%).
The average single-coverage deductible rose from $869 to $2,085 nationally (+139.9%) and from $1,058 to $2,273 in Texas (+114.8%) (AHRQ, 2021) (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) .
Texas deductibles were higher than the national average in both years.
The share of private-sector employers that offer health insurance also changed.
Nationally it fell from 56.4% in 2008 to 49.0% in 2024.
Texas was lower than the nation in 2008 (48.8%) and was reported at 54.1% in 2024, after 46.4% in 2023 (AHRQ, 2021) (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) .
The one-year jump in the Texas estimate is large enough that it should be confirmed in future releases before anyone relies on it.
7.
Medical Prices Insurance premiums pay for medical care, so the prices of that care are part of the picture.
From 2008 to 2024 the overall Consumer Price Index rose 45.7%.
Prices for medical care as a whole rose 54.9%, and medical care services rose 59.1%.
Hospital services rose fastest, by 104.0%, which means hospital prices roughly doubled.
Prescription drug prices rose 47.2%, close to general inflation.
Physicians’ services rose 34.2%, less than general inflation (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
8.
National Health Spending Total U.S. health spending, which includes public and private payers, rose from $2,401 billion in 2008 to $5,279 billion in 2024 (+119.9%).
Spending per person rose from $7,907 to $15,474 (+95.7%, or +34.3% after inflation).
Health care’s share of the economy rose from 16.3% of GDP to 18.0%, peaking at 19.7% in 2020, when pandemic spending rose and GDP fell (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) .
9.
Federal Health Spending Federal health spending grew faster than any other measure in this report.
Counting Medicare, the federal share of Medicaid, marketplace subsidies, coverage for federal employees, and other programs, it rose from $588.8 billion in 2008 to $1,652.0 billion in 2024 (+180.6%, or +92.6% after inflation).
The federal government’s share of all U.S. health spending rose from 24.5% to 31.3%.
Per U.S. resident, federal health spending rose from about $1,939 to $4,843 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) .
By program, federal Medicaid spending rose 193.7% (from $203.2 billion to $596.7 billion) and net federal Medicare spending rose 161.6% (from $194.8 billion to $509.6 billion).
Marketplace premium subsidies, which did not exist before 2014, reached $116.6 billion in 2024 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) .
The spike in “all other” spending in 2020–2021 reflects pandemic-era federal programs.
10.
Texas State Health Spending The Texas Comptroller reports state spending by function in Table 7 of each Annual Cash Report, covering all funds except trust funds, so federal money that passes through the state is included.
Health and human services spending was $29.7 billion in FY2008 and $67.7 billion in FY2024, an increase of 128.1% (Comptroller, 2012) (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) .
After inflation, the increase was about 56.6%.
In FY2025 the category reached $73.4 billion, 40.3% of all state net expenditures and the largest single function of Texas government (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) .
Health and human services grew from 36.2% of state spending in FY2008 to a peak of 46.1% in FY2022, during the period of pandemic-era continuous Medicaid enrollment, then fell to 38.5% in FY2024.
That year, HHS spending fell 7.8% while education spending rose 21.3% and transportation spending rose 31.7% (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) .
Per Texas resident, it rose from about $1,246 to $2,199 (+76.5%).
Where the Money Comes From A large part of Texas health and human services spending is federal money.
The Health and Human Services Commission reports that federal funds made up 55% of the HHS system’s $49.5 billion in all-funds spending in state fiscal year 2024 ($27.4 billion federal).
Texas’ standard federal Medicaid matching rate for federal fiscal year 2024 was 60.15% (HHSC, 2024a) .
The HHSC report covers a narrower set of agencies and excludes some items, so its total differs from the Comptroller’s function-level figure.
11.
Everything Together Placing every measure on the same 2008-to-2024 timeline shows how they relate.
Public health spending grew fastest: federal spending rose 180.6% and Texas state HHS spending 128.1%.
Total national health spending and hospital prices came next, followed by employer premiums at roughly double their 2008 level.
Household incomes grew more slowly, general prices more slowly still, and Texas’ population more slowly than that.
The number of uninsured Texans was about the same at the end as at the start, while the number of insured Texans rose 40.4% (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025b) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .

Conclusions The record from 2008 to 2024 supports these factual conclusions: Texas’ uninsured rate fell, and it remains the highest of any state.
It dropped from 23.4% to 16.7%.
The point gap with the nation was about the same at the end as at the start (8.8 vs.
8.5 points), while the ratio grew (1.60× to 2.04×) because the national rate fell to a smaller base (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
Many more Texans have coverage.
Texas’ population grew 29.2%, more than twice the national rate.
The insured population rose about 7.4 million (40.4%), while the uninsured count changed by −7.4% (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) .
How Texans are covered differs from the nation.
Employer coverage held steady in Texas while it declined nationally.
Medicaid covered about the same share of Texans in 2024 as in 2008, while the national share rose.
Texas has not adopted the optional adult Medicaid expansion (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025a) (Texas Health and Human Services Commission [HHSC], 2024b) .
Coverage costs much more than it used to.
The family premium roughly doubled in both Texas and the U.S.
It grew more than twice as fast as general prices and faster than median household income.
Worker contributions and deductibles also rose, and Texas deductibles stayed above the national average (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [AHRQ], 2025) (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) (U.S.
Census Bureau, 2025b) .
Medical prices rose faster than prices in general.
Hospital-service prices rose 104.0%, more than twice the general inflation rate (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026) .
Cost is the most common reason people give.
In 2024, 61.9% of uninsured U.S. adults ages 18–64 named affordability as a reason they lacked coverage (Cohen & Giri, 2026) .
Public spending grew at every level.
Federal health spending rose 180.6%, national health spending 119.9%, and Texas state HHS spending 128.1%.
All three grew faster than inflation, population, and household income (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2025) (Comptroller, 2012) (Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts [Comptroller], 2025) .
Some Basic Statistical Facts About These Numbers A falling rate and a steady count can both be true when the population is growing fast.
Texas’ rate fell while its uninsured count barely changed.
A ratio between two shrinking rates can grow even when the point gap holds steady.
“Twice the national rate” and “about 8.5 points higher” describe the same 2024 data.
Year-to-year changes of a few tenths of a point in the uninsured rate are near the ±0.2-point margin of error for Texas (±0.1 for the U.S.).
Single-year swings in state employer-survey estimates should be read with caution.
Nominal growth over 16 years overstates real growth.
Every dollar figure here should be compared with the 45.7% rise in general prices.
These data show that measures moved together in time, not that one caused another.
The Full Picture Texas’ uninsured rate describes one part of a national health-care system in which the cost of insurance, the price of care, and public spending all grew faster than incomes and prices over the same years.
Whether a high uninsured rate is a failing, a trade-off, or a sign of other priorities is a judgment readers will make for themselves.
The data here are meant to make that judgment an informed one.
