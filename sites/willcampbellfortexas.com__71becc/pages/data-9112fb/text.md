HD109 cities carry a heavier city-tax burden than the statewide average every year from 2017-2025.
The gap has narrowed from 31.1% above the state average in 2017 to 16.8% above in 2025, driven mainly by rate cuts in Lancaster, Glenn Heights, and DeSoto [Texas Comptroller, 2017-2025].
HD109's rate advantage over Dallas County as a whole flipped in 2023.
HD109 cities were taxed 7.9% above the Dallas County city average in 2017, but by 2023-2025 HD109 cities' average rate fell slightly below the county-wide average, reflecting faster local rate reductions relative to the rest of the county.
Seagoville, DeSoto, and Hutchins carry the highest city tax rates in HD109 as of 2025 (0.721, 0.685, and 0.657 per $100, respectively), while Combine and Wilmer carry the lowest (0.350 and 0.468).

The rate never moved, but revenue kept growing.
Texas set its state sales and use tax rate at 6.25% in 1990, effective fiscal 1991, and the Legislature has not raised it since.
Over the following 33 years, collections rose from $8.26 billion to $47.24 billion — a 472% increase driven entirely by a growing population, rising incomes, and expanding consumer spending, not by any tax increase.
Even COVID-19 did not produce a real decline.
Fiscal 2020, the year the pandemic hit hardest, saw collections essentially hold flat (up 0.2% to $34.04 billion) rather than collapse.
Growth resumed immediately in fiscal 2021 (+5.6%) and accelerated sharply in fiscal 2022 (+19.4%, to $42.99 billion) as the economy reopened and consumer spending surged — the strongest single-year gain of the entire 33-year fixed-rate period.
A fixed-rate consumption tax captures economic growth automatically.
Because the tax is a percentage of what people actually spend, a bigger economy and a bigger population mean bigger dollar collections at the exact same rate — no vote, no rate hike, and no reassessment required.
This is the core mechanical argument for why a broad sales tax can fund government more predictably, and with more built-in room to eventually lower the rate, than a property tax system tied to fluctuating home valuations.

Texas home values have grown almost three times faster than household income in real, inflation-adjusted terms since 1984.
Real median household income rose about 17% from 1984 to 2024, while real median home value rose about 48% over the same period (Federal Reserve Bank of St.
Louis, 2025; Federal Housing Finance Agency, 2026).
The home-value-to-income ratio has nearly tripled since 1970.
A median Texas home cost about 1.4 times median household income in 1970; by 2024 it cost about 3.7 times median household income, with the sharpest jump occurring between 2020 and 2024.
Real household income was essentially flat for three decades.
Adjusted for inflation, Texas median household income in 2000 was barely above its 1970 level, and dipped below it during the 1980s oil-bust years, before modest real gains resumed after 2010.

The federal government spent more on healthcare than on national defense over 2000–2024: $21.087 trillion in CMS federal sponsor spending versus $15.004 trillion in OMB national-defense outlays, a cumulative ratio of 1.41 to 1.
The series use different accounting periods—calendar years for CMS and federal fiscal years for OMB—so the comparison establishes scale rather than a reconciled accounting identity (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2024); (Office of Management and Budget [OMB], 2026).
Selected federal healthcare-related net outlays totaled $1.599 trillion from January through August 2026.
Each observed monthly total was 43.3 to 94.7 times the $3 billion high-intensity Iran-conflict benchmark.
The benchmark is a scenario—not an assertion that exactly $3 billion was spent in every month (Congressional Budget Office [CBO], 2026).
More federal healthcare spending did not translate into lower household medical prices across the period.
Employer premiums, worker contributions, deductibles, out-of-pocket spending, and major medical price indexes generally rose, while health outcomes moved in different directions.
Those patterns are descriptive and do not, by themselves, establish that federal spending caused the changes.
