Executive Summary
Automated license plate readers, networked cameras, facial-recognition systems, cell-site simulators, and other surveillance technologies can help police locate stolen vehicles, identify wanted suspects, find missing people, and respond more quickly to dangerous situations.
They can process information at a speed and scale that no individual officer could match.
But that same scale creates a serious risk.
A camera that helps find one stolen car may also record thousands of innocent drivers.
A system designed to generate a narrowly targeted alert can become a searchable history of where people traveled, worshiped, sought medical care, attended political meetings, met with attorneys, or associated with others.
A database intended for legitimate investigations can also be searched by an employee for personal reasons, exposed through a security failure, shared with distant agencies, or repurposed for a use that the public never approved.
These are not merely theoretical concerns.
In Kansas, the state commission responsible for police-certification standards found that a police chief used Flock Safety’s license-plate system to search for a former girlfriend’s vehicle 164 times and her boyfriend’s vehicle 64 times.
He entered purported law-enforcement reasons such as “drug investigation” and “missing child,” but ultimately admitted that jealousy influenced his conduct.
The commission revoked his law-enforcement certification (Kansas Commission on Peace Officers’ Standards and Training, 2024).
In Frisco, Texas, an officer mistakenly entered an Arkansas license plate as an Arizona plate.
The incorrect return caused officers to believe that a family’s vehicle might be stolen and led to a high-risk traffic stop.
A supervising sergeant identified the error and ended the stop, and the department publicly acknowledged the mistake and initiated a review (Frisco Police Department, 2023).
That Frisco incident was caused by erroneous human entry rather than an automated Flock match, but it demonstrates the danger of treating a database result as conclusive.
A single incorrect state code was enough to transform an innocent family’s trip into a high-risk stolen-vehicle response.
The proper lesson is not that officers should stop using vehicle information; it is that every automated or database-generated result must be verified before coercive action is taken.
Facial recognition presents the same problem at a different level.
The National Institute of Standards and Technology found substantial variation among facial-recognition algorithms and documented demographic differences in false-positive rates across many systems.
NIST also found that better-performing algorithms can reduce those differences, which means that accuracy depends heavily on the system, operating conditions, comparison process, and human review—not merely on whether a product is labeled “artificial intelligence” (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2020).
The proposed Texas Public Surveillance Technology Privacy, Security, and Accountability Act responds to these risks without banning useful technology.
It establishes a lawful-use framework built around several principles:
- Government surveillance must serve a specific and documented public-safety purpose.
- Real-time alerts may continue for stolen vehicles, valid arrest warrants, missing or endangered people, crimes in progress, emergencies, and active pursuits.
- Historical searches must be narrow, documented, connected to an identified investigation, and supported by reasonable suspicion.
- Broad or repeated movement tracking requires a warrant.
- Facial recognition, biometric identification, and cell-site simulators generally require judicial authorization.
- An automated alert or algorithmic match is an investigative lead—not proof of identity, probable cause, or guilt.
- Searches must be assigned to individual users, connected to a case or authorized purpose, and preserved in tamper-evident audit records.
- Innocent people’s data generally must be deleted within 30 days.
- Government surveillance data may not be sold, commercially exploited, used for social scoring, or used to monitor lawful political, religious, journalistic, or associational activity.
- Surveillance near houses of worship, polling places, medical facilities, schools, shelters, news organizations, and legal-service locations receives heightened protection.
- Government bodies must publicly disclose the technologies they use, the companies involved, the data collected, retention periods, sharing arrangements, costs, security requirements, and material misuse findings.
- Vendors do not acquire ownership or commercialization rights in data collected under a government contract.
- Surveillance data must be protected with encryption, multifactor authentication, unique credentials, access controls, continuous logging, security testing, and incident reporting.
- Officers and first responders acting in good-faith reliance on valid policies, warrants, court orders, or supervisory directions receive express protection.
- Individuals, agencies, vendors, and contractors that knowingly misuse the systems face meaningful discipline, civil liability, contract consequences, and—in serious cases— criminal penalties.
The bill’s central distinction is simple: detecting a known stolen vehicle in real time is not the same as reconstructing an innocent person’s movements for weeks or months.
Searching for an abducted child is not the same as searching for a former romantic partner.
Using a warrant to locate a known violent suspect is not the same as scanning every face in a crowd.
The law should recognize those differences.
Available evidence supports this balanced approach.
A randomized experiment conducted with the Mesa Police Department found that license-plate readers allowed officers to check eight to ten times as many plates, produced nearly three times as many stolen-vehicle hits, and resulted in approximately twice as many vehicle recoveries as manual checking and ordinary patrol.
However, the study did not establish that the ALPR deployment itself produced a broader reduction in auto theft during the tested period (Office of Justice Programs, 2011).
A later analysis of two randomized experiments similarly found that license-plate readers increased the likelihood of detections, arrests, and recoveries, but the outcomes were generally rare and only the increase in detections was statistically significant.
That evidence supports describing ALPRs as useful investigative tools, not as a proven universal solution to crime (National Institute of Justice, 2018).
The goal of this legislation is therefore neither unrestricted surveillance nor a technology ban.
It is to preserve legitimate public-safety capabilities while establishing enforceable boundaries that protect innocent Texans, honest peace officers, local governments, criminal investigations, and the integrity of evidence.
