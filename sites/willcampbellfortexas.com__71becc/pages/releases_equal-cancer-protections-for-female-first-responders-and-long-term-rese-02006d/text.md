Equal Cancer Protections for Female First Responders and Long Term Research
Equal Cancer Protections for Female First Responders
What the proposed Texas law would change, why the change matters, and how a permanent first-responder health research program would turn better evidence into better protection.
This proposal has not been filed or enacted and remains subject to formal legislative, agency, medical, privacy, constitutional, and fiscal review.
This article is not legal or medical advice.
Executive Summary
What the bill does, why it is needed, and the central takeaway
Texas already recognizes that qualifying firefighters and emergency medical technicians can develop certain cancers through occupational exposure.
Government Code Chapter 607 creates a rebuttable presumption for a limited list of cancers: when an eligible responder meets the law’s examination, service, occupational-response, disability, and other requirements, the cancer is presumed work-related unless the employer or workers’ compensation carrier proves a qualifying nonoccupational cause.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
The problem is that the current list includes prostate and testicular cancers but omits breast cancer and the principal female reproductive cancers.
The law is facially sex-neutral, but its practical coverage is incomplete for women serving in the same carcinogenic work environment.
The Equal Cancer Protections for Female First Responders Act addresses that gap without creating an automatic payout or declaring that every cancer is caused by the job.
The proposal would:
- Add 16 named cancers to the existing list: breast, cervical, uterine—including endometrial—ovarian, fallopian-tube, primary-peritoneal, vaginal, vulvar, thyroid, basal-cell, bladder, esophageal, leukemia, lung, mesothelioma, and pancreatic cancer.
- State expressly that the presumption applies without regard to sex or whether a cancer predominantly or exclusively affects one sex.
- Preserve the existing five-year service requirement, qualifying physical examination, occupational-response requirements, tobacco limitation, disability-or-death requirement, and employer or carrier right to rebut the presumption.
- Allow any cancer listed in Section 607.055 to be discovered during covered employment or within three years after the responder’s last day of covered employment.
- Apply the amended law to new and pending claims and provide a limited opportunity to request reprocessing of certain claims previously denied because the cancer was not listed, without reopening a final court judgment.
- Preserve the existing connection to Texas’s separate post-retirement supplemental-benefit law, while leaving that law’s department-size threshold, benefit limit, and other restrictions in place.
The bill also creates the Texas First Responder Health Research Program at the Department of State Health Services.
The program would connect existing employment, exposure, claims, disease, injury, disability, and mortality records; support voluntary research; publish privacy-protected aggregate data; and create a recurring process for turning validated findings into better training, protective equipment, decontamination, screening, medical surveillance, station design, and exposure controls.
Texas’s September 2026 study of 3,330 certified female firefighters did not find a statistically significant increase in cancer compared with Texas women generally.
The same report expressly warned that its small cohort, small number of site-specific cases, limited follow-up, long cancer latency, and healthy-worker bias meant the study did not rule out occupational cancer risk.
The responsible conclusion is neither “the study proved the added cancers are work-caused” nor “the study proved women firefighters are safe.” It established a useful baseline and demonstrated why Texas needs longer, stronger, exposure-informed research.[Texas DSHS, 2026]
For casual readers, the central point is simple: the proposal gives eligible women and men access to the same rebuttable evidentiary framework, while building the evidence system Texas needs to prevent disease and make future decisions from stronger data.
The Current Law and the Gap
How Chapter 607 works, why parity is needed, and what Texas has learned
The Existing Gap
What Chapter 607 does
Texas Government Code Chapter 607 contains several presumptions concerning diseases and illnesses suffered by public-safety personnel.
For cancer, Section 607.055 applies to a qualifying firefighter or emergency medical technician whose cancer results in death or total or partial disability and who regularly responded to fires or firefighting calls, or regularly responded to a documented release of radiation or a known or suspected carcinogen.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
Current Section 607.055 covers cancers originating in the stomach, colon, rectum, skin, prostate, testis, or brain, together with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, multiple myeloma, malignant melanoma, and renal-cell carcinoma.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
The presumption is not stand-alone insurance.
Chapter 607 states that it does not create a cause of action, enlarge an underlying right to benefits, or make an otherwise ineligible person eligible.
Instead, it affects the determination of whether a covered disease or death occurred in the course and scope of employment for benefits or compensation available under another law, employee-benefit arrangement, or pension plan.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
Who qualifies now
The current framework generally requires a claimant to satisfy all of the following:
- A physical examination received on entering or during covered employment did not reveal the disease.
- The firefighter or emergency medical technician accumulated at least five years of covered employment.
- The disease was discovered during covered employment.
- The firefighter or emergency medical technician satisfies the statutory occupational-response test.
- The cancer resulted in death or total or partial disability.
- The person falls within the statute’s definitions, including the special rules applicable to qualifying volunteer firefighters.
- The tobacco-related limitation does not prevent use of the presumption.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
The tobacco limitation removes the presumption in specified circumstances; it does not erase the responder’s ability to try to prove occupational causation without the presumption.
Likewise, emergency medical technicians are not covered merely because of their title.
They must satisfy the existing service and response conditions.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
What “rebuttable” means
Once the presumption applies, an employer or carrier may rebut it by a preponderance of the evidence.
The opposing party must show that a non-service risk factor, accident, hazard, or other cause was a substantial factor in bringing about the disease and that the disease would not have occurred without that cause.
The rebuttal must identify the evidence reviewed, and an administrative law judge must consider whether a qualified expert, relying on evidence-based medicine, offered an opinion in terms of reasonable medical probability.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
A rebuttable presumption changes who must initially prove occupational causation.
It does not make a claim automatic or prevent contrary evidence.
That structure matters.
The bill does not make occupational causation irrebuttable.
It changes the initial allocation of proof for specifically listed cancers while preserving an individualized claim process.
Why Parity Is Needed
The current law does not say “women are excluded.” Its text applies to eligible firefighters and emergency medical technicians regardless of sex.
The disparity arises from the cancer list: prostate and testicular cancers are named, while breast, cervical, uterine, endometrial, ovarian, fallopian-tube, vaginal, and vulvar cancers are not.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
The most accurate description is therefore unequal practical coverage, not an accusation that Chapter 607 expressly bars women.
A female firefighter with an unlisted cancer can still pursue a workers’ compensation claim, but she begins without the evidentiary presumption available for listed cancers.
She may have to prove occupational causation from the ground up through medical experts, exposure history, contested proceedings, and appeals.
The proposed equal-application clause is genuinely sex-neutral.
A qualifying male firefighter with breast cancer would receive the same statutory treatment as a qualifying woman.
The bill focuses on female first responders because that is where the most visible gap exists, but its operative protections do not condition coverage on sex.
A Texas Example
Austin Fire Department Lieutenant Suzanne LaFollette’s case illustrates the difference between winning an individual claim and having presumptive coverage.
According to the International Association of Fire Fighters, LaFollette was diagnosed with Stage 4 uterine—or endometrial—cancer after nearly two decades in the fire service.
Her claim was initially denied because uterine cancer was not on Texas’s presumptive list.[IAFF, 2026]
The association reports that, after testimony and a contested proceeding, an administrative law judge ruled on April 21, 2026, that she had sustained a compensable occupational disease and was eligible for workers’ compensation benefits.[IAFF, 2026] That outcome is important, but it should be described carefully: the ruling concerned the evidence in one firefighter’s case.
It did not amend Chapter 607 or establish a statewide rule that every uterine cancer is occupationally caused.
Before LaFollette’s case is used as a formal legislative finding, legislative or committee staff should obtain the certified Division of Workers’ Compensation decision, denial notice, docket information, and finality or appeal record.
The public association account is useful stakeholder evidence, but it is not a substitute for the official administrative record.
What Texas Studied
House Bill 1639, enacted in 2025, directed the Department of State Health Services to work with the Texas Commission on Fire Protection and use Texas Cancer Registry information to compare cancer rates among female firefighters with rates among other Texas women.
It required attention to female-specific cancers, including ovarian and breast cancer, and a public report by September 1, 2026.[Texas H.B. 1639, 2025]
DSHS linked fire-service personnel records with statewide cancer-registry records.
The final cohort included 3,330 certified female firefighters and 43,503 person-years of follow-up.
The analysis identified 122 women who developed 136 cancers, because some women had more than one primary cancer.[Texas DSHS, 2026]

A standardized incidence ratio, or SIR, compares the observed number of cancers in the firefighter cohort with the number expected from Texas female cancer rates after adjustment for age, race or ethnicity, and calendar year.
An SIR of 1.00 means observed and expected incidence are equal.
An estimate is not conventionally considered statistically significant when its 95% confidence interval includes 1.00.[Texas DSHS, 2026]
What the result means
The study did not demonstrate a statistically significant statewide increase for all cancers combined or for the reported site-specific cancers.
That finding must be stated plainly.
It is equally important not to turn “not statistically significant” into “proved no occupational risk.” DSHS identified several limitations:
- The available cohort included only 3,330 women.
- Several site-specific analyses involved fewer than five cancers, producing unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals.
- More than half of the cohort was first certified from 2010 through 2023.
- Many participants therefore had limited follow-up compared with the latency period of many cancers.
- The available records did not permit detailed analysis by years of service, career versus volunteer status, personal protective equipment use, job assignment, specific exposure, or exposure-response relationship.
- Comparison with the general population was vulnerable to the “healthy worker effect,” in which people able to enter and remain in a physically demanding occupation may initially be healthier than the general population.[Texas DSHS, 2026]
The study’s most durable accomplishment may be methodological: Texas demonstrated that it can link statewide firefighter personnel records with cancer-registry outcomes.
The next task is to improve the denominator, add exposure and work-history detail, extend follow-up, include understudied service groups, and repeat the analysis over time.
What Firefighters Encounter
The International Agency for Research on Cancer, the cancer agency of the World Health Organization, classifies occupational exposure as a firefighter as carcinogenic to humans, Group 1.
IARC found sufficient human evidence for mesothelioma and bladder cancer, limited evidence for several additional cancers, and strong mechanistic evidence involving genotoxicity, epigenetic alteration, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and receptor-mediated effects.[IARC, 2022/2023]
Group 1 is a hazard classification.
It means the occupation can cause cancer; it does not mean every exposure produces cancer or that every cancer in every firefighter was caused by the job.
That distinction is essential to an honest defense of a rebuttable presumption.
Firefighters may encounter combustion products, particulate matter, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, asbestos, diesel exhaust, chemicals in firefighting foams, PFAS, flame retardants, ultraviolet radiation, contaminated equipment, and night-shift work.[DeBono et al., 2023] Exposure may occur through inhalation, skin absorption, ingestion, contaminated turnout gear, apparatus, or the station environment.
A 2024 systematic review focused on breast-cancer-related exposures identified 12 chemicals or chemical groups that were both associated with breast-cancer evidence and present as firefighter occupational exposures.
The researchers reported strong evidence of elevated firefighter exposure for nine groups, including benzene, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, acetaldehyde, styrene, dioxin-like compounds, flame retardants, PFAS, and PCBs.[Cardona et al., 2024]
This supports biological plausibility and exposure reduction.
It does not by itself establish a Texas incidence ratio or prove causation in an individual claim.
The article, bill, and legislative testimony should maintain that boundary.
What the Bill Changes
Expanded cancers, equal application, transition rules, and legal boundaries
Sixteen added cancers
The proposal preserves the existing Section 607.055 list and adds:
- Basal-cell carcinoma.
- Bladder cancer.
- Breast cancer.
- Cervical cancer.
- Esophageal cancer.
- Fallopian-tube cancer.
- Leukemia.
- Lung cancer.
- Mesothelioma.
- Ovarian cancer.
- Pancreatic cancer.
- Primary-peritoneal cancer.
- Thyroid cancer.
- Uterine cancer, including endometrial cancer.
- Vaginal cancer.
- Vulvar cancer.
The list combines parity cancers with additional cancers recognized in federal guidance, other states, occupational-hazard evidence, or the policy record.
Medical nomenclature should receive final review from an occupational oncologist and cancer-registry specialist before filing—for example, whether the existing “skin cancer” category already encompasses basal-cell carcinoma and whether leukemia should be divided by subtype.
Equal application
The bill states that the presumption applies without regard to:
- The sex of the firefighter or emergency medical technician.
- Whether the cancer predominantly or exclusively occurs in persons of one sex.
This avoids creating a sex-exclusive benefit and prevents similarly situated responders from being treated differently solely because a cancer is associated with one sex.
Three-year discovery window
Current law generally requires discovery during employment.
The proposal treats a Section 607.055 cancer discovered within three years after the responder’s last day of qualifying employment as discovered during that employment.
The three-year duration is a limited Texas-alignment choice, not a medical declaration that every occupational cancer appears within three years.
It matches the period already used by Texas’s separate retiree supplemental-benefit statute.
Arizona, by contrast, allows certain former firefighters age 65 or younger to use its presumption for a listed cancer diagnosed within 15 years after employment.[Arizona Rev.
Stat. § 23-901.09]
The Texas First Responder Health Research Program would study latency and claims outcomes, giving future legislatures an evidence base for deciding whether three years is sufficient or whether specific diseases or occupations require a longer period.
Pending and denied claims
The bill applies the expanded cancer provisions to:
- Claims filed on or after the effective date.
- Pending claims that have not produced a final judgment.
- Certain earlier claims denied wholly or partly because the cancer was not listed, if the claimant submits a reprocessing request by September 1, 2028.
A carrier would have 60 days to accept the reprocessed claim or issue a written denial explaining every basis and the dispute procedure.
The claimant would still have to prove all other eligibility requirements.
A final court judgment would not be reopened.
Texas has used a similar time-limited reprocessing mechanism for first-responder COVID-19 claims.
Under Senate Bill 22, eligible claimants could request reprocessing, carriers had 60 days to respond, and DWC created a standardized notice and dispute process.[Texas DWC, S.B. 22] That precedent supports the mechanism, but the final cancer provision still requires constitutional, retroactivity, vested-rights, and claim-finality review.
What It Does Not Do
The proposal does not:
- Guarantee payment for every person diagnosed with a listed cancer.
- Eliminate the five-year service requirement.
- Eliminate the qualifying physical-examination requirement.
- Eliminate the occupational-response test for firefighters or emergency medical technicians.
- Remove the statutory tobacco limitation.
- Remove the requirement that the cancer result in death or total or partial disability.
- Prevent an employer or carrier from rebutting the presumption with qualifying evidence.
- Convert a research finding into an individual medical diagnosis.
- Declare that every added cancer is conclusively caused by firefighting.
- Make every emergency medical technician eligible regardless of actual fire or carcinogen response.
- Create a new general health-insurance entitlement.
- Reopen a final court judgment.
- Make voluntary surveys, genetic testing, imaging, clinical examinations, biomarker work, or biospecimen collection a condition of employment, promotion, certification, assignment, or benefits.
These limits keep the proposal focused on equal access to an existing evidentiary framework and on improving the quality of future evidence.
Texas’s Larger Framework
The proposal complements—rather than replaces—Texas laws addressing screening, active-employment presumptions, and limited retiree benefits.

HB 198 took effect June 1, 2026.
Its covered screening includes a urine test, pulmonary-function test, electrocardiogram, infectious-disease screening, breast-cancer screening, blood test, and a chest X-ray once every five years.
A physician-endorsed annual occupational examination substantially complying with National Fire Protection Association standards can satisfy the requirement, subject to Texas Commission on Fire Protection implementation.[Texas H.B. 198, 2025]
HB 4144 applies to covered persons retiring on or after January 1, 2026.
It also contains an exception for a political subdivision that already provides a retiree health plan comparable in coverage and cost to the plan offered immediately before retirement.
Expanding Section 607.055 may therefore have both workers’ compensation and retiree-benefit fiscal effects; both belong in the fiscal note.[Texas H.B. 4144, 2025]
Other Government Models
Federal firefighters
Federal law, 5 U.S.C.
Section 8143b, creates a presumption for federal employees in qualifying fire-protection activities who have at least five aggregate years of service and are diagnosed with a listed condition within the applicable post-service period.
Its initial list includes bladder, brain, colorectal, esophageal, kidney, lung, prostate, testicular, and thyroid cancers, leukemia, mesothelioma, multiple myeloma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and melanoma, among other conditions.[5 U.S.C. § 8143b]
The Department of Labor later identified breast, cervical, uterine, ovarian, and basal-cell cancers for a high-risk, expedited process under FECA Bulletin 25-01.
Those claims receive medical-adviser review; that administrative process should not be described as identical to the statutory presumption.[U.S.
DOL FECA Bulletin 25-01]
Arizona
Arizona’s current statute expressly includes breast and ovarian cancer.
It generally requires a pre-employment physical examination and at least five years of hazardous duty, reaches certain former firefighters and fire investigators diagnosed within 15 years after employment, and permits rebuttal only with clear and convincing evidence of a specific nonoccupational cause.[Arizona Rev.
Stat. § 23-901.09]
The Texas proposal does not copy Arizona’s stronger rebuttal standard or longer post-service period.
It keeps Texas’s existing preponderance standard and begins with a three-year window.
Florida
Florida expressly covers breast, cervical, ovarian, and thyroid cancers, among others.
Its law uses a different model: qualifying full-time firefighters may receive employer-funded treatment-cost reimbursement, a $25,000 initial-diagnosis payment, leave and retention protection, disability benefits, and death benefits as an alternative to pursuing workers’ compensation.
Certain treatment and cash benefits may continue for up to 10 years after employment when statutory conditions are satisfied.[Florida Stat. § 112.1816]
Florida demonstrates that women-inclusive coverage is administratively possible, but its benefit design should not be confused with Texas’s Chapter 607 presumption.
The Research Program
A permanent statewide system for evidence, prevention, privacy, and accountability
The second half of the proposal is broader than cancer and broader than firefighting.
It creates a permanent, DSHS-led system for understanding and reducing occupational disease, injury, disability, and death across Texas first-responder professions.
Who it includes
The program’s research population includes:
- Career, part-time, volunteer, industrial, airport, marine, and wildland firefighters.
- Fire investigators and fire-service instructors.
- Emergency medical services personnel.
- Peace officers, county jailers, detention officers, custodial officers, and correctional personnel.
- Public-safety telecommunicators, 9-1-1 call takers, and dispatchers.
- Emergency-management and field disaster-response personnel.
- Other materially similar emergency-response occupations designated by DSHS rule.
This does not extend Chapter 607’s cancer presumption to every group in the research program.
Benefit eligibility and research inclusion are separate legal questions.
The broader program population exists so Texas can identify occupation-specific hazards before deciding whether prevention, training, equipment, medical, administrative, or legislative changes are warranted.
What data it connects
DSHS would serve as lead agency and coordinate through memoranda of understanding with agencies that already maintain relevant information, including the Texas Commission on Fire Protection, Division of Workers’ Compensation, Texas Commission on Law Enforcement, State Fire Marshal, Texas Department of Criminal Justice, Texas Juvenile Justice Department, Texas A&M Forest Service, Texas Division of Emergency Management, Commission on State Emergency Communications, and DSHS emergency-medical and registry programs.
The statewide system is designed to follow:
- Employment and service history.
- Job assignment and operational role.
- Incident type and duration.
- Known or suspected occupational exposures.
- Respiratory protection and personal protective equipment.
- Equipment condition, cleaning, and decontamination.
- Training and workplace prevention practices.
- Workers’ compensation claims and outcomes.
- Disease, injury, disability, retirement, and mortality outcomes.
Using an occupational denominator—knowing who served, where, when, and in what role—is essential.
A database containing only sick responders cannot determine rates or compare risk across assignments.
That is why the bill prioritizes reliable roster information before publishing disease-rate comparisons.
Administrative reporting
First-responder entities would submit minimum administrative data in standardized electronic formats.
The bill calls for annual roster and prevention-practice reporting, quarterly reporting of hires, separations, retirements, and deaths when electronically available, and incident or exposure reporting within 30 days or through an approved batch process.
The bill deliberately rejects punitive local enforcement.
No civil, administrative, licensing, certification, grant, or monetary penalty could be imposed for late or incomplete reporting.
DSHS would instead provide forms, specifications, technical assistance, confidential data-quality notices, cooperative correction plans, and—when appropriated—implementation grants prioritizing small, rural, and volunteer organizations.
Voluntary research
Administrative reporting is distinct from direct participation in research.
Surveys, interviews, clinical examinations, imaging, genetic tests, biomarkers, and biospecimen collection would remain voluntary and require informed consent.
A participant could not be required to enroll as a condition of employment, certification, promotion, assignment, benefits, or the employer’s participation.
Research requiring human-subject review would need institutional review board approval.
Consent documents would address purpose, collection, risk, record linkage, retention, destruction, confidentiality limits, return of results, withdrawal, and the participant’s right not to know.
Return of results
A protected linkage key could be used to contact a consenting participant only under a controlled return-of-results protocol.
The finding would have to be scientifically valid, clinically confirmed, potentially serious and actionable, approved for return by an institutional review board, and communicated by DSHS or a designated clinical team—not by a researcher possessing only coded data.
An unconfirmed research signal could not be presented as a medical diagnosis.
The participant would receive referral information and retain the right not to receive individual findings, subject to existing law governing serious and imminent threats.
Privacy safeguards
The proposal separates information into distinct privacy tiers:
- Identifiable source data: held only where legally authorized and needed for linkage or administration.
- Coded research data: direct identifiers removed and replaced with a program identifier.
- Protected linkage key: stored separately under restricted control.
- Public data: de-identified, aggregated, and reviewed for disclosure risk.
The bill requires encryption, role-based access, multifactor authentication, audit logging, minimum-necessary access, retention and destruction schedules, breach procedures, and secure research environments.
Researchers would work under data-use agreements prohibiting reidentification, unauthorized contact, and redisclosure.
Identifiable or coded research information could not be given to employers, prospective employers, licensing authorities, insurers, carriers, or benefit plans for employment, discipline, licensing, underwriting, or individual-benefit decisions unless the person specifically authorized the use, a court ordered it, or another law expressly required it.
Voluntary research information could not be used to rebut a Chapter 607 presumption without the participant’s specific written authorization.
Public website and API
DSHS would publish a public website using existing Texas open-data infrastructure where practical.
It would provide:
- Privacy-protected dashboards.
- Downloadable CSV and JSON files.
- A documented and versioned application programming interface, or API.
- Data dictionaries and codebooks.
- Protocols, methods, limitations, suppression rules, quality statements, and revision histories.
- Annual and five-year reports.
- Validated trends, publications, and prevention guidance.
The public system could not publish names, direct identifiers, the protected linkage key, a stable person-level identifier, precise histories creating unreasonable identification risk, or other high-risk fields.
Small-cell suppression and other disclosure controls would be especially important for rare cancers, small rural agencies, and uncommon job assignments.
Scientific standards
The program must distinguish among four different propositions:
- Hazard: an exposure can cause harm.
- Association: an exposure and outcome occur together more often than expected.
- Causation: the total evidence supports a causal relationship.
- Individual adjudication: a particular responder satisfies the legal and medical standard in a specific claim.
Research would use accepted methods; prospective, longitudinal designs when feasible; healthy comparison responders when needed; and occupation- and sex-specific analysis.
Reports would disclose denominators, uncertainty, missing data, confounding, limitations, multiple-comparison issues, methods, and revisions.
Major protocols would be registered when appropriate, independently reviewed, and accompanied by public methods or code when lawful.
From Research to Prevention
The program’s purpose is not to build a database for its own sake.
When surveillance identifies a potentially preventable concern, DSHS would follow a recurring implementation cycle:
- Validate the pattern.
- Identify modifiable practices or exposures.
- Support a targeted pilot.
- Measure adoption, exposure reduction, health outcomes, feasibility, operational capability, and unintended effects.
- Publish the results.
- Issue evidence-based guidance.
- Recommend standards, appropriations, procurement changes, training, or legislation when warranted.
- Continue surveillance to determine whether the intervention worked.
Examples could include improved apparatus exhaust capture, turnout-gear handling, decontamination, respiratory protection, station design, sleep and fatigue practices, hearing protection, cancer screening, behavioral-health programs, or safer equipment procurement.
The statute does not predetermine which intervention is correct; it creates the process for testing and improving them.
That process benefits professional effectiveness as well as health.
A responder who avoids a preventable exposure, receives appropriate early evaluation, or returns safely after illness is better positioned to remain ready, experienced, and available to the community.
Departments also gain better information for training and procurement instead of relying on anecdotes or one-size-fits-all recommendations.
Governance and Independence
The bill creates a Texas First Responder Health Research Consortium and a 24-member advisory council.
The consortium would competitively select a coordinating center and give qualified institutions across multiple Texas university systems and regions a reasonable opportunity to participate.
Work could include epidemiology, medicine, industrial hygiene, toxicology, behavioral and reproductive health, engineering, equipment testing, privacy, data science, implementation science, screening, and medical surveillance.
The advisory council would combine state-agency designees with appointed responders, nonprofit or association representatives, physicians, epidemiologists, exposure scientists, equipment experts, behavioral- or reproductive-health experts, implementation scientists, privacy and cybersecurity specialists, and university researchers.
Appointments would seek meaningful representation of women, active and retired responders, different regions, and different forms of service.
The council would advise; it could not run the program, direct scientific findings, decide individual claims, possess the linkage key, or receive unrestricted access to identifiable records.
Grants and contracts would preserve scientific independence and publication rights.
A funder could not control the methods, suppress a result, materially alter a finding, or unreasonably delay publication.
How It Complements Research
The proposal should not duplicate the National Firefighter Registry for Cancer or the UTHealth Texas Firefighter Cancer Study.
It should make Texas data interoperable with those efforts and fill gaps they were not designed to cover.
The National Firefighter Registry is a voluntary national system open to active, former, and retired firefighters; career, volunteer, structural, wildland, industrial, military, instructional, and investigative personnel; and firefighters with or without cancer.
NIOSH links work-history and exposure information with state cancer-registry records to study outcomes over time.[NIOSH National Firefighter Registry]
UTHealth’s Texas Firefighter Cancer Study has three connected projects: carcinogenic-exposure assessment and biomarker analysis, occupational cancer epidemiology using work history and Texas Cancer Registry data, and firefighter-specific screening promotion.
The initiative studies new recruits, active firefighters, and retirees.[UTHealth Texas Firefighter Cancer Study]
The proposed state program adds four elements:
- Permanence beyond a two-year appropriation or individual grant.
- Administrative denominators and state-agency linkage authority.
- Coverage of first-responder occupations beyond firefighting.
- A statutory prevention-and-implementation cycle tied to public reporting and legislative review.
The best implementation would reuse validated instruments, avoid asking responders or local departments for data already held by the state, and permit national or university collaboration under Texas privacy rules.
Reporting and Accountability
The bill requires three reporting levels:
- Annual report: program status, trends, research, evidence gaps, prevention results, expenditures, guidance, and recommendations.
- Even-numbered-year legislative report: costs, performance, and conditions that may warrant presumptive or other occupational protection.
- Five-year comprehensive review: independently reviewed assessment of epidemiology, exposure, prevention, privacy, and data quality.
Initial rules, council appointments, and DWC cancer-claim forms would be due by March 1, 2028.
Roster reporting would begin by September 1, 2028; statewide exposure and prevention reporting and the public website and API would be targeted for September 1, 2029; and the first annual report would be due December 1, 2029.
The staggered schedule is intentional.
Reliable denominators, privacy controls, data standards, and local technical assistance should come before statewide disease-rate claims.
Costs and Fiscal Questions
The bill would require state appropriations for DSHS staffing, secure linkage, technology, public data, the university consortium, research, prevention pilots, and local technical assistance.
Federal grants, gifts, interagency transfers, and university cost sharing could supplement—but not replace—core appropriations.
The expanded cancer list may also affect local workers’ compensation costs and, through the Section 607.055 cross-reference, HB 4144 retiree benefits.
Those effects are real policy questions, not reasons to avoid transparent analysis.
Before filing, the fiscal record should seek:
- DWC claims by cancer site, sex, occupation, employer type, acceptance status, dispute outcome, and benefits paid.
- Historical denials involving newly listed cancers.
- Active, former, retired, and volunteer responder counts by age and service length.
- Counts inside and outside HB 4144’s 50-responder threshold.
- Expected diagnoses during the proposed three-year period.
- Municipal risk-pool and self-insured estimates.
- Reprocessing costs.
- State and local technology, reporting, privacy, cybersecurity, staffing, and training costs.
- Potential savings from using existing systems and avoiding duplicate collection.
Until those data are obtained, the proposal should not claim a precise cost or savings figure.
Questions and Bottom Line
Direct answers for responders, departments, lawmakers, and constituents
Common Questions
Didn’t the Texas study find no increased cancer risk?
It found no statistically significant difference in the available dataset.
DSHS also stated that the result did not rule out occupational risk because the cohort was small, several cancer counts were very low, follow-up was limited relative to cancer latency, exposure detail was unavailable, and healthy-worker bias could suppress estimates.
Both parts of the result must be presented together.[Texas DSHS, 2026]
If the science is uncertain, why create a presumption?
A rebuttable presumption is a legal rule for allocating proof under uncertainty.
Texas already uses that framework for listed cancers.
The proposal extends the same process to additional cancers while keeping service requirements and the employer’s right to prove a substantial nonoccupational cause.
Isn’t the current law already sex-neutral?
Its wording is sex-neutral, but its list includes prostate and testicular cancers while omitting breast and female reproductive cancers.
The proposal corrects practical coverage without creating a women-only entitlement.
Will every newly diagnosed responder automatically receive benefits?
No.
The claimant must satisfy the underlying benefit law and Chapter 607 requirements.
The presumption can be rebutted.
Chapter 607 itself does not create a new cause of action or a guaranteed payment.[Texas Gov’t Code ch. 607]
Does this give all first responders the firefighter cancer presumption?
No.
The cancer amendment remains within Section 607.055 for qualifying firefighters and emergency medical technicians.
The broader definition of “first responder” applies to the research program, not automatically to workers’ compensation presumptions.
Does annual cancer screening solve the problem already?
No.
Screening and compensation serve different purposes.
The Wade Cannon Act promotes confidential early detection.
The presumption governs how occupational causation is evaluated after an eligible responder develops a disabling or fatal listed cancer.[Texas H.B. 198, 2025]
Is three years long enough?
Three years is a conservative starting point aligned with existing Texas retiree-benefit law, not a claim about medical latency.
The research program would evaluate whether later legislation should adopt longer or cancer-specific periods.
Will the research database expose private medical information?
The proposal forbids public person-level data, separates identifiers from research records, restricts access, requires security controls and disclosure review, and bars use of voluntary research information against a claim without specific written authorization.
Will small or volunteer departments be fined for reporting problems?
No.
The bill prohibits civil, administrative, licensing, grant, certification, and monetary penalties for incomplete or late reporting.
It relies on technical assistance, confidential quality notices, cooperative correction, standardized tools, and implementation grants when funded.
Does the program create unnecessary bureaucracy?
That risk must be managed.
The bill directs DSHS to use existing state systems first, reduce duplicate reporting, coordinate through memoranda of understanding, competitively select research partners, publish costs and performance, and subject the overall program to recurring independent review.
Why It Matters
First responders accept unusual occupational hazards on behalf of the public.
Texas owes them neither unsupported promises nor impossible evidentiary burdens.
It owes them rules that are even-handed, evidence-conscious, and honest about uncertainty.
The proposal advances that standard in two ways.
First, it corrects the practical imbalance in the cancer list while preserving eligibility rules and rebuttal.
Second, it creates an evidence system capable of improving over time—one that can identify hazards earlier, test prevention, protect individual privacy, publish useful aggregate findings, and tell future legislatures when the evidence supports a change.
The bill’s strongest argument is not that one study or one case proves everything.
It is that the existing law should provide an equal rebuttable framework for responders facing comparable occupational exposures, while Texas builds the long-term evidence needed to protect the health, readiness, and professional capability of every first-responder community.

ARTICLE 1.
SHORT TITLE AND PURPOSE
SECTION 1.01.
SHORT TITLE.
This Act may be cited as the Equal Cancer Protections for Female First Responders Act.
SECTION 1.02.
PURPOSE.
The purposes of this Act are to:
(1) ensure that the statutory presumption for occupational cancer applies without regard to the sex of an eligible firefighter or emergency medical technician or whether a covered cancer predominantly or exclusively occurs in persons of a particular sex;
(2) modernize the list of cancers covered by the presumption;
(3) provide a limited post-service period for discovery of a covered cancer;
(4) provide a time-limited opportunity to request reprocessing of certain previously denied claims; and
(5) establish a continuing statewide program to study the occupational health of first responders and translate scientifically validated findings into improved training, equipment, screening, medical practices, and exposure controls.

ARTICLE 3.
TEXAS FIRST RESPONDER HEALTH RESEARCH PROGRAM
SECTION 3.01.
Subtitle B, Title 2, Health and Safety Code, is amended by adding Chapter 55 to read as follows:
CHAPTER 55.
TEXAS FIRST RESPONDER HEALTH RESEARCH PROGRAM
SUBCHAPTER A.
GENERAL PROVISIONS
Sec. 55.001.
DEFINITIONS.
In this chapter:
(1) "Advisory council" means the Texas First Responder Health Research Advisory Council.
(2) "Coded data" means information concerning an individual that is assigned a unique program identifier and from which direct identifiers have been removed, but for which the department or a trusted linkage unit maintains a separately secured key that permits reidentification for a purpose authorized by this chapter.
(3) "Department" means the Department of State Health Services.
(4) "De-identified data" means information processed in accordance with applicable state and federal law and generally accepted statistical disclosure-control standards so that the information does not identify an individual and there is no reasonable basis to believe the information can be used to identify an individual.
(5) "First responder" means:
(A) a public safety employee as defined by Section 607.001, Government Code;
(B) a firefighter as defined by Section 607.051, Government Code, including a career, part-time, volunteer, industrial, airport, marine, or wildland firefighter and a fire investigator or fire-service instructor;
(C) emergency medical services personnel as defined by Section 773.003;
(D) a peace officer, county jailer, or telecommunicator licensed, certified, or otherwise regulated under Chapter 1701, Occupations Code;
(E) a detention officer or custodial officer as defined by Section 607.051, Government Code;
(F) an employee of the Texas Department of Criminal Justice, the Texas Juvenile Justice Department, a county, a municipality, or another governmental entity whose regular duties include the custody, supervision, transportation, or direct care of a person confined or detained in a correctional, detention, or juvenile facility;
(G) an employee or volunteer of the Texas A&M Forest Service, a political subdivision, a volunteer fire department, or another recognized organization whose regular duties include wildfire suppression, prescribed-fire operations, or emergency wildland response;
(H) a public safety telecommunicator, 9-1-1 call taker, or dispatcher employed by or serving a public safety answering point or public safety agency;
(I) emergency management or disaster-response personnel whose regular duties include field response during an emergency or declared disaster; or
(J) another occupational group designated by department rule whose members regularly perform substantially similar emergency-response duties and experience material occupational health exposures.
(6) "First responder entity" means a state agency, political subdivision, department, authority, public safety answering point, emergency medical services provider, volunteer organization, nonprofit organization, or other public or private entity that employs, appoints, certifies, credentials, supervises, or maintains service records for a first responder.
(7) "Institution of higher education" has the meaning assigned by Section 61.003, Education Code.
(8) "Participant" means a first responder or former first responder who voluntarily consents to participate in a research activity under this chapter that requires individual informed consent.
(9) "Program" means the Texas First Responder Health Research Program.
(10) "Protected linkage key" means the information maintained separately from coded data that permits an authorized person to associate a program identifier with a specific individual.
Sec. 55.002.
ESTABLISHMENT AND PURPOSE.
(a) The department shall establish and administer the Texas First Responder Health Research Program.
(b) The purposes of the program are to:
(1) conduct continuing longitudinal surveillance of occupational exposures, diseases, injuries, disabilities, and mortality among first responders;
(2) identify health conditions and harmful exposures that occur disproportionately or demonstrate a scientifically concerning pattern among first responders, including differences associated with occupation, assignment, geography, type of service, and sex;
(3) support rigorous, reproducible occupational-health research;
(4) evaluate training, equipment, personal protective equipment, decontamination, station design, staffing practices, screening, medical surveillance, and other prevention measures;
(5) develop and evaluate practical interventions to reduce occupational disease, injury, disability, and death and to improve first-responder readiness and professional capability;
(6) coordinate state agencies, first responder entities, nonprofit organizations, professional associations, and institutions of higher education engaged in first-responder health research and prevention; and
(7) provide policymakers, first responders, employing entities, researchers, and the public with accurate, privacy-protected information concerning first-responder occupational health.
Sec. 55.003.
ADMINISTRATION; INTERAGENCY COORDINATION.
(a) The department is the lead agency for the program and retains authority over program administration, rulemaking, contracting, appropriations, data custody, privacy, and publication.
(b) The department shall enter into memoranda of understanding as necessary with the Texas Commission on Fire Protection; Texas Department of Insurance, Division of Workers' Compensation; Texas Commission on Law Enforcement; State Fire Marshal's Office; Texas Department of Criminal Justice; Texas Juvenile Justice Department; Texas A&M Forest Service; Texas Division of Emergency Management; Commission on State Emergency Communications; the department's emergency medical services and registry programs; and other state agencies that collect information relevant to the program.
(c) A memorandum of understanding must address information sharing, transmission, data quality, privacy, security, access, retention, redisclosure, costs, and reduction of duplicative reporting.
(d) The department shall use existing state data systems and interoperable exchanges to the greatest extent practicable before requiring a new reporting system.
SUBCHAPTER B.
DATA COLLECTION AND RESEARCH
Sec. 55.051.
STATEWIDE DATA SYSTEM.
(a) The department shall maintain a statewide data system permitting longitudinal analysis of first-responder employment, service, exposure, prevention, health, claims, disability, and mortality information.
(b) The system must use a unique coded identifier; separate the protected linkage key; permit lawful record linkage; support occupation-, exposure-, geography-, and sex-specific analysis; distinguish relevant service categories; and include active, former, and retired first responders to the extent lawful information is available.
(c) The department may not publish the protected linkage key or a stable person-level identifier.
Sec. 55.052.
REQUIRED ADMINISTRATIVE REPORTING.
(a) Each first responder entity shall submit the minimum information required by department rule to establish a reliable occupational denominator and evaluate occupational exposures and prevention practices.
(b) Required information may include dates of service; occupation and assignment; incident type, operational role, duration, and known or suspected exposures; respiratory protection and personal protective equipment use; fit testing, equipment condition and cleaning; decontamination practices; workplace conditions; and occupational-health training.
(c) A first responder entity shall report roster and service information annually; electronically available hires, separations, retirements, and deaths quarterly; incident and exposure information within 30 days or by approved quarterly batch; and prevention-practice information annually.
(d) The department may adjust frequency to reduce burden, improve quality, or address scientific or privacy needs but may not eliminate annual roster and prevention-practice reporting.
(e) The department shall provide standardized forms, specifications, technical assistance, and, from appropriated money, implementation grants, with priority for small, rural, and volunteer entities.
(f) No civil, administrative, licensing, certification, grant, or monetary penalty may be imposed for incomplete or untimely reporting.
The department shall use technical assistance, confidential data-quality notices, and cooperative correction plans.
(g) The department may publish privacy-protected aggregate measures of data completeness.
Sec. 55.053.
RECORD LINKAGE AND INTERAGENCY DATA.
(a) Subject to federal law, a state agency or first responder entity may disclose information necessary to carry out this chapter.
Information confidential under other law remains confidential.
(b) The department shall conduct scientifically appropriate, lawful linkages at least annually.
(c) The Division of Workers' Compensation shall provide standardized information concerning first-responder claims, including claimed condition, use of a Chapter 607 presumption, status, denial basis, decision time, adjudication, and benefits paid.
Sec. 55.054.
VOLUNTARY INDIVIDUAL PARTICIPATION.
(a) Participation in surveys, interviews, clinical examinations, imaging, genetic tests, biomarker assessments, biospecimen collection, or other direct research intervention is voluntary and requires informed consent.
(b) Participation may not be required as a condition of employment, certification, promotion, assignment, benefits, or employer participation in the program.
(c) A participant may withdraw as provided by the protocol without adverse employment or benefit consequence.
(d) The department and participating institutions shall obtain institutional review board approval when required.
(e) Consent must describe collection, purposes, risks, uses, linkages, return of findings, right not to know, withdrawal, retention, destruction, and confidentiality limits.
Sec. 55.055.
RETURN OF INDIVIDUAL FINDINGS.
(a) The department shall establish a return-of-results protocol.
(b) The department or its designated clinical team may use the protected linkage key to contact a participant only if the participant consented; the finding is scientifically valid and clinically confirmed; a qualified health professional determines it concerns a serious condition or risk for which timely evaluation may benefit the participant; the institutional review board approved the procedure; and contact is not made by a researcher having only coded or de-identified data.
(c) The department shall offer referral information and may not present an unconfirmed research finding as a medical diagnosis.
(d) This section does not prohibit a disclosure otherwise authorized or required by law to prevent or lessen a serious and imminent threat.
Sec. 55.056.
SCIENTIFIC STANDARDS.
Research must use accepted methods; use prospective longitudinal design when feasible; include healthy responders when needed; evaluate exposure-response and latency; perform valid occupation- and sex-specific analyses; address bias, missing data, confounding, and multiple comparisons; report denominators, uncertainty, methods, limitations, and revisions; register major protocols when appropriate; undergo independent review; publish methods and code when lawful; and distinguish hazard, association, causation, and individual adjudication.
Sec. 55.057.
MINIMUM SUBJECT AREAS.
The program shall, subject to feasibility, study cancer; cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease; pulmonary disease; reproductive and maternal health; neurological and renal disease; hearing loss; infectious disease; musculoskeletal injury; sleep, fatigue, and shift work; behavioral health, post-traumatic stress, substance-use disorders, and suicide; occupational injury, disability, and mortality; and other conditions designated by the department.
SUBCHAPTER C.
UNIVERSITY RESEARCH CONSORTIUM
Sec. 55.101.
TEXAS FIRST RESPONDER HEALTH RESEARCH CONSORTIUM.
(a) The department shall establish a consortium of qualified institutions of higher education and appropriate nonprofit research organizations.
(b) The department shall competitively select a coordinating center and provide qualified institutions across multiple Texas university systems and regions a reasonable opportunity to participate.
(c) Activities may include epidemiology, medicine, industrial hygiene, toxicology, behavioral health, engineering, equipment testing, data science, privacy, implementation science, screening, and medical surveillance.
(d) Institutions may integrate approved work into supervised educational programs subject to privacy, data-use, and institutional review requirements.
(e) A grant or contract must preserve scientific independence and publication rights.
A funder may not control methods or suppress or materially alter findings.
Sec. 55.102.
COMPETITIVE RESEARCH AND PREVENTION PROJECTS.
(a) From available money, the department may fund priority research, rapid investigation, equipment and training evaluation, risk-reduction pilots, and implementation evaluation.
(b) Selection must be competitive and merit based and consider scientific quality, operational relevance, responder participation, feasibility, privacy, and population inclusion.
(c) A recipient may be required to share de-identified data, methods, and code.
SUBCHAPTER D.
ADVISORY COUNCIL
Sec. 55.151.
ADVISORY COUNCIL.
(a) The Texas First Responder Health Research Advisory Council is established to advise the department.
(b) The council consists of 24 members.
(c) The following officials, or their designees, serve: the commissioner of state health services; executive director of the Texas Commission on Fire Protection; commissioner of workers' compensation; executive director of the Texas Commission on Law Enforcement; state fire marshal; executive directors of the Texas Department of Criminal Justice and Texas Juvenile Justice Department; director of the Texas A&M Forest Service; executive director of the Commission on State Emergency Communications; and chief of the Texas Division of Emergency Management.
(d) The commissioner appoints 14 members: one career firefighter; one volunteer or wildland firefighter; one EMS professional; one peace officer; one detention, custodial, jail, or correctional officer; one telecommunicator; one emergency-management professional; one association or nonprofit representative; one occupational-medicine or oncology physician; one epidemiologist or biostatistician; one industrial-hygiene, toxicology, engineering, or protective-equipment expert; one behavioral-health, reproductive-health, or implementation-science expert; one privacy, cybersecurity, linkage, or ethics expert; and one additional relevant researcher.
(e) At least four scientific appointees must be affiliated with institutions representing at least three Texas public university systems.
(f) Appointments must seek meaningful representation of women, active and retired responders, geographic diversity, and varied forms of service.
Sec. 55.152.
TERMS; MEETINGS.
(a) Agency members serve at the designating official's pleasure.
Appointed members serve staggered four-year terms.
(b) The commissioner or designee presides.
The council meets at least quarterly.
(c) Members are unpaid but may receive travel reimbursement under the General Appropriations Act.
(d) Chapter 2110, Government Code, applies, except Section 2110.008 does not apply.
Sec. 55.153.
DUTIES AND LIMITATIONS.
(a) The council advises on data standards, research priorities, scientific and privacy standards, public reporting, prevention recommendations, five-year reviews, and legislative recommendations.
(b) The council may not administer the program, direct a finding, decide a claim, receive the linkage key, or have unrestricted identifiable-data access.
SUBCHAPTER E.
PRIVACY, ACCESS, AND PUBLICATION
Sec. 55.201.
CONFIDENTIALITY AND SECURITY.
(a) Identifiable or reasonably identifiable information maintained under this chapter is confidential and excepted from Chapter 552, Government Code.
(b) The department shall separate the linkage key; use encryption, role-based access, multifactor authentication, audit logging, and minimum-necessary access; conduct assessments; establish retention and destruction schedules; maintain breach procedures; and comply with applicable law.
(c) Identifiable or coded research information may not be disclosed to an employer, prospective employer, licensing authority, insurer, carrier, or plan for employment, discipline, licensing, underwriting, or individual benefits unless specifically authorized by the individual, required by court order, or expressly required by law.
(d) Voluntary research information may not be used to rebut a Chapter 607 presumption unless the participant specifically authorizes that use in writing.
Sec. 55.202.
RESEARCHER ACCESS.
The department may grant minimum-necessary coded-data access through a secure research environment only for an approved use, with required institutional review, a data-use agreement prohibiting reidentification and unauthorized contact or disclosure, audit logging, and disclosure-risk review.
Sec. 55.203.
PUBLIC WEBSITE AND APPLICATION PROGRAMMING INTERFACE.
(a) The department shall maintain a public website using existing state open-data infrastructure to the greatest extent practicable.
(b) The department shall publish dashboards; downloadable CSV and JSON data; a documented, versioned API; dictionaries and codebooks; protocols, methods, quality statements, limitations, suppression rules, and revisions; annual and five-year reports; valid privacy-safe trends; publications; and prevention guidance.
(c) Public information must be de-identified and aggregated when necessary using appropriate disclosure controls.
(d) The public system may not publish the linkage key, a stable person identifier, direct identifiers, precise histories creating unreasonable identification risk, or another high-risk field.
(e) Non-sensitive operational information must be refreshed at least quarterly and validated health outcomes at least annually.
SUBCHAPTER F.
PREVENTION, REPORTS, AND FUNDING
Sec. 55.251.
PREVENTION AND IMPLEMENTATION CYCLE.
For a potentially preventable concern, the department shall validate the pattern; identify modifiable practices; support a pilot; evaluate adoption, exposure reduction, health, feasibility, capability, and unintended effects; publish results; issue guidance; recommend standards, appropriations, procurement, training, or legislation when warranted; and continue surveillance.
Sec. 55.252.
REPORTS.
(a) By December 1 annually, the department shall publish program status, trends, research, evidence gaps, prevention results, expenditures, guidance, and recommendations.
(b) By December 1 of each even-numbered year, the department shall report costs, performance, and conditions warranting legislative consideration of presumptive or other occupational protection to statewide elected leadership, the Legislative Budget Board, and appropriate committees.
(c) At least every five years, the department shall publish an independently reviewed comprehensive epidemiological, exposure, prevention, privacy, and data-quality review.
(d) Reports may not identify a first responder.
Sec. 55.253.
FUNDING.
(a) The program is funded from money appropriated to the department.
(b) The department shall include staffing, secure linkage, technology, public data, consortium, research, prevention pilots, and local assistance in its legislative appropriations request.
(c) The department may accept federal funds, grants, gifts, interagency transfers, and university cost sharing as supplements, not substitutes, for core appropriations.
(d) The department may not accept a condition allowing a funder to control methods, suppress or materially alter a finding, or unreasonably delay publication.
Sec. 55.254.
RULES.
The executive commissioner shall adopt rules governing data elements and formats; reporting; consent and return of results; privacy and researcher access; grants and contracts; council procedures; and phased implementation.
ARTICLE 4.
TRANSITION AND IMPLEMENTATION
SECTION 4.01.
APPLICABILITY.
The changes made by Article 2 apply to claims filed on or after the effective date and pending claims without a final judgment.
They do not affect a final judgment rendered before that date.
SECTION 4.02.
REPROCESSING CERTAIN CANCER CLAIMS.
(a) A claimant whose claim was denied before the effective date wholly or partly because the cancer was not then listed in Section 607.055(b), Government Code, may, not later than September 1, 2028, submit a written reprocessing request with existing or supplemental evidence.
(b) The carrier shall reprocess the claim under amended law and, within 60 days, issue written acceptance or a denial stating every basis and the dispute procedure.
(c) A timely request is not barred by another claim-filing or notice deadline, but the claimant must otherwise establish eligibility.
(d) This section does not reopen a final court judgment.
(e) The commissioner of workers' compensation shall adopt necessary rules and forms.
SECTION 4.03.
INITIAL PROGRAM IMPLEMENTATION.
(a) Initial rules and council designations and appointments are due March 1, 2028.
(b) DSHS shall begin roster reporting by September 1, 2028; achieve statewide exposure and prevention reporting by September 1, 2029; establish the public website and API by September 1, 2029; and publish the first annual report by December 1, 2029.
(c) Implementation shall prioritize existing systems, privacy, assistance to small and volunteer entities, and reliable denominator data before disease-rate comparisons.
SECTION 4.04.
RULES FOR CANCER CLAIMS.
The commissioner of workers' compensation shall adopt necessary rules and forms by March 1, 2028.
ARTICLE 5.
EFFECTIVE DATE
SECTION 5.01.
This Act takes effect September 1, 2027.
