Code and Code of Federal Regulations Peer-reviewed research (see References) Executive Summary What the bill does, why it matters, and what it is not What the bill does.
The Texas High School Equivalency and Early College Access bill lets any Texas resident age 13 or older who is ready to graduate take the state's high school equivalency exam.
A student who passes earns the Texas Certificate of High School Equivalency, completes high school, and chooses what comes next: a career, college, or both.
The goal is education freedom.
Students who are ready should not be held in high school only to satisfy a calendar or district graduation requirements, and families should have another path besides four more years of seat time.
Here is what the bill guarantees: Free preparation and testing.
Preparation, testing, retesting, and the certificate itself are free.
Prep is offered in person and online, and every district provides testing access every week.
The same standard, without the barriers.
The bill does not change what a student must know to pass.
It removes the barriers around the exam: age limits, fees, and dependence on a single testing company.
Choice of exam provider.
School districts, charter schools, education service centers, and public colleges may choose any qualifying exam provider or build their own, as long as it meets the state's approval standards.
No single company controls access.
Every provider must test students 13 and older.
An exam or provider that refuses cannot be approved.
Parents are informed.
Parents are notified whenever their student registers for a test.
Notice is informational and does not require their consent.
Earning the certificate is completing school, not dropping out.
A student who earns the certificate has completed secondary education.
Districts may not record that student as a dropout.
Fair credit for districts.
A district counts the student as a graduate in state ratings when the student completed the district's own exam prep course.
A student who prepared another way is treated like a student who transferred to another school, so the district is neither credited nor penalized.
Districts may not pressure students into district classes to collect the credit.
Every door stays open.
For students who choose college, public colleges and universities must treat the certificate the same as a diploma for admission and financial aid.
Public institutions must also build online pathways so students anywhere in Texas can earn a certificate or degree remotely, including while working.
Definition Texas Certificate of High School Equivalency.
The state credential issued to a person who passes every subject of a state-approved high school equivalency exam.
Today it is governed by Section 7.111 of the Texas Education Code, which this bill rewrites (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 7.111) .
Why it matters.
Texas already accepts the principle.
Texas law already lets a student graduate early, at any age, once the student shows mastery in every subject (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 28.0253) .
A completed credential opens career doors.
In 2024, workers age 25 and older with a high school diploma had median weekly earnings of $930 and an unemployment rate of 4.2%.
Workers without one earned $738 and had a 6.2% unemployment rate (U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2025) .
In federal labor statistics, the high school graduate category includes people with a diploma or its equivalent (BLS, 2026a) .
Ready students who move ahead do better.
They outperform same-age peers and keep pace with the older students they join (Steenbergen-Hu et al., 2016) , without long-term harm to their well-being (Bernstein et al., 2021) .
For students who choose college, an early start helps.
Students admitted to early college by lottery earned degrees at higher rates and sooner than students who were not (Song et al., 2024) .
What it is not.
This is not a dropout program, and it is not a college mandate.
It serves students who are still in school, have done the work, and are ready to graduate.
Research on adults who left school and later took a test does not describe these students.

1 Why This Bill Texas already accepts early, mastery-based graduation; the equivalency route still carries old barriers Texas already agrees that mastery, not seat time, should decide when a student is ready Texas has already decided, in law, that a student who has mastered the material should not have to sit through years of school just to satisfy a calendar.
Texas First already allows early graduation at any age.
Under the Texas First Early High School Completion Program, a district must let a student graduate and receive a diploma once the student demonstrates mastery and early readiness for college in every subject, "notwithstanding any other local or state requirements" (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 28.0253(e)) .
The statute sets no minimum age.
The Legislature made issuing that diploma mandatory in 2023 (TEA, n.d.-c) .
The state already accepts high school equivalency results, at the 80th percentile, as one way to qualify for the Texas First Scholarship (THECB, n.d.) .
State academies already let students leave high school early for a university campus.
Students enrolled in the Texas Academy of Mathematics and Science and three similar state academies are exempt from compulsory attendance at their home high school (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 25.086(a)(8)–(11)) .
Many high schoolers already do college work.
In fall 2025, 276,064 high school students were taking dual credit college courses, which is 16.5% of all students enrolled in Texas higher education.
In fall 1999 the number was 11,921 (THECB, 2026a) .
Some leave high school for college already.
In the 2023–24 school year, the state recorded 281 students who left high school to enroll full time in college.
They are counted as students who left for another purpose, not as dropouts (TEA, 2025b) .
“A school district or open-enrollment charter school shall allow a student to graduate and receive a high school diploma under the program if . . . the student demonstrates mastery of and early readiness for college . . . notwithstanding any other local or state requirements.” — Texas Education Code § 28.0253(e), Texas First Early High School Completion Program What stands in the way today The high school equivalency certificate is the most direct way for a student to show high school mastery and move on.
The rules around it were written for a different purpose: Age limits.
A person generally must be over 17 to test.
Sixteen-year-olds may test only in narrow circumstances, such as Job Corps enrollment or a recommendation from a court-supervising agency (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 7.111(a-1)) ; (TEA, n.d.-a) .
One provider, with its own age limit.
The GED test is currently the only exam listed by the state.
Its provider states that no one under 16 can take the test (GED Testing Service, n.d.) .
The state's other exam, HiSET, ended in Texas in August 2024 (TEA, 2025a) .
Cost.
Testing costs $145 for all four subjects in person, or $165 online (TEA, n.d.-b) .
Graduation-rate accounting.
In the state's own graduation rate calculations, a student who earns the certificate counts as a non-graduate.
That student is included in a district's student count but never as a graduate (TEA, 2025b) .
A district that helps a student finish early takes a hit for it.
How many Texans use this credential now The route is already used widely, mostly by young Texans.

Any Texas resident age 13 or older without a high school diploma may take an approved exam.
No permission slips.
Eligibility cannot depend on a school, court, or agency signing off, or on a student withdrawing from school.
Retesting.
A student may retake any subject not yet passed once a week.
Passed subjects stay valid.
Each passed subject counts for a full year while the student works on the rest.
Fast certificate.
Once every subject is passed, the state must issue the certificate within three business days, and if the state is late, the student's score records serve as proof of the credential.
Keeps today's standard The bill does not raise or lower what a student must know to earn the certificate.
The State Board of Education continues to set objective, published standards for approving exams and for passing scores.
What changes is who may test, what it costs, and how many providers may offer the exam.
Opens the market to any qualifying exam provider Local choice.
School districts, charter schools, education service centers, and public colleges and universities may choose any approved exam and provider, or develop their own.
One standard for everyone.
A locally developed exam must pass the same State Board of Education approval process as any other.
No favored vendor.
The state may not require a single or favored vendor.
Every provider tests students 13 and up.
An exam or provider that turns away eligible students is not approved, or loses its approval.
Keeps parents informed When a student under 18 registers for a subject test, whoever registers the student must notify the parent or guardian of the subject, date, time, and location.
The notice informs; it does not create a consent requirement, and a missed notice does not cancel a test or invalidate a score.
Families and districts decide everything else together.
Treats completion as completion The certificate is completion.
A student who earns the certificate has completed secondary education, and districts may not report that student as a dropout.
Choices after earning it.
The student's duty to attend school ends on the date the certificate takes effect.
The student may stay enrolled voluntarily through the end of that school year.
Disability rights continue.
Federal rights for students with disabilities, including the right to a free appropriate public education, are preserved (34 C.F.R. § 300.102) .
Gives districts fair credit Credit for district-prepared students.
If a student earns the certificate while enrolled and successfully completed the district's own preparation course, the district counts that student as a graduate in state accountability ratings.
Completion is defined by state rule.
No penalty otherwise.
If the student prepared another way, the student is treated the same as a student who withdrew to enroll in another school.
The student is not counted as a graduate, a non-graduate, or a dropout, and the certificate cannot lower any district or campus rating.
No pressure on students.
Districts may not require or pressure students into district classes to capture the credit; preparation stays voluntary.
Federal rate is separate.
Federal law requires its own graduation-rate calculation for federal reports, and the bill keeps that separate (20 U.S.C. § 7801(25)) .
Keeps every door open: career, college, or both Equal treatment for students who choose college.
Public colleges and universities must treat the certificate the same as a diploma for admission, enrollment, scholarships, and financial aid.
They may not add extra courses or hurdles because of the credential or the student's age.
They may still apply the same neutral academic standards they apply to diploma holders, and they may require parental consent before a minor signs contracts or takes on costs.
Federal aid already recognizes the credential.
Federal student aid rules already treat a state certificate earned by passing a state-authorized exam as the equivalent of a high school diploma (34 C.F.R. § 600.2) .
Online pathways.
Public institutions must phase in fully online pathways for every certificate and degree program between the 2027–28 and 2030–31 academic years.
In-person requirements are allowed only for components that truly need them, such as clinical work or lab equipment.
Remote advising, tutoring, disability services, and financial aid counseling are required.
Pays for it without charging students Paid through school funding.
Public school costs are paid through the Foundation School Program, using an activity-based schedule.
It pays for verified preparation, completed tests, and certificates, but not for simply opening an account.
Not tied to pass rates.
Payment cannot depend entirely on a student passing, so providers have no reason to discourage testing or retesting.
Audits and reports.
Claims are matched against score records, audited, and reported publicly every year.

The strongest available research says yes.
Academic gains.
A review of a century of research found that accelerated students outperformed similar same-age students by a large margin (g = 0.70).
They did about as well as the older students they joined (g = 0.09), which means they kept up after moving ahead (Steenbergen-Hu et al., 2016) .
A separate review of 38 studies found a positive academic effect (g = 0.180) (Steenbergen-Hu & Moon, 2011) .
Social and emotional effects.
The common worry is that a young student who moves ahead will struggle socially.
The same 2011 review found a slightly positive social-emotional effect (g = 0.076) (Steenbergen-Hu & Moon, 2011) .
A study that followed more than 1,600 talented students identified before age 13 for over 35 years found that the amount of acceleration had no link to adult psychological well-being.
Participants' well-being was above national averages (Bernstein et al., 2021) .
Long-term achievement.
In a 40-year study, researchers compared 363 students who skipped a grade with 657 closely matched students who did not.
The grade skippers were more likely to earn advanced degrees and earned them sooner (Park et al., 2013) .
For students who choose college, an early start raises degree completion.
The most direct evidence comes from early college high schools that admitted students by lottery, which creates a fair comparison between winners and non-winners.

5 Completing Early Is Not Dropping Out Why research on adults who left school does not describe students who finish early Critics of high school equivalency often point to research on the GED, the best-known equivalency exam.
That research deserves a direct answer.
What that research studied The best-known studies examine people who left school and later used the GED as a second-chance credential.
Many of those people never went on to further education.
Earnings and outcomes.
A major review concluded that, on average, the GED had "minimal value" in the labor market for those recipients and that few went on to complete college (Heckman et al., 2010) .
Health.
A national health study found GED holders' health was comparable to dropouts' and worse than diploma holders' (Zajacova & Everett, 2014) .
Mixed earnings effect.
Another study found that earning the GED raised later earnings by #% to #% for young white dropouts near the passing line, with no significant gain for nonwhite dropouts (Tyler et al., 1998) .
Why those results do not describe this bill's students This bill serves a different group.
Applying conclusions about people who left school without a credential to students who stay in school, do the work, and pass the exam is a category error.
Those are different people, on different paths.
The bill creates only one way out, and it is a completed credential.
Still in school.
A student's attendance duty continues until the certificate is earned.
No one stops attending school to "go take the test," and a student who has not passed every subject stays in school.
Same standard.
The student must pass the same state-approved exam, under the same standards, that Texans take today.
Nothing is made easier.
A completion, not an exit.
The certificate is earned while enrolled and is recorded as completing secondary education.
The student then decides whether to work, go to college, or both.
The dropout-incentive concern is about leaving without finishing.
One frequently cited study found that easier GED access led some students to drop out (Heckman et al., 2012) .
In that setting, a student could leave school first and test later, or never test at all.
Under this bill a student cannot leave school before completing, so the bill offers no route to leaving without a credential.
Two states took different paths.
In 1988, Wisconsin raised its minimum age for the GED to 18½, citing concern about teenagers using the test as a shortcut (Quinn & Pawasarat, 2011) .
California went the other direction.
Since 1974, students age 16, or those who have completed one year of 10th grade, have been able to pass a state proficiency exam that is "the legal equivalent to a high school diploma" and leave school with a parent's permission (California Department of Education [CDE], n.d.-a) ; (CDE, n.d.-b) .
This bill keeps students in school until they finish, informs parents at every test, and leaves the next step to the student and family.
The bottom line A student who earns the certificate under this bill has done the work and proved it.
That student has completed high school early.
6 Careers, College, or Both A completed credential opens career doors; college remains an option, including online College is not for everyone, and the bill does not assume it is.
Many students are ready to start a trade, an apprenticeship, a family business, military service, or a job, and then build on it.
The certificate gives them a completed high school credential to do that years sooner.
In federal labor statistics, people with a high school diploma or its equivalent are counted as high school graduates (BLS, 2026a) .
The same federal data also note that education categories do not capture apprenticeships and other on-the-job training, which also shape earnings (BLS, 2026b) .
A completed credential matters in the job market.
In 2024, median weekly earnings were higher, and unemployment lower, at each step of education (BLS, 2025) .
These are national associations, not proof that a credential alone causes higher pay.

Online learning can work, with support.
A U.S.
Department of Education review of 45 studies found that purely online instruction performed about the same as face-to-face instruction on average.
Blended instruction performed better (U.S.
Department of Education, 2010) .
A study of more than 40,000 community college students found that younger students were among those who struggled most in online courses (Xu & Jaggars, 2014) .
That is why the bill requires remote advising, tutoring, faculty office hours, and disability services for online students on terms equal to in-person students.
Statewide online access is still limited.
In 2019, 1,265 of the 8,937 programs in the state inventory were offered fully online (THECB, 2019) .
That is the most recent statewide count available.
The bill would require a current inventory from every institution and a public online catalog.
7 Funding, Accountability, and Local Control Local choice under one standard, payment for verified services, and fair district credit Local choice, one standard.
Under this bill: The state sets one approval standard and keeps a nonexclusive roster of approved providers.
Each district, charter school, service center, college, and university chooses how to deliver services, using ordinary procurement and conflict-of-interest rules.
No sole-source contracts are authorized.
Paying for what is delivered.
Payments follow verified activity: preparation milestones, completed tests, accommodations, and certificates.
Reporting and audits.
Providers report monthly and districts verify services.
The state matches claims to score records and recovers overpayments, and the state auditor may audit any claim.
What it would cost.
Today's testing fee is $145 per student for all four subjects in person (TEA, n.d.-b) .
The total cost depends on how many students take part, which no one can know in advance, so this article does not estimate it.
The bill requires the state's education and higher education agencies to project volume and costs in each budget request.
Fair accountability.
The problem today.
The state counts certificate earners as non-graduates when it calculates graduation rates (TEA, 2025b) .
The fix.
The bill gives graduate credit to a district when the student completed the district's own preparation course.
When the student prepared another way, the bill treats the student like one who transferred to another school, with no credit and no penalty.
It builds on a model Texas already uses to exclude certain students from graduation rates (Tex.
Educ.
Code § 39.053(g)) .
The federal rate.

HIGH SCHOOL EQUIVALENCY EXAMINATION AND CERTIFICATE SECTION 1.01.
Section 7.111, Education Code, is amended to read as follows: Sec.
7.111.
HIGH SCHOOL EQUIVALENCY EXAMINATION AND CERTIFICATE. (a) The State Board of Education shall provide for the administration of one or more approved high school equivalency examinations and for the issuance by the agency of the Texas Certificate of High School Equivalency. (b) In this section: (1) "Agency" means the Texas Education Agency.
(2) "Approved examination" means a provider-neutral examination approved by the board to determine whether an examinee has demonstrated academic knowledge and skills equivalent to those expected of a high school graduate in this state.
(3) "Board" means the State Board of Education.
(4) "Business day" means a day other than a Saturday, Sunday, or state holiday.
(5) "Certificate" means the Texas Certificate of High School Equivalency.
(6) "Examination provider" means a person with which the agency contracts, that the agency approves, or that a school district, open-enrollment charter school, education service center, or institution of higher education selects or establishes under Subsection (e-1) to develop, score, administer, or provide an approved examination.
(7) "Qualifying score" means the objectively determined minimum passing score established and published for a subject of an approved examination.
(8) "Subject examination" means the separately scored portion of an approved examination that measures one required subject or competency area.
(9) "Texas resident" means an individual who resides in this state, without regard to the duration of the individual's residence. (c) A Texas resident is entitled to take an approved examination if the resident: (1) is at least 13 years of age on the date of the examination; (2) has not received a high school diploma; and (3) satisfies reasonable identity and examination-security requirements adopted under this section. (d) Eligibility under Subsection (c) may not be conditioned on: (1) the consent of a parent, guardian, school district, open-enrollment charter school, court, public agency, or other person; (2) withdrawal, nonenrollment, or termination of enrollment in a public, private, parochial, or home school or any other education program; (3) a recommendation or order of a court or public agency; (4) completion of a grade level, course, minimum number of instructional hours, preparation program, practice examination, or readiness assessment; (5) the examinee's reason for taking the examination or intended education or employment path; or (6) any condition not expressly authorized by this section. (e) The board shall adopt objective, publicly available standards for approval and passage of an examination.
The board shall maintain a nonexclusive approval process that: (1) permits approval of more than one examination provider; (2) uses published academic, security, accessibility, reliability, and data-interoperability criteria; (3) does not confer an exclusive statutory preference on a privately owned examination or trademark; and (4) permits the agency to develop, procure, or approve a replacement examination if necessary to preserve continuous statewide access. (e-1) A school district, open-enrollment charter school, education service center, or institution of higher education may select any approved examination and any examination provider approved under this section, or may develop or administer its own examination.
An examination developed by an entity described by this subsection may be used to qualify an examinee for the certificate only after the board approves the examination under the standards adopted under Subsection (e), which apply on the same terms to every examination regardless of who develops it.
The state may not require a school district, open-enrollment charter school, education service center, or institution of higher education to use a single or favored examination or examination provider. (e-2) Each examination provider, and each entity described by Subsection (e-1) that develops, administers, or provides an approved examination, shall accept for examination every person who is eligible under Subsection (c), including a person who is at least 13 years of age, and may not impose a minimum age above 13 years of age or a condition prohibited by Subsection (d).
The board may not approve, and shall revoke the approval of, an examination or examination provider that does not comply with this subsection. (f) The board by rule shall provide reasonable procedures for verifying identity, protecting examination security, preventing fraud, and providing accommodations required by state or federal law.
A procedure adopted under this subsection may not have the purpose or effect of materially burdening a right established by this section. (g) An eligible examinee may take an unpassed subject examination once during each consecutive seven-day period.
An examination provider, testing center, or governmental entity may not: (1) impose a numerical limit on attempts to pass an unpassed subject examination; (2) require a waiting period longer than the period prescribed by this subsection; or (3) refuse an otherwise available testing appointment because the examinee remains enrolled in an education program. (g-1) If an examinee is younger than 18 years of age and the examinee's disabilities of minority have not been removed, the examination provider, testing center, school district, open-enrollment charter school, or other entity that registers the examinee for a subject examination shall provide written or electronic notice to the examinee's parent, guardian, or other person having lawful control of the examinee that the examinee is registered to take the subject examination, including the subject and the date, time, and location of the examination.
The notice must be provided at the time of registration or as soon as practicable after registration and, if registration occurs at least one business day before the examination, before the examination begins.
Notice under this subsection is informational only.
This subsection does not authorize a consent requirement prohibited by Subsection (d)(1), and a failure to provide notice does not invalidate an examination, score, or certificate or delay or cancel a testing appointment. (h) A qualifying score on a subject examination: (1) remains valid until the first anniversary of the date on which the score was earned; (2) may not be repeated while the score remains valid; and (3) expires only if the examinee has not earned valid qualifying scores on all required subject examinations before that anniversary. (i) An examinee qualifies for the certificate when the examinee concurrently holds a valid qualifying score on every required subject examination.
Expiration of an underlying score after the certificate is earned does not affect the validity of the certificate. (j) The agency shall maintain an electronic account through which an examinee may view: (1) each score and the date on which the score was earned; (2) the expiration date of each qualifying score; (3) a diagnostic report identifying tested competencies and areas in which further preparation may be useful; and (4) the examinee's certificate, transcript, and official verification after qualification. (k) The agency shall provide notice to an examinee not later than the 90th, 60th, 30th, and seventh day before a qualifying score expires.
A change in examination provider, examination design, agency contract, or agency rule may not shorten the validity period of a qualifying score. (l) An examination provider shall transmit a complete and authenticated score record to the agency not later than the next business day after the provider scores the examination.
The provider shall simultaneously make the score record available to the examinee. (m) Issuance of the certificate is a ministerial duty.
Not later than the third business day after the agency receives the final qualifying score required by Subsection (i), the agency shall issue electronically and without charge: (1) the certificate; (2) an official transcript of examination scores; and (3) an official electronic verification of the certificate. (n) The legal effective date of a certificate is the date the agency receives the final qualifying score required by Subsection (i).
If the agency does not issue the certificate within the period required by Subsection (m), the authenticated score records showing that the examinee has satisfied Subsection (i), together with an automatically generated agency verification of receipt, constitute the certificate by operation of law until the agency performs its duty under Subsection (m).
A person or governmental entity may not deny the credential's legal effect because the agency failed to issue the formal document on time. (o) The agency, board, examination provider, or testing center may not delay or deny a certificate based on the examinee's: (1) age, if the examinee was eligible under Subsection (c) when tested; (2) continued enrollment in an education program; (3) reason for taking the examination; (4) intended withdrawal from or continuation in an education program; or (5) intended college attendance or other postsecondary plan. (p) An examinee must receive, without a means test or charge: (1) preparation services and required preparation materials provided under Subchapter C-2, Chapter 29; (2) each examination and retest authorized by this section; (3) required accommodations; (4) each score and diagnostic report; (5) the initial electronic certificate, transcript, and ordinary electronic verification; and (6) electronic replacement copies of the certificate and transcript. (q) The agency may charge only the actual production and mailing cost of an optional physical replacement document requested after the initial certificate is issued.
Inability or refusal to pay for an optional physical copy does not affect the validity or electronic availability of the certificate. (r) An examinee may submit electronically to the agency a notice alleging an error or failure relating to score transmission, score validity, qualification, or certificate issuance.
The agency shall: (1) acknowledge the notice not later than the next business day; and (2) correct the error or issue a written decision supported by the record not later than the second business day after acknowledgment. (s) After expiration of the period prescribed by Subsection (r), the examinee, the examinee's parent or next friend, or an attorney acting for the examinee may bring an action for declaratory or injunctive relief in a district court in Travis County or the county in which the examinee resides.
The court shall give the action priority over other civil matters that are not entitled to priority by law.
An authenticated score record is prima facie evidence of the score shown. (t) Sovereign and governmental immunity are waived and abolished to the extent of liability for prospective declaratory or injunctive relief, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees in an action under Subsection (s).
A claimant is not required to exhaust another administrative remedy after complying with Subsection (r). (u) The agency and board shall adopt rules necessary to administer this section.
A rule, contract, provider term, local policy, or institutional policy that conflicts with this section is void to the extent of the conflict.
ARTICLE 2.
PUBLIC SCHOOL PREPARATION, TESTING ACCESS, AND ACCOUNTABILITY SECTION 2.01.
Chapter 29, Education Code, is amended by adding Subchapter C-2 to read as follows: SUBCHAPTER C-2.
HIGH SCHOOL EQUIVALENCY PREPARATION AND TESTING ACCESS Sec.
29.111.
DEFINITIONS.
In this subchapter: (1) "Approved provider" means a public or private entity approved under Section 29.118 to provide preparation, testing, or related services.
(2) "Fully online" means accessible by a student through the Internet without requiring the student to travel to a particular physical location.
(3) "Preparation services" means instruction, tutoring, instructional materials, practice opportunities, diagnostic services, and live academic assistance designed to prepare a student for an approved high school equivalency examination under Section 7.111.
(4) "Testing center" means a location approved by the agency at which an approved examination is administered under secure and accessible conditions.
Sec.
29.112.
APPLICABILITY.
This subchapter applies to: (1) a school district or open-enrollment charter school that serves students in any grade from grade 6 through grade 12; and (2) an education service center that provides a service under this subchapter.
Sec.
29.113.
PREPARATION SERVICES REQUIRED. (a) Each school district and open-enrollment charter school to which this subchapter applies shall provide or contract for preparation services that: (1) cover every subject and competency tested by each approved examination reasonably available to students served by the district or school; (2) are available both in person and fully online; (3) include all required instructional materials and reasonable practice opportunities; (4) include diagnostic feedback and access to live assistance from a qualified instructor or tutor; (5) comply with applicable state and federal disability and accessibility requirements; and (6) are offered without charge or means testing. (b) Fully online preparation services must be available to any Texas resident who is enrolled in grade 6 or a higher grade or is otherwise eligible to take an examination under Section 7.111. (c) A district or school may not require a person seeking preparation services to: (1) withdraw from or, if the person is eligible to take the examination under Section 7.111, enroll in the district or school; (2) state an intention to leave school; or (3) agree to take the examination or leave an education program after receiving preparation. (d) Participation in preparation services is voluntary and may not be made a condition of examination eligibility.
Sec.
29.114.
WEEKLY TESTING ACCESS. (a) Each school district shall ensure that an eligible resident of the district and each student attending a campus in the district has access within the district to: (1) at least one approved testing center; and (2) at least one opportunity to take each available subject examination during each consecutive seven-day period. (b) An open-enrollment charter school shall ensure that each student attending the school has access to the testing opportunities required by Subsection (a) at a campus, administrative facility, or testing center reasonably accessible to the student. (c) A district or school may satisfy this section by: (1) operating a testing center; (2) contracting with an approved provider; (3) entering into a written joint arrangement with another district, school, education service center, or institution of higher education; or (4) using another facility controlled by the district or school. (d) A joint arrangement is permitted only if it guarantees weekly capacity, accessibility, and service without cost to the examinee.
A district or school may not require a testing center in every building. (e) A district or school shall use existing facilities and space to the extent reasonably practicable before acquiring or constructing additional space.
Sec.
29.115.
PROTECTED ABSENCE AND RELEASE TIME. (a) A student registered for a subject examination is entitled to the time reasonably necessary to travel to, take, and return from the examination. (b) If the examination schedule or necessary travel makes attendance impracticable, the student is entitled to an excused absence for the entire school day. (c) A district or school may require the student to complete missed work under the same rules applied to other excused absences but may not impose loss of course credit, disciplinary action, a truancy referral, loss of eligibility, or another penalty because of the absence.
Sec.
29.116.
CONTINUED ENROLLMENT AFTER CERTIFICATE. (a) A student's duty to attend school ends on the legal effective date of the student's certificate under Section 7.111. (b) A certificate holder who was enrolled in a school district or open-enrollment charter school on the certificate's legal effective date may, but is not required to, remain enrolled through the end of the school year in which the certificate became effective if the holder satisfies the ordinary eligibility and conduct requirements that apply to other voluntarily enrolled students. (c) Voluntary continued enrollment under this section does not renew or create a compulsory attendance duty.
Enrollment after the school year described by Subsection (b) is governed by otherwise applicable law. (d) Nothing in this section limits a right or service guaranteed to a student with a disability by federal law, including the right to a free appropriate public education if applicable under federal law.
Sec.
29.117.
STUDENT RECORDS. (a) A student who earns a certificate under Section 7.111 has completed the student's secondary education.
The agency shall establish a completion code for a student who earns a certificate under Section 7.111. (b) A district or school may not report a student who earns a certificate under Section 7.111 as a dropout.
Sec.
29.118.
APPROVED PROVIDERS AND LOCAL SELECTION. (a) The agency shall maintain a nonexclusive roster of approved nonprofit and for-profit preparation and testing providers. (b) Approval standards must be objective, published, competitively neutral, and reasonably related to instructional quality, examination security, accessibility, data reporting, financial integrity, and student protection. (c) The agency shall: (1) accept applications for approval at least twice each year; (2) publish each approved provider's ownership, services, geographic coverage, and maximum approved rates; (3) prohibit an undisclosed conflict of interest or preferential treatment for a provider; and (4) provide notice and an opportunity for review before denying, suspending, or revoking approval. (d) A district, charter school, or education service center may provide services directly or select one or more approved providers under the entity's ordinary procurement and conflict-of-interest requirements.
This subchapter does not authorize a sole-source contract or an exemption from otherwise applicable competitive procurement law. (e) A provider selected locally under this section may submit a claim for direct state payment only after the selecting entity verifies the services as required by Section 48.318.
Sec.
29.119.
ACCESS MAY NOT DEPEND ON APPROPRIATION OR PROVIDER CONTRACT.
A district or school remains responsible for providing the access required by this subchapter regardless of whether the district or school provides the service directly or through a contractor.
A contract may not restrict a student's statutory right because a payment rate, contract allocation, or provider quota has been reached.

(i) The agency shall provide an administrative review process for a disputed payment, recovery, suspension, or provider-approval decision. (j) Student-level information may be used only for administration, payment, audit, authorized evaluation, and another purpose permitted by state or federal law.

(k) Not later than December 1 of each year, the agency shall publish a report showing by provider type, region, county, district, and age band, as statistically appropriate: (1) preparation participation and modality; (2) examinations, attempts, passage, and certificate completion; (3) average certificate-issuance time; (4) expenditures by payment category; (5) testing-center availability and utilization; and (6) other outcome measures adopted by rule. (l) The allotment under this section is part of the Foundation School Program.

51.996.

ARTICLE 6.
ONLINE HIGHER EDUCATION ACCESS SECTION 6.01.
Chapter 61, Education Code, is amended by adding Subchapter SS to read as follows: SUBCHAPTER SS.
ONLINE COURSE AND PROGRAM ACCESS Sec.
61.1001.
DEFINITIONS.
In this subchapter: (1) "Fully online course" means a course for which all instruction, interaction, submission of work, examinations, orientation, and ordinarily required academic activity may be completed remotely through the Internet without travel to a particular physical location, except for an approved physical-presence component under Section 61.1004.
(2) "Fully online program pathway" means a published sequence through which a student may complete every course, credit, examination, orientation, advising requirement, and other academic condition for a certificate or degree remotely, except for an approved physical-presence component under Section 61.1004.
(3) "Institution" means an institution of higher education, as defined by Section 61.003, that offers an academic or technical course or certificate or degree program.
(4) "Physical-presence component" means a specifically identified portion of a course or program that objectively requires physical performance or in-person supervision and cannot reasonably be replaced by remote instruction, simulation, remote equipment, or an approved local placement.
Sec.
61.1002.
ONLINE AVAILABILITY REQUIRED. (a) Each institution shall offer as a fully online course every course for which no physical-presence component is approved under Section 61.1004. (b) Each institution shall provide at least one fully online program pathway for each certificate and degree program the institution offers, except for the approved physical-presence components of the program. (c) The institution shall structure course scheduling, prerequisites, and frequency so that a student may make timely progress and complete the online pathway within the period ordinarily available to an otherwise similarly situated in-person student. (d) An institution may participate in a consortium, cross-registration arrangement, or shared-course agreement to satisfy this section if: (1) the course applies to the student's program without loss of credit; (2) the arrangement does not increase the student's tuition or mandatory fees; (3) the course is reasonably available when needed for timely progress; and (4) the institution remains responsible for compliance.
Sec.
61.1003.
REMOTE STUDENT SERVICES.
An institution shall make registration, academic advising, tutoring, faculty office hours, library resources, disability services, financial-aid counseling, payment services, technical support, career services, and complaint procedures remotely accessible to a student in a fully online course or pathway on terms reasonably equivalent to those available to an in-person student.
Sec.
61.1004.
PHYSICAL-PRESENCE COMPONENT. (a) An institution may require physical presence only for the portion of a course or program that the institution proves by clear and specific evidence: (1) requires direct patient or client care, clinical education, student teaching, supervised professional practice, fieldwork, physical performance, studio or shop work, or use of specialized equipment; (2) cannot reasonably be completed through remote instruction, a simulation of adequate educational quality, remotely operated equipment, or an approved placement near the student's residence; or (3) is expressly required in person by state or federal law or by a recognized professional licensing or accrediting body. (b) An exception must be limited to the necessary component and may not be applied automatically to an entire course, program, department, or institution. (c) Before requiring travel to the institution, the institution shall determine whether the component can be completed through a qualified local placement, partner facility, or supervised arrangement reasonably accessible to the student. (d) An institution shall publish for each exception: (1) the course and component affected; (2) the educational, legal, licensing, or accreditation basis; (3) the evidence showing why a remote alternative is not reasonably sufficient; (4) the expected location, duration, and cost to the student; and (5) the date on which the institution will review the continued necessity of the exception.
Sec.
61.1005.
INSTITUTIONAL INVENTORY AND IMPLEMENTATION PLAN. (a) Each institution shall submit to the coordinating board an inventory of every course and certificate and degree program the institution offers.
The inventory must identify: (1) current modality; (2) existing online availability; (3) each claimed physical-presence component; (4) the institution's evidence supporting each claimed component; (5) the action, resources, and date necessary to provide required online access; and (6) the institutional officer responsible for implementation. (b) The coordinating board shall approve, conditionally approve, or return an implementation plan for correction.
A plan may not waive a statutory deadline except as expressly authorized by Section 61.1007.
Sec.
61.1006.
IMPLEMENTATION SCHEDULE. (a) Not later than the 2027-2028 academic year, each institution shall: (1) complete and publish the inventory required by Section 61.1005; (2) identify all current online offerings and claimed physical-presence components; and (3) make the remote student services required by Section 61.1003 available. (b) Not later than the 2028-2029 academic year, each institution shall provide online availability for each feasible: (1) core curriculum course; (2) lower-division academic course; (3) general academic certificate; and (4) high-demand program pathway designated by the coordinating board. (c) Not later than the #-# academic year, each institution shall provide a complete online pathway for the nonexcepted components of each certificate and degree program or operate under a corrective order issued by the coordinating board. (d) Not later than the 2030-2031 academic year, each institution shall fully comply with this subchapter unless a time-limited extension is granted under Section 61.1007.
Sec.
61.1007.
LIMITED EXTENSION. (a) The coordinating board may grant one extension of not more than one academic year for a specifically identified course, component, or program if the institution proves that timely compliance is impracticable despite diligent efforts. (b) An extension must state: (1) the specific obstacle; (2) the actions already taken; (3) the funding and resources committed; (4) enforceable interim milestones; and (5) an expiration date. (c) The coordinating board may not grant an indefinite, institution-wide, or renewable extension.
Sec.
61.1008.
STATEWIDE ONLINE CATALOG.
The coordinating board shall maintain a searchable public catalog that identifies: (1) each fully online course and program pathway; (2) cross-registration or shared-course availability; (3) each approved physical-presence component and its stated basis; (4) each institution's implementation milestones and compliance status; and (5) information necessary for a student to apply, register, obtain support, and file a complaint.
Sec.
61.1009.
COMPLAINTS AND ENFORCEMENT. (a) A student may file without charge a complaint with an institution alleging a violation of this subchapter.
The institution shall issue a written decision not later than the 10th business day after receiving the complaint. (b) The student may appeal an adverse decision or failure to decide to the coordinating board.
The coordinating board shall issue a decision not later than the 20th business day after receiving the appeal. (c) The coordinating board may order an institution to: (1) provide remote access or a reasonable local alternative; (2) remove an unsupported physical-presence requirement; (3) enroll the student in an appropriate course or arrange cross-registration without additional tuition or mandatory fees; (4) adjust or refund tuition and fees caused by noncompliance; (5) adopt and comply with a corrective action plan; and (6) take other prospective action necessary to enforce this subchapter. (d) For knowing, repeated, or uncorrected noncompliance, the coordinating board may withhold discretionary administrative or program funds over which the board has allocation authority.
The board may not withhold student financial aid, formula funding attributable to students, or money whose withholding would materially impair a student's statutory access. (e) After the institutional decision period expires, a student may bring an action for declaratory or injunctive relief in a district court in Travis County or the county in which the institution is located.
Sovereign and governmental immunity are waived and abolished to the extent of liability for prospective declaratory or injunctive relief, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees.
Sec.
61.1010.
HIGHER EDUCATION HIGH SCHOOL EQUIVALENCY SERVICES. (a) Each institution shall ensure that a student attending the institution has practical access on the attended campus or through a no-cost written arrangement to: (1) an approved high school equivalency testing center; and (2) at least one opportunity during each consecutive seven-day period to take each available subject examination for which the student is eligible. EXISTING PASSING SCORES.
A passing subject score earned before the effective date of this Act remains valid for the longer of: (1) the period for which the score was valid under the law, rule, or provider policy in effect when earned; or (2) one year after the date the score was earned.

DATA SYSTEMS.
Not later than September 1, 2028, the Texas Education Agency and Texas Higher Education Coordinating Board shall establish the electronic accounts, secure data exchanges, claims systems, complaint procedures, and public reporting systems required by this Act.

SECTION 7.06.
FUNDING PLAN.
In preparing legislative appropriations requests for the state fiscal biennium beginning September 1, 2029, and each subsequent biennium, the Texas Education Agency and Texas Higher Education Coordinating Board shall separately identify: (1) projected student and examination volume; (2) preparation, testing, accommodation, certificate, data-system, and provider-payment costs; (3) public-school costs payable through the Foundation School Program; (4) higher-education costs payable from general revenue or another lawful higher-education appropriation; and (5) administrative costs and projected savings or offsets.
SECTION 7.07.
CONFLICT OF LAW.
A rule, contract, local policy, institutional policy, examination-provider term, or preparation-provider term in effect on or after the effective date of this Act is void to the extent the rule, contract, policy, or term conflicts with a right or duty established by this Act.
SECTION 7.08.
SEVERABILITY.
If any provision of this Act or its application to any person or circumstance is held invalid, the invalidity does not affect another provision or application of this Act that can be given effect without the invalid provision or application, and to this end the provisions of this Act are severable.
SECTION 7.09.
EFFECTIVE DATE.
This Act takes effect September 1, 2027.

Academic acceleration in gifted youth and fruitless concerns regarding psychological well-being: A 35-year longitudinal study.
Journal of Educational Psychology, 113 (4), 830–845. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000500 This peer-reviewed study followed more than 1,600 intellectually talented young people identified before age 13 for over 35 years, and checked its results against a second sample of graduate students.
It found that the amount of academic acceleration a student experienced had no meaningful relationship with adult psychological well-being, and that participants' well-being was above national averages.
The article uses it to answer the common concern that moving ahead harms young students socially or emotionally.

When less is more: Effects of grade skipping on adult STEM productivity among mathematically precocious adolescents.
Journal of Educational Psychology, 105 (1), 176–198. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029481 This peer-reviewed study tracked mathematically talented students for about 40 years and used matching methods to compare 363 students who skipped a grade with 657 similar students who did not.
Grade skippers were more likely to earn doctorates and other advanced degrees, earned them at younger ages, and produced more patents and other professional accomplishments by midlife.

Song, M., Neering, K., Zeiser, K.

(2024).
Accelerated postsecondary pathways: The longer-term impact of the early college high school model on postsecondary degree attainment.
American Educational Research Journal . https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312241276855 This peer-reviewed study compared 2,388 students who applied to early college high schools that admitted students by lottery.
Because chance decided who was offered a seat, winners and non-winners were alike in every other way, which makes this an unusually fair test of what early college access does.

Review of Educational Research, 86 (4), 849–899. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316675417 This peer-reviewed synthesis combines the findings of research reviews covering a century of studies on ability grouping and academic acceleration in kindergarten through grade 12.

The effects of acceleration on high-ability learners: A meta-analysis.
Gifted Child Quarterly, 55 (1). https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986210383155 This peer-reviewed review combined the results of 38 studies, published from 1984 to 2008, on the effects of acceleration for high-ability students.

Journal of Labor Economics, 30 (3). https://doi.org/10.1086/664924 This peer-reviewed study by leading labor economists examined how changes in the difficulty and availability of the GED affected high school dropout rates in several settings.
