Texas must protect families from abuse without abandoning due process, presuming guilt, forcing victims to participate in prosecutions, or interfering with legitimate decisions by judges, prosecutors, law-enforcement officers, and medical professionals.
Recent reporting from Bexar County raised serious questions after a local analysis found that nearly nine out of every ten eligible misdemeanor domestic-violence cases reviewed during the first half of 2026 had been dismissed. [KSAT, 2026a] That analysis does not establish that every dismissal was improper, and it should not be generalized to every Texas county without comparable statewide data.
It does, however, expose a basic accountability problem: Texas cannot adequately evaluate its family-violence system when courts and prosecutors do not use sufficiently detailed, standardized, and publicly reportable disposition data.
The challenge extends beyond what happens in a courtroom.
A family-violence case can be weakened long before a prosecutor reviews it if a 911 recording is deleted, a body-worn camera is not activated, visible injuries are not photographed, delayed injuries are not documented, prior protective orders are not identified, or evidence remains scattered among agencies. [KSAT, 2026b] Protective orders cannot provide their intended protection if they are not promptly transmitted, entered into law-enforcement databases, served, and enforced.
A victim may also remain trapped when leaving would mean losing a job, housing, transportation, child care, or access to legal assistance.
The proposed Protecting Families From Abuse legislative package addresses those connected failures through five coordinated bills: The Family Violence Evidence and Court Accountability Act strengthens first-response documentation, body-worn-camera practices, evidence preservation, follow-up investigations, victim consultation, dismissal reporting, and statewide case-disposition data.
The Protective Order and High-Risk Offender Act improves protective-order transmission, database entry, service, enforcement, expedited court settings, and carefully limited location monitoring for defendants who present a demonstrated continuing threat.
The Survivor Economic Independence Act creates family-safety leave and expands access to independent legal assistance, emergency financial help, safer housing, lock changes, emergency transfers, and protection against abuse-related eviction and rental-history penalties.
The Family Protection Government Accountability Act makes clearly defined ministerial duties enforceable while preserving judicial, prosecutorial, medical, and emergency-response discretion.
It emphasizes correction, training, administrative accountability, and limited entity-level liability for especially serious knowing or reckless failures.
The Family Protection Implementation and Appropriations Act funds evidence storage, protective-order systems, advocates, legal services, emergency housing, training, monitoring, reporting, and implementation grants.
This is not a mandatory-prosecution or “no-drop” package.
It does not allow an accusation, arrest, risk assessment, or protective order to prove guilt.
It does not eliminate the State’s burden of proof, authorize automatic electronic monitoring, create an automatic right to damages for ordinary mistakes, or require victims to testify.
It instead seeks to build cases from reliable evidence, protect voluntary victim participation, identify demonstrated danger through individualized judicial findings, make government perform duties the law clearly assigns, and give families a realistic opportunity to reach safety.

1 The Problem Is Larger Than a Dismissal Rate A local warning, not a statewide verdict The public discussion that helped prompt this proposal began with reporting about misdemeanor family-violence cases in Bexar County.
KSAT reported that its analysis found an approximately 89 percent dismissal rate among eligible cases reviewed during the first half of 2026. [KSAT, 2026a] Dr.
Edgardo Rafael Báez cited that reporting in a public statement and called for stronger evidence practices, greater transparency, improved protective-order enforcement, support for survivors, and closer attention to repeat or high-risk offenders. [Báez, 2026] A high dismissal rate deserves investigation, but the number alone cannot tell the public why the cases were dismissed.
A dismissal may result from insufficient admissible evidence, an unavailable witness, a legal defect, a superseding charge, successful completion of an authorized program, a plea in another case, inability to locate a defendant, or another lawful reason.
Nor does dismissal prove that the original report was false or that the accused committed the alleged offense.
That is why this package does not direct prosecutors to pursue cases that cannot lawfully be proven.
Texas law already recognizes that the State—not an individual complainant—has responsibility for deciding whether to file, continue, or dismiss a criminal case involving family violence.
Current law also states that an official may not dismiss or delay a family-violence case merely because a victim asks that the case be dismissed. [Texas Legislature, n.d.-a] The proper legislative response is not to assume wrongdoing from a percentage.
It is to require enough neutral, standardized information to distinguish among lawful dismissals, avoidable evidentiary failures, administrative delays, and recurring system problems.
Texas needs comparable information The Texas Department of Public Safety collects statewide crime information, while the Office of Court Administration collects court activity and publishes judicial reports. [Texas Department of Public Safety, 2024] [Texas Office of Court Administration, n.d.-a] These systems provide important data, but they do not currently give the public a complete, easily comparable picture connecting family-violence charges to detailed disposition reasons across every county and court.
A statewide dashboard should not be designed to shame courts, prosecutors, defendants, or victims.
It should help Texas answer practical questions: How many family-violence cases are filed?
How many end in conviction, acquittal, dismissal, diversion, or another disposition?
Why are cases dismissed?
How often is evidence missing, unavailable, suppressed, or legally insufficient?
How long do cases remain pending?
How frequently do defendants charged with violating protective orders receive repeat charges?
Are outcomes materially different among counties, courts, or offense categories?
Which differences remain after accounting for case type, evidentiary strength, and procedural posture?
Without standardized answers, elected officials may overreact to isolated figures, while real failures remain hidden inside broad categories.
2 The Principles Behind the Package Family violence involves competing duties that must be handled together rather than treated as political slogans.
The State has a duty to protect life, preserve public order, investigate reported crimes, enforce valid court orders, and use public resources responsibly.
It also has a duty to protect the constitutional rights of the accused, respect the autonomy and privacy of victims, and avoid converting professional judgment into automatic liability whenever an outcome is tragic.
The package therefore rests on seven principles.
Build cases from evidence A prosecution should not depend entirely on whether a frightened, injured, financially dependent, or threatened person is willing and able to recount the incident months later.
Officers should collect available evidence at the scene, document what they personally observed, preserve recordings and digital evidence, and perform appropriate follow-up.
Keep the burden on the State The accused remains presumed innocent.
The State must prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt.
A report, arrest, protective order, risk-screening result, or allegation does not itself establish guilt.
Respect victim autonomy A victim may decline an interview, medical treatment, photography, a medical-record authorization, safety screening, advocacy, or continued contact.
Refusing a service should not be treated as proof that the report was false or as justification for withholding other assistance.
Focus restrictions on demonstrated risk Electronic monitoring and other substantial restrictions should depend on individualized judicial findings, disclosed evidence, counsel, an opportunity to contest the restriction, consideration of less restrictive alternatives, and periodic review.
Make clear duties enforceable Professional judgment must remain protected.
By contrast, once the law imposes a clear duty—such as transmitting an issued protective order, preserving identified evidence, entering an order into a database, or providing a legally required notice—the responsible government entity should not be free to disregard that duty without correction or accountability.
Give families a viable path to safety A paper order offers limited protection when a victim cannot miss work, pay a deposit, replace locks, obtain child care, secure transportation, or consult an independent attorney.
Safety policy must account for these practical barriers.
Measure results honestly Texas should report outcomes using neutral definitions, protect confidential information, disclose data limitations, and allow counties to be compared fairly.
Transparency should identify problems without exposing victims or prejudicing defendants.

Injuries can change appearance.
Damaged property can be repaired.
Messages can be deleted.
Witnesses can become unavailable.
Memory can fade.
A 911 recording may capture facts, fear, threats, background sounds, and timing that cannot be reconstructed later.
Current Texas law requires officers investigating family-violence incidents to prepare written reports containing specified information, including the names of the suspect and complainant, the date and location, visible or reported injuries, a description of the incident, and whether an arrest occurred. [Texas Legislature, n.d.-b] The proposed legislation builds on that foundation rather than replacing it.
The enhanced report would distinguish among: Facts personally observed by an officer.
Statements attributed to victims, suspects, children, and other witnesses.
Information drawn from prior reports or databases.
Inferences or professional conclusions.
Visible injuries and reported pain.
Indications of strangulation or suffocation.
Firearm access or threatened weapon use.
Prior protective orders, bond conditions, calls, or reported violations.
The presence and condition of children.
Evidence collected and evidence that was unavailable, unsafe to collect, declined, or prohibited by law.
This distinction matters for both sides.
Prosecutors need to know what can be authenticated and offered in court.
Defense counsel must be able to identify hearsay, inconsistencies, missing evidence, or unsupported conclusions.
Courts need reliable information when setting bond or considering an emergency protective order.
Body-worn cameras are a tool, not a verdict Research on body-worn cameras does not support treating cameras as a cure-all.
Effects vary by policy, officer compliance, agency practice, and the outcome being measured.
The National Institute of Justice explains that body-worn-camera evidence can improve documentation and review, but results across studies remain mixed and context dependent. [National Institute of Justice, n.d.] Research focused specifically on intimate-partner-violence cases found that body-worn-camera evidence was associated with differences in criminal-justice processing, including charging and case outcomes, in the jurisdiction studied. [Morrow et al., 2016] The study does not prove that the same results will occur in every Texas jurisdiction, but it supports preserving first-response recordings as potentially important evidence rather than relying exclusively on later recollection.
The proposal generally requires an equipped officer dispatched to possible family violence to activate the camera before arrival when reasonably practicable and continue recording during active participation.
It also recognizes legitimate exceptions: A specific threat to safety.
Equipment failure or unavailability.
Interference with emergency medical care.
A legal prohibition.
A victim’s privacy request after the immediate emergency has ended, when continued recording is not needed for evidence or safety.
Another circumstance making recording objectively unreasonable or impracticable.
An officer who delays or stops recording must document the reason, with supervisory review of unexplained or extended failures.
The purpose is accountability, not automatic punishment.
Preserve inculpatory and exculpatory evidence The preservation rules expressly cover evidence that tends to implicate a suspect and evidence that tends to clear one.
Covered evidence includes 911 recordings, dispatch records, body-worn and dashboard-camera video, surveillance recordings, photographs, witness statements, digital communications, physical evidence, medical authorizations, emergency-medical records, high-risk screening documents, and records concerning protective orders or bond conditions.
Unless another law requires longer retention, covered evidence would be preserved until the latest applicable event, including: Five years after the incident.
Expiration of the relevant limitation period.
Final disposition and expiration of the time for direct appeal.
Final disposition of a related protective-order proceeding.
Final disposition of a related civil claim after proper notice.
Expiration or withdrawal of a valid preservation notice.
A victim, defendant, defense attorney, prosecutor, court, or other authorized person could issue a sufficiently specific preservation notice.
The recipient would have to acknowledge the notice and state whether the evidence is preserved, unavailable, outside its control, nonexistent, or unlocatable after a reasonable search.
Preservation does not equal disclosure.
The bill does not allow unauthorized access to confidential evidence, medical records, criminal-history information, or protected victim information.
It preserves material so that a court can later determine who may lawfully receive it.
Move evidence promptly Within 24 hours after an arrest, the investigating agency would transmit reasonably available safety and probable-cause information to the magistrate and prosecutor.
Additional evidence would be transmitted when it becomes reasonably available.
The proposal expressly preserves the prosecution’s constitutional and statutory discovery duties.
Texas law requires the State to disclose designated evidence to the defense, including material evidence that may be favorable to the defendant, subject to lawful restrictions and protective measures. [Texas Legislature, n.d.-c] Follow injuries that develop later The evidence bill requires officers to offer follow-up contact and photographs approximately 24 to 72 hours after an incident when strangulation, suffocation, internal injury, delayed bruising, or another developing injury is reported or reasonably suspected.
Participation remains voluntary, and emergency treatment may never be delayed for evidence collection.
The accompanying accountability bill would require emergency-medical personnel responding to specified danger indicators to offer transport or evaluation, document whether it was accepted, provide standardized information about delayed symptoms, and preserve the medical-response record.
Diagnosis, treatment, triage, transport priority, and allocation of personnel during competing emergencies remain professional medical judgments.
5 Protect Victims Without Controlling Them The State prosecutes; the victim does not The phrase “victim dropped the charges” often misstates how criminal prosecution works.
The government—not the complaining witness—files and disposes of a criminal charge.
Texas law specifically provides that a prosecutor may not dismiss or delay a family-violence case solely because the victim requests dismissal. [Texas Legislature, n.d.-a] That does not mean the victim’s wishes are irrelevant.
A victim may have information about safety, coercion, financial dependence, child care, housing, immigration consequences, or the practical effects of continued litigation.
The State should listen, provide accurate information, protect against intimidation, and avoid making participation a condition of receiving help.
Independent consultation before an affidavit An affidavit of non-prosecution can have serious consequences, yet a victim may be asked to sign one while under pressure from the accused, relatives, financial circumstances, or fear of retaliation.
The evidence bill would require an offer of confidential consultation with a qualified family-violence advocate before such an affidavit is accepted for a family-violence case.
The advocate would explain: That the prosecutor, not the victim, controls the charging decision.
That signing does not guarantee dismissal.
That refusing to sign does not guarantee prosecution.
Available protective orders and safety-planning resources.
Possible housing, employment, financial, and legal assistance.
The consequences of knowingly making a false statement.
The victim’s right to decline advocacy or continued participation.
The consultation is not intended to pressure the victim toward prosecution.
Its purpose is to ensure that an important decision is informed and, as far as reasonably possible, voluntary.
Services cannot depend on cooperation Victim Autonomy The package repeatedly prohibits conditioning assistance on filing charges, obtaining a protective order, permanently leaving the alleged offender, testifying, signing an affidavit, or cooperating with law enforcement.
This separation is important because safety is not a single event.
A person may leave and return multiple times, remain in contact because of children or finances, decline prosecution, or be unable to relocate immediately.
Refusing one option should not disqualify that person from shelter, medical care, legal assistance, safety planning, or emergency financial help.
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identifies economic security, healthy relationship skills, protective environments, and survivor-centered support as interconnected components of intimate-partner-violence prevention and response. [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2017] The legislative package applies that practical insight without adopting any source’s policy conclusions wholesale: physical safety often depends on whether a family can obtain housing, income, transportation, child care, communication, and legal help.
6 Make Protective Orders Operational A protective order must move from the courtroom to the systems used by officers in the field.
Texas maintains a statewide protective-order registry, while law-enforcement access and public access differ according to law and the sensitivity of the records. [Texas Office of Court Administration, n.d.-b] [Texas Office of Court Administration, 2024] The proposed Protective Order and High-Risk Offender Act establishes measurable duties at each stage.
Transmission A court or magistrate issuing an emergency protective order would transmit it electronically to every required government entity immediately and no later than four hours after issuance.
A major emergency or insufficient identifying information may make that deadline impossible.
In that circumstance, the issuing authority must document the specific problem and transmit the order as soon as practicable.
Database entry The receiving agency would enter the order into the Texas Crime Information Center as soon as practicable and no later than 12 hours after receipt.
If a major emergency or insufficient information temporarily prevents validated entry, the agency would document the problem, create a temporary statewide notice accessible to law enforcement, and complete the validated entry when the problem ends.
Service Each order would designate the agency responsible for service.
The agency would promptly begin reasonable service efforts, document unsuccessful attempts, and continue until service is completed, another authorized entity confirms service, the order expires, or the court directs otherwise.
Verification and enforcement When an alleged violation is reported, an officer would attempt to verify the order through the Texas Crime Information Center, National Crime Information Center, issuing court, a reliable copy, or another reliable source.
A delayed database entry would not invalidate an otherwise enforceable order.
The legislation does not require arrest based solely on an accusation.
Officers must still determine whether probable cause exists and act according to existing arrest authority.
If probable cause is not found, the material facts supporting that determination must be documented.
Due-Process Safeguard This balances urgency with constitutional restraint: police should not ignore a credible report merely because a database is incomplete, but neither should a person be arrested solely because someone alleges a violation.
7 Concentrate Restrictions on Demonstrated Danger Monitoring must be individualized Electronic location monitoring can be a substantial restriction.
It may affect work, travel, medical care, child visitation, religious activity, privacy, and a defendant’s ability to comply with other court requirements.
It also creates sensitive location data and the possibility of technical errors.
The proposed legislation therefore rejects automatic GPS monitoring for everyone accused of family violence.
At magistration, a court could impose temporary monitoring only when sworn information establishes probable cause to believe that the defendant committed a family-violence offense and that monitoring is immediately necessary to address a specific, articulable continuing threat. [Texas Legislature, n.d.-e] The temporary condition would expire on the third business day unless the defendant waives a hearing after consulting counsel or the court holds an adversarial hearing.
Continued monitoring would require written findings that: The State proved a continuing threat by clear and convincing evidence.
Less restrictive conditions would not reasonably protect the alleged victim or public.
Each geographic restriction is sufficiently specific.
The monitoring system can reasonably implement the condition.
The burden is not excessive compared with the identified threat.
The defendant would have a right to counsel, reasonable notice of the factual basis, and enough disclosure to contest the restriction.
Sensitive information could be redacted, protected, or submitted under seal, but not withheld so completely that the hearing becomes meaningless.
The court may consider patterns, not stereotypes Relevant evidence may include prior convictions, prior judicial findings, protective-order violations, strangulation, stalking, unlawful tracking, credible threats, weapon access, escalation, kidnapping, forced entry, harm to children or pets, and the alleged victim’s assessment of danger.
A rebuttable presumption of continuing threat would apply when reliable evidence establishes that a defendant committed a new family-violence offense while subject to an active protective order or family-violence bond condition, or repeatedly violated the order or condition.
Even then, the presumption may be contested.
An arrest, unsupported allegation, or risk-screening score cannot alone establish a continuing threat.
Protect against technical injustice A technical alert, temporary signal loss, charging failure, malfunction, or third-party interference would not automatically establish a violation.
Before imposing sanctions, a court would have to find that the defendant knowingly or recklessly violated the monitoring condition.
Monitoring data would have to be encrypted, access limited and logged, commercial use prohibited, and data destroyed after the required period unless a court orders continued preservation.
A defendant could not be detained or punished merely because the defendant cannot afford the monitoring fee.
If the court requires monitoring for an indigent defendant, public or otherwise authorized funds would cover the cost.
The condition would be reviewed at least every 60 days, after a material change in circumstances, or on motion of either party.
Give genuinely dangerous cases reasonable priority The bill would also direct courts to give reasonable docket priority to cases involving alleged new offenses committed while an order or family-violence bond condition is active, alleged order violations, strangulation, stalking, kidnapping, deadly weapons, serious bodily injury, credible homicide or suicide threats, or another articulable continuing threat.
Priority is not permission to rush a defendant to trial unprepared.
Courts must preserve reasonable time to obtain counsel, investigate, review discovery, retain experts, summon witnesses, and litigate constitutional and evidentiary issues.
8 Give Families a Realistic Path Out Economic pressure is a safety issue Family violence can affect physical safety, employment, housing, transportation, child care, credit, identity documents, and access to money. [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, n.d.] National public-health guidance treats economic support and survivor-centered services as relevant components of prevention and safety. [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2017] Texas already provides certain lease-termination and lock-change rights under defined circumstances.
Existing Property Code provisions allow qualifying tenants to terminate leases early or obtain lock changes in specified family-violence situations, subject to statutory documentation and notice rules. [Texas Legislature, n.d.-d] The proposed Survivor Economic Independence Act builds on those protections.
Independent civil legal assistance The Health and Human Services Commission would administer grants for independent attorneys assisting victims with: [Texas Health and Human Services Commission, n.d.] Protective orders.
Housing and lease termination.
Employment rights.
Child custody and support.
Immigration consequences.
Crime-victim compensation.
Identity theft and financial abuse.
Restitution.
Enforcement of legal rights.
The attorney would represent the victim—not the State, police, or prosecutor.
Assistance could not be conditioned on cooperation in a criminal investigation or prosecution.
Flexible emergency assistance Qualified family-violence service providers could use stabilization grants for immediate needs such as: [Texas Health and Human Services Commission, 2024] Emergency lodging.
Deposits and temporary rent.
Utilities and lock replacement.
Transportation.
Child care.
Replacement identification.
Communication and technology safety.
Food, medication, clothing, and household necessities.
Another expense directly connected to immediate safety or separation.
A flexible program recognizes that different families face different barriers.
One person may need a hotel room; another may need a rental deposit, gasoline, a replacement telephone, medication, or child care for a court hearing.
Family-safety leave Employees who have worked for a covered employer for at least 30 days would be entitled to as many as ten unpaid workdays of family-safety leave during a 12-month period.
The requirement would apply to employers with at least 15 employees.
Leave could be used to: Obtain medical or mental-health treatment.
Seek victim services or safety planning.
Relocate or secure housing.
Seek a protective order.
Meet with police, an advocate, or an attorney.
Attend legal or child-related proceedings.
Help the employee’s child with a qualifying need.
An employee could choose to use accrued paid leave but could not be forced to exhaust it first.
Advance notice would be required when practicable, but not during an emergency.
Employers could request reasonable documentation, including a sworn employee statement, but could not demand a confidential shelter address, privileged communications, or proof that criminal charges were filed.
Information would remain confidential except with consent, when legally required, or when reasonably necessary to address an immediate threat.
The Texas Workforce Commission would investigate complaints and first attempt conciliation.
Civil action would become available only after the administrative process, with remedies including reinstatement, lost wages, actual damages, equitable relief, costs, and attorney’s fees.
Exemplary damages would not be authorized.
Texas generally allows private employers to establish their own vacation and sick-leave policies unless a specific law, contract, or policy provides otherwise. [Texas Workforce Commission, n.d.] That makes a carefully defined family-safety leave statute necessary if Texas wants these protections to apply consistently across covered employers.
Housing stability The bill would: Require expedited hearings for temporary exclusive possession of a residence.
Prohibit conditioning that relief on continuation of a criminal case.
Require state-supported housing to permit an emergency transfer to an available comparable unit when necessary for safety.
Prohibit eviction or nonrenewal based substantially on abuse committed against the tenant, calls for emergency assistance, or perpetrator-caused damage, subject to a limited safety exception.
Require an expedited lock change within 24 hours after qualifying documentation.
Prohibit denial of a rental application based substantially on abuse-related eviction, debt, damage, or lease termination.
Protect the confidentiality of the victim’s request, supporting information, new address, and transfer location.
These protections do not require a landlord to disregard unrelated rental history, dangerous conduct by the applicant, ordinary nonpayment unrelated to abuse, or other material information.
9 Explain Dismissals Instead of Politicizing Them Neutral public codes The Family Violence Evidence and Court Accountability Act would require standardized disposition and dismissal categories.
Public-facing codes should be factual and neutral—for example: Evidentiary insufficiency.
Essential witness unavailable.
Evidence suppressed or legally inadmissible.
Superseding indictment or refiled charge.
Plea or disposition in another case.
Defendant deceased or incompetent.
Diversion or authorized program completion.
Interests-of-justice dismissal.
Administrative or jurisdictional defect.
Other reason requiring confidential explanation.
A short public code cannot capture every case.
Prosecutors would therefore maintain a more detailed confidential explanation for auditing and oversight.
That explanation would remain protected from public release when necessary to preserve victim safety, attorney work product, ongoing investigations, medical information, juvenile information, or the defendant’s right to a fair trial.
Data must include context A statewide dashboard should publish aggregated information by county, court, offense category, filing year, disposition type, dismissal reason, and time to disposition.
Small-cell suppression and other privacy protections should prevent identification of victims or defendants from rare combinations of data.
The Office of Court Administration would establish definitions, validate submissions, identify missing information, and explain limitations. [Texas Office of Court Administration, n.d.-a] The dashboard should distinguish: Cases filed from incidents reported.
Defendants from charges.
Original charges from reduced or superseding charges.
Dismissals before and after indictment or information.
Dismissals followed by refiling.
Deferred dispositions from convictions.
Pending cases from completed cases.
The purpose is not to create a prosecution quota.
A court or prosecutor should never be rewarded for convictions without regard to justice, evidence, or constitutional rights.
The purpose is to detect where evidence disappears, orders are delayed, cases repeatedly fail for the same preventable reason, or resources do not match caseloads.
10 Hold Government Accountable Without Punishing Judgment Distinguish ministerial duties from discretionary decisions A central drafting challenge is separating a ministerial duty from a professional or discretionary decision.
Definition A ministerial duty is a clearly assigned act that leaves little or no room for deciding whether the government should perform it.
Examples include transmitting an already-issued order, entering it into a required database, correcting a rejected entry after notice, preserving identified evidence, or providing a notice that the law expressly requires.
Discretionary decisions include: Whether probable cause exists.
What charge to file.
Whether evidence is sufficient to continue prosecution.
Whether to offer or accept a plea.
How a judge rules.
How emergency personnel allocate resources among competing emergencies.
Medical diagnosis and treatment selection.
The proposed accountability system targets the first category while protecting the second.
Equitable enforcement A victim, guardian, or authorized legal representative could ask a district court to order performance of a clear legal duty.
Available relief could include: Preservation of identified evidence.
Correction of an inaccurate database entry.
Transmission or entry of an order.
Required notice or consultation.
Performance of a reporting duty.
A corrective-action plan.
Injunctive, declaratory, or mandamus relief.
The remedy could not be used to compel prosecution, dictate a charging or plea decision, reverse a judicial act, interfere with a defendant’s rights, or obtain discovery unavailable under other law.
Except in an emergency involving imminent injury, retaliation, destruction of evidence, or disclosure of protected information, the responsible entity would receive notice and an opportunity to correct the failure before litigation.
Distributed oversight The package does not create a new statewide bureaucracy.
Existing inspectors general, internal auditors, professional-standards units, compliance officers, and equivalent offices would handle complaints within their existing jurisdictions.
Responsibility would be divided among: The Office of Court Administration for court administration and reporting.
The Department of Public Safety for database and criminal-history systems.
The Texas Commission on Law Enforcement for peace-officer training and licensing.
The Health and Human Services Commission for victim services and designated health-service duties.
The Texas Workforce Commission for family-safety leave.
The attorney general for statewide legal coordination and authorized enforcement.
These offices would investigate complaints, protect confidential information, allow a response, issue findings, require or recommend corrective action, refer matters outside their authority, and publish de-identified annual reports.
Correction before punishment An isolated good-faith mistake by a peace officer would ordinarily be addressed through correction and training.
Knowing or repeated failures involving ministerial preservation, reporting, transmission, entry, or notification duties could lead to reprimand, corrective training, suspension, or licensing action after notice and a hearing.
Retaliation against a victim, witness, advocate, complainant, or employee reporting noncompliance would constitute a separate violation.
Narrow entity-level liability The accountability bill creates a limited cause of action against a governmental unit—not an individual employee—for bodily injury or death caused by a knowing or reckless failure to perform certain enumerated immediate-safety duties.
A claimant would have to prove, among other elements: Actual notice of a specific and articulable threat.
Satisfaction of the statutory conditions requiring performance.
That performance was reasonably possible.
More than ordinary negligence.
Foreseeability.
A legally sufficient causal connection between the failure and injury.
The proposal does not impose damages for ordinary negligence, a bad outcome, a dismissal, an acquittal, a technical failure outside the entity’s control, good-faith emergency judgment, inability to stop another person’s criminal act, or a victim’s decision to decline services.
Exemplary damages would not be available.
Existing statutory damage limits would apply.
Judges would remain protected for judicial acts, prosecutors for prosecutorial decisions, medical professionals for protected professional judgment, and public employees from new personal liability while acting within the scope of employment.

12 What the Package Does Not Do The package is intentionally limited.
It does not presume guilt An allegation, arrest, protective order, screening score, or monitoring alert does not prove a criminal offense.
The State retains the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.
It does not prohibit legitimate dismissals Prosecutors may dismiss cases when evidence is legally insufficient, prosecution would be unjust, a witness is unavailable, evidence is inadmissible, another disposition resolves the conduct, or another lawful reason applies.
The proposal requires an accurate reason, not a predetermined outcome.
It does not force victims to testify Services cannot be conditioned on cooperation.
Advocacy and medical authorizations remain voluntary.
Victims may decline interviews, photographs, follow-up contact, screening, or assistance.
It does not establish automatic electronic monitoring Continued monitoring requires counsel, an adversarial hearing, clear and convincing evidence of a continuing threat, written findings, consideration of less restrictive alternatives, and periodic review.
It does not punish poverty A defendant may not be jailed or sanctioned solely because the defendant cannot afford monitoring.
When monitoring is judicially required for an indigent defendant, an authorized public source must cover the cost.
It does not create automatic dismissal or suppression Government noncompliance does not automatically exclude evidence, dismiss a prosecution, prove innocence, prove guilt, or create a ground for reversal.
Existing constitutional and statutory remedies remain available when their legal standards are met.
It does not impose personal liability for good-faith service The limited civil remedy runs against the governmental unit and requires knowing or reckless failure, actual notice, foreseeability, and causation.
Good-faith emergency, medical, judicial, prosecutorial, and probable-cause decisions remain protected.
It does not create a red-flag firearm-seizure program The bills require documentation of firearm access and enforcement of existing lawful orders and conditions.
They do not create a new firearm-confiscation process.
It does not create a conviction quota Public reporting is intended to identify outcomes and system failures.
It may not lawfully substitute statistics for individualized justice.
13 What This Means for House District 109 House District 109 includes communities with different levels of staffing, transportation access, housing availability, court capacity, and proximity to specialized services.
Cedar Hill, Lancaster, eastern DeSoto, Glenn Heights, Wilmer, Hutchins, Combine, the district’s portion of Ferris, and Seagoville should not receive different levels of protection merely because services, courts, agencies, and shelters operate across city and county lines.
The package would support the district by: Creating consistent evidence and preservation rules across jurisdictions.
Making protective orders more readily verifiable by officers working across city boundaries.
Supporting regional advocates who can provide secure remote consultation.
Funding local and regional evidence-storage infrastructure.
Helping smaller agencies meet statewide standards without building separate systems.
Providing employment protections to eligible workers.
Expanding access to independent legal help and emergency assistance.
Requiring state-supported housing providers to respond to qualifying safety needs.
Giving residents a process to correct a missed ministerial duty without directing prosecutors or judges how to decide a case.
Producing statewide and county-level data that residents and legislators can use to assess performance.
Local governments should retain authority over their departments and operations, but state law should establish a dependable floor when life, liberty, court orders, and constitutional rights are at stake.
Local control works best when duties are clear, results are measurable, and citizens can hold both local and state institutions accountable.
14 The Five-Bill Package Family Violence Evidence and Court Accountability Act This bill governs the investigation, preservation, prosecution, disposition, and reporting of family-violence cases.
Its principal provisions concern enhanced incident reports, body-worn-camera activation, five-year minimum evidence retention, preservation notices, prompt evidence transfer, voluntary medical-record authorizations, follow-up investigation, victim consultation, dismissal reasons, statewide reporting, and training.
Protective Order and High-Risk Offender Act This bill governs response to reported order violations, prompt transmission and database entry, documented service efforts, individualized location monitoring, privacy and technical safeguards, periodic review, and reasonable docket priority for cases presenting specified continuing dangers.
Survivor Economic Independence Act This bill establishes independent legal-assistance and flexible emergency-support grants, ten workdays of family-safety leave for eligible employees of covered employers, expedited exclusive-possession proceedings, emergency housing transfers, anti-eviction protection, 24-hour qualifying lock changes, and protection against abuse-related rental-history penalties.
Family Protection Government Accountability Act This bill authorizes prospective enforcement of clear legal rights, assigns compliance responsibilities to existing agency offices, creates administrative accountability for knowing or repeated failures, defines emergency-medical ministerial duties, and establishes a narrow governmental remedy for bodily injury or death caused by knowing or reckless failure to perform enumerated immediate-safety duties.
Family Protection Implementation and Appropriations Act This bill supplies the financial and grant structure needed to implement the other four bills.
Final appropriation amounts would be inserted only after official fiscal review.
15 A Better Standard for Texas Texas does not have to choose between protecting victims and protecting due process.
It does not have to choose between local control and statewide accountability.
It does not have to choose between respecting professional judgment and enforcing clear legal duties.
A responsible system should collect reliable evidence, preserve what it collects, deliver orders promptly, concentrate restrictions on demonstrated danger, give families a practical path to safety, explain case outcomes, and correct government failures.
It should also remain humble about what government can prove and what any safety tool can guarantee.
No statute can prevent every act of violence.
No protective order, monitoring device, prosecution, shelter, or grant program eliminates risk.
The duty of the Legislature is to define lawful responsibilities, provide workable resources, preserve constitutional protections, measure results honestly, and improve the system when evidence shows that it is failing.
That is the purpose of the Protecting Families From Abuse package.

(b) Subject to Subsection (a), a victim, guardian, or legal representative has standing to seek enforcement of a clear legal right provided by: (1) Section 30, Article I, Texas Constitution; (2) this chapter; (3) Chapter 5A; (4) Article 17.292; (5) Chapter 71, 83, 85, 86, or 93, Family Code; or (6) another provision expressly made enforceable under this article. (b-1) An action lies against the governmental entity responsible for the ministerial duty, not against a judge for a judicial act or a prosecutor for a prosecutorial act.
Sovereign and governmental immunity to suit are waived only for the prospective equitable relief authorized by this article.
Venue is in a district court in the county where the duty must be performed. (c) Available relief includes: (1) mandamus; (2) temporary or permanent injunctive relief; (3) declaratory relief; (4) correction of an inaccurate or incomplete database entry; (5) preservation of specifically identified evidence; (6) lawful production of evidence; (7) required notice or consultation; (8) performance of a reporting duty; (9) a corrective-action plan; and (10) reasonable attorney’s fees and costs when the claimant substantially prevails. (c-1) Damages are not recoverable under this article. (d) Unless imminent harm, retaliation, or destruction of evidence is alleged, the claimant shall provide written notice and three business days to cure before seeking relief. (e) A court shall protect confidential information and may seal records or limit disclosure when necessary. (f) A proceeding under this article may not be used to: (1) compel prosecution; (2) direct a charging or plea decision; (3) contest a lawful disposition; (4) interfere with the defendant’s constitutional rights; or (5) obtain discovery unavailable under other law. (g) A court shall expedite a proceeding alleging: (1) an immediate safety threat; (2) failure to transmit or enter a protective order; (3) imminent destruction of evidence; or (4) unlawful disclosure of a confidential address or shelter location.
SECTION 3.
DISTRIBUTED FAMILY-PROTECTION COMPLIANCE.
Subchapter C, Chapter 71, Government Code, is amended by adding Section 71.0356 to read as follows: Sec.
71.0356.
FAMILY-PROTECTION COMPLIANCE COORDINATION. (a) The existing inspector general, internal auditor, professional-standards office, compliance officer, or equivalent office of the Office of Court Administration, Department of Public Safety, Texas Commission on Law Enforcement, Health and Human Services Commission, Texas Workforce Commission, and office of the attorney general shall perform the family-protection compliance duties within that agency's jurisdiction.
This section does not create a new office or require a separate staff unit. (b) Each responsible office shall receive and investigate complaints, protect confidential information, provide notice and an opportunity to respond, issue written findings, require or recommend corrective action within the agency's authority, refer matters outside its jurisdiction, monitor compliance, and publish an annual de-identified report. (c) The Office of Court Administration shall coordinate complaints concerning court administration and reporting; the Department of Public Safety shall coordinate database entry and criminal-history systems; the Texas Commission on Law Enforcement shall coordinate peace-officer training and licensing; the Health and Human Services Commission shall coordinate victim services, advocates, and covered health-service duties; the Texas Workforce Commission shall coordinate family-safety leave; and the attorney general shall coordinate statewide legal enforcement and interagency referrals. (d) An office may not direct a prosecutor's charging or disposition decision, reverse a judicial act, or disclose confidential victim, medical, shelter, juvenile, or criminal-history information except as authorized by law. (e) Before formal enforcement, the responsible office shall ordinarily provide 30 days to cure.
No cure period is required when delay presents an imminent threat of bodily injury or death, retaliation is occurring, or evidence is being intentionally destroyed, concealed, or falsified.

1701.658.
FAMILY-PROTECTION DUTIES; DISCIPLINE. (a) A knowing or repeated failure by a licensed peace officer to comply with a ministerial evidence-preservation, reporting, order-entry, transmission, or notification duty may constitute grounds for: (1) corrective training; (2) a written reprimand; (3) suspension; or (4) license action by the Texas Commission on Law Enforcement after notice and hearing. (c) Retaliation against a victim, witness, advocate, complainant, or employee reporting noncompliance constitutes a separate violation. The rules must provide due process and judicial review to the extent provided by this chapter. (e) The commission may subpoena a record or witness only under authority otherwise provided by this chapter. (f) A law-enforcement agency may not retaliate against a person who in good faith reports noncompliance. (g) Nothing in this section limits an agency's authority to impose appropriate employment discipline.

EMERGENCY MEDICAL RESPONSE DUTIES.

773.0635.
FAMILY-VIOLENCE RESPONSE; MINISTERIAL DUTIES. (a) When emergency medical services personnel respond to a possible family-violence incident and strangulation, suffocation, loss of consciousness, difficulty breathing or swallowing, neurological symptoms, pregnancy-related injury, or another condition presenting an immediate risk is reported or observed, the provider shall: (1) offer transport or emergency medical evaluation consistent with the provider's authorized level of service; (2) document whether transport or evaluation was offered and accepted or declined; (3) provide standardized written and oral information concerning delayed symptoms requiring immediate medical attention; (4) offer an optional medical-record authorization approved under Article 5A.0161, Code of Criminal Procedure; (5) preserve the emergency medical services record as required by law; (6) perform a mandatory child-abuse report when required by Chapter 261, Family Code; and (7) provide meaningful language access when reasonably available. SECTION 6.
CERTAIN FAMILY-PROTECTION DUTIES Subchapter B, Chapter 101, Civil Practice and Remedies Code, is amended by adding Section 101.0217 to read as follows: Sec.
101.0217.
CERTAIN FAMILY-PROTECTION DUTIES (a) In this section, “covered duty” means a ministerial duty expressly imposed by law to: (1) transmit an active protective order after actual notice of a specific and articulable threat; (2) enter an issued protective order into a required database; (3) correct a rejected or incomplete protective-order entry after actual notice; (4) provide a legally required notice of an order, release, or monitoring alert; (5) transmit a confirmed monitoring violation or emergency alert under an adopted protocol; (6) provide a legally required emergency medical transport offer, delayed-injury warning, or language-access service after actual notice of a condition presenting an immediate risk of bodily injury; or (7) perform another immediate-safety duty that the applicable statute expressly designates as ministerial and enforceable under this section. (a-1) In this section, "knowing" means actual awareness that the statutory conditions requiring performance are satisfied.
"Reckless" means conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that failure to perform the covered duty will cause bodily injury or death. (b) A governmental unit is liable for bodily injury or death proximately caused by a knowing or reckless failure to perform a covered duty if: (1) the governmental unit or responsible employee had actual notice of a specific and articulable threat to the claimant; (2) the statutory conditions requiring performance were satisfied; (3) performance was reasonably possible; (4) the failure constituted more than ordinary negligence; (5) the threatened injury was reasonably foreseeable; and (6) the claimant proves either that timely performance more likely than not would have prevented or materially reduced the injury or that the failure was a substantial factor that materially increased the risk and produced the injury. (c) Sovereign and governmental immunity to suit and liability are waived and abolished only to the extent of liability expressly created by this section. (d) Notwithstanding Sections 101.055 and 101.062, the exclusions relating to emergency response, police protection, fire protection, or 911 service do not bar a claim satisfying every requirement of this section. (e) Damages are subject to Section 101.023.
An action must be brought not later than the second anniversary of the injury in a district court in the county where the failure occurred or where the claimant resided when injured. (f) Exemplary damages are not recoverable. (g) This section does not impose liability for: (1) a charging, plea, trial, or dismissal decision; (2) a judicial decision or other judicial act; (3) a probable-cause determination; (4) a medical diagnosis or treatment decision; (5) allocation of personnel during competing emergencies; (6) inability to perform caused by circumstances outside the governmental unit’s reasonable control; (7) a good-faith technical failure that the unit could not reasonably prevent or correct; (8) a victim’s decision not to accept services; (9) failure to prevent criminal conduct when no covered duty was violated; or (10) an injury based solely on dismissal or acquittal of a defendant. (h) This section creates liability against the governmental unit and does not create personal liability against an employee acting within the scope of employment. (i) This section does not limit liability otherwise available for: (1) intentional evidence destruction; (2) falsification of an official record; (3) retaliation; (4) corruption; (5) conduct outside the scope of employment; or (6) a violation of federal law.
SECTION 7.
NOTICE AND OPPORTUNITY TO CURE (a) Except when the claim involves death or an emergency preventing notice, a claimant shall provide notice under Section 101.101. (b) Before seeking equitable relief, a claimant shall provide a reasonable opportunity to cure unless: (1) injury is imminent; (2) evidence is at risk of destruction; (3) confidential information is at risk of disclosure; or (4) the governmental unit has stated that it will not comply.
SECTION 8.
NO DEFENDANT WINDFALL Except as required by the federal or state constitution, Article 39.14, Code of Criminal Procedure, or other law: (1) governmental noncompliance with this Act does not automatically require exclusion of evidence; (2) noncompliance does not create an automatic ground for dismissal; (3) noncompliance does not establish guilt; (4) noncompliance may not be used to reduce the State’s evidentiary burden; and (5) a civil or administrative remedy may not restrict a defendant’s existing constitutional or statutory remedy.
SECTION 9.
TRANSITION. (a) The equitable and administrative remedies apply to a failure occurring on or after January 1, 2028. (b) Section 101.0217, Civil Practice and Remedies Code, as added by this Act, applies only to conduct occurring on or after January 1, 2028; prior conduct is governed by prior law. (c) Each agency shall designate its responsible existing compliance office and adopt required procedures not later than December 1, 2027.
SECTION 10.
EFFECTIVE DATE.
This Act takes effect September 1, 2027.
