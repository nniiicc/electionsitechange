uncap the house of representatives
The way the United States House of Representatives is organized does not correctly represent America.
Why it matters: U.S.
House seats have been capped at 435 since 1929.
Population growth has led to each member representing more people than intended.
Uncapping the House would result in reduced distortions in the system and increased democratic accountability.
The Backstory: The cap at 435 grew out of early apportionment fights, a 1920s rural–urban power struggle, and the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929.
Plans to increase the size of the House are part of a modern reform of our democratic system. response to that history.
Reformers frame expansion as finishing the framers’ design, not “blowing up” the system: the Constitution expects a growing House that tracks population, which 435 no longer does.
Uncapping is bundled with other structural reforms as part of a broader project to revitalize representative democracy rather than just fix isolated bugs.
The rationale is that large districts mute individual voice and skew power toward large donors and organized interests; smaller districts rebalance who has leverage over members.
A larger House also tightens the link between population and political power, narrowing gaps between states’ people-per-representative and people-per-elector in the Electoral College.
Congress is under‑capacitated relative to the scale and complexity of federal policy.
Adding members is meant to grow committee depth, oversight, and policy expertise.
The idea is that a somewhat larger, more specialized House could reclaim ground ceded to the executive branch and lobbyists resulting in actual self‑government.
