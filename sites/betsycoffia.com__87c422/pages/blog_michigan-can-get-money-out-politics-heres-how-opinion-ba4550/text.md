That’s how much was spent nationwide in the 2024 presidential election.
The 2024 Michigan House elections alone spent $67 million.
It begs the question: how much is our vote worth?
This is the sobering reality voters have dealt with since the disastrous 2010 U.S.
Supreme Court decision in Citizens United v.
FEC.
The decision struck down century-old bans on corporate “independent” spending, or money that doesn’t go directly to a candidate or party, equating it with free speech and ruling that the government could not restrict this spending by corporations, associations or labor unions.
The ruling opened the floodgates to “dark money” in politics, or spending meant to influence political outcomes where the source of the money is not disclosed.
Since then, corporations have been able to utilize nonprofits like 501(c)(4)s and shell companies to give unlimited amounts of money to “super PACs,” and the nonprofits do not have to disclose their donors.
This super PAC money is then used on television and online ads, as well as on mailers — or — political/election activity.
Supporters for the Michiganders for Money out of Politics campaign gather outside of the Secretary of state's Lansing headquarters on Wednesday, May 27, 2026.
Craig Mauger Since 2010, voters have been flooded with messages about candidates or specific issues, often stealthily paid for by wealthy corporations to influence election outcomes and support candidates and issues that ultimately benefit the same corporations in policymaking.
We’ve witnessed a 2,800% rise in outside spending since Citizens United (2008-2024).
These outcomes have rightly eroded voter trust in their elected officials and the means by which they are elected.
A 2025 YouGov poll commissioned by IssueOne found that 79% of Americans agreed that large independent expenditures by wealthy donors and corporations give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption, including 84% of Democrats, 79% of Independents, and 74% of Republicans.
Sixty-three percent of voters also disagreed with the Citizens United v.
FEC ruling.
In Montana, a group known as the “Transparent Election Initiative” filed two ballot initiatives: a constitutional and statutory proposal aimed at getting big money out of politics.
The measure is known as the Montana Plan, or I-194.
The ballot initiative is expected to appear on the Montana ballot in this year’s November election.
The plan aims to limit the consequences of Citizens United v.
FEC.
If approved, it will curb election spending by “artificial persons” and eliminate anonymous money, putting voters back at the center of democracy.
The plan focuses on eliminating these entities' power to spend in elections.
Now, you might be wondering: Doesn’t that violate Citizens United v.
FEC?
No.
It makes the decision irrelevant.
The 2010 decision found that a fully empowered corporation had certain rights.
However, the Montana Plan limits corporations' power.
It rests on more than 200 years of precedent the U.S.
Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed: states have the sovereign power to charter corporations and define their specific powers.
If these incorporated entities don’t have the power to spend in elections, the right to do so is irrelevant.
This approach would also prohibit out-of-state corporations from spending money in Montana elections, since they would still be subject to the same restrictions as in-state corporations.
Michigan now has the opportunity to learn from Montana’s leadership and take action to ensure our government answers to Michiganians, not concentrated corporate wealth.
It's time Michigan joined other states that have introduced similar measures, including California, Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Maryland, Minnesota, Missouri, New York, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia and Hawaii, which most recently saw their legislation signed into law.
When voters across the country were asked whether they would support a similar initiative in their own states, 72% said they would, including 82% of Democrats, 64% of Republicans and 60% of Independents.
That is why we introduced Michigan’s version of this proposal to refocus our democracy on voters — and not let wealthy corporations dictate the messages voters receive or influence the decisions elected officials make.
Senate Bills 1085, 1086 and 1087; Senate Joint Resolution L; and House Bills 6216, 6217, 6218; and House Joint Resolution Z would curb the consequences of Citizens United and regulate corporate spending power in Michigan elections.
Corporations, nonprofits and limited liability companies would no longer have the power to pay, contribute or expend money or anything of value in support of or opposition to a candidate, political party, political committee or ballot question.
Getting money out of politics is not an easy task, but it is well past time we got creative with a functional solution to restore power to voters and trust in our democracy.
