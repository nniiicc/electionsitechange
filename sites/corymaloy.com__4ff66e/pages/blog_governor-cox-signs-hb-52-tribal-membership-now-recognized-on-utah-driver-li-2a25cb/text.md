Today, Governor Spencer Cox signed HB 52 into law, and I had the privilege of standing alongside Delegate Curtis Yanito of the Navajo Nation Council when he did.
It was one of those moments in public service that reminds you exactly why the work matters.
HB 52 allows members of Utah's federally recognized tribes to request a notation of "Federally Recognized Tribal Member" on their Utah driver license or state ID.
It sounds straightforward, and it is.
But the impact is real.
Utah is home to eight federally recognized tribes.
Their members are our neighbors, our fellow citizens, and contributors to the fabric of this state.
For years, there has been no official way for a Utah-issued ID to reflect their tribal status, something that carries deep personal significance and opens practical doors in daily life.
This bill changes that.
The notation is voluntary.
It does not identify a specific tribe.
It simply affirms what tribal members already know: that they belong to a sovereign nation recognized by the United States government.
That recognition now has a place on a document they carry every day.
The bill passed every step of the legislative process without a single opposing vote.
That unanimity says something.
This was good policy, brought to us by tribal leaders themselves representing all eight of Utah's federally recognized tribes, and the Legislature agreed.
Delegate Yanito's presence at the signing was meaningful to me.
This bill came from his community and communities like his.
Seeing it become law in the company of the people it serves most directly is a memory I will carry for a long time.
This is the kind of work I show up to do.
Good governance does not always make headlines.
But it makes a difference.
