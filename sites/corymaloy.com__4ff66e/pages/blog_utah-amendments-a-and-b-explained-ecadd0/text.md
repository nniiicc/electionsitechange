At a glance Amendment A Public notice.

My vote: For.

Your ballot this fall asks two questions about the Utah Constitution.
Amendment A changes how the state notifies you about proposed constitutional amendments.
Amendment B requires 60% voter approval before a citizen initiative can raise taxes.
I support both.
Election Day is Nov.
3.
You will mark “For” or “Against” on each question, and a simple majority decides each one.
Here is what each amendment does, what happens if it passes, and how I reached my position.
Amendment A: Public notice that reaches voters What it changes.
Today the Utah Constitution requires the state to print every proposed amendment in at least one newspaper in every county for two months before the election.
Amendment A replaces that rule.
The state would publish each proposed amendment for the 60 calendar days before the election, in a manner the Legislature sets by law.
What happens if it passes.
The Legislature already passed that law.
It takes effect Jan.
1, 2027 , and only if Utah citizens approve Amendment A this November.
The law requires the lieutenant governor’s office to post the full text of every proposed amendment on the Utah Public Notice Website and on its own website for the 60 days before the election.
Why I support it.
The newspaper rule dates to a time when print was how Utahns got their news.
Fewer papers print today, and most of us read online.
In 2024, courts voided two amendments in part because the state did not meet the newspaper requirement.
Both questions appeared on your ballot.
Neither vote counted.
A constitutional rule that no longer works needs a constitutional fix, and that means citizens vote to approve the amendment.
Amendment A passed the House and Senate without a single vote against it.
Amendment B: A higher bar to raise your taxes What it changes.
Utah voters can write and pass state laws through a statewide initiative.
Today an initiative passes with a simple majority.
Under Amendment B, an initiative needs at least 60% of the vote if it does any of four things: Creates a new tax Applies an existing tax to more items or transactions Raises an existing tax rate Changes a property tax rate so it drops less than current law requires What stays the same.
Amendment B does not raise or cut any tax.
Initiatives without those tax changes still pass with a simple majority.
So do referendums on laws the Legislature passes, and so do city and county initiatives.
What happens if it passes.
The amendment takes effect Nov.
23, 2026.
No statewide initiatives qualified for this year’s ballot, so the 60% rule would first apply to a future election.
Utah has drawn this kind of line before.
The Constitution already requires a two-thirds vote for initiatives on hunting and wildlife.
Why I support it.
A tax increase takes money from every household in the state, including the ones that voted no.
A decision that size should have broad agreement behind it, not 50% plus one.
Opponents argue Amendment B weakens the initiative process.
I read it differently.
Citizens keep the full power to propose and pass laws through initiatives.
Amendment B asks one thing: before an initiative raises your neighbor’s taxes, six in 10 voters need to agree.
Your vote decides both The Legislature cannot change a word of Utah’s Constitution on its own.
Lawmakers propose.
Voters approve or reject.
That is how the founders of this state designed it, and both of these questions now sit with you.
Read each one before you mark your ballot.
The lieutenant governor’s office posts the full text of both amendments and the official voter information pamphlet at vote.utah.gov .
I will vote for Amendment A and for Amendment B, and I invite you to join me.
