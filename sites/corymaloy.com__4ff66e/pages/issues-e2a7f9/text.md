The Question Is Whether the Roads Keep Up.
Traffic is the first thing people bring up here.
Housing is the second.
They are the same conversation.
You cannot keep adding rooftops and expect the commute to hold.
We funded the I-15 Technology Corridor and moved it up two years.
I voted for the transportation bonding package that paid for that road through Lehi, Main Street to SR-92.
That was a $430 million project.
It was originally scheduled to begin construction in 2020.
The bonding moved it up the priority list, construction started in April 2018, and the corridor was finished in 2020, the year it was supposed to start.
The 2100 North Freeway is under construction now.
This is work our delegation does together, with the legislators who represent the rest of this corridor.
Traffic here is still hard and I am not going to tell you it is solved.
Picture this district today without that corridor finished.
That is the argument.
On housing, growth has to work for the families already here.
I voted no on the HTRZ expansion, SB 221 , and we killed it in committee before it reached the floor.
I voted for HB 507 , which makes developers disclose PID assessments so families know what they are buying into and starts winding down the HTRZ framework.
I voted for some of those financing tools early on, when they looked like a way to help with the housing shortage.
They have real problems.
They shift the burden onto homeowners and onto cities.
I do not support them now, and I watch for them when they turn up inside other bills.
Roads, water, and neighborhoods should get decided by the people who live with those decisions, not handed down from above.

More Opportunity.
As chair of the House Business, Labor, and Commerce Committee, cutting unnecessary regulation is my primary legislative responsibility.
I authored and expanded Utah’s Regulatory Sandbox Program, a nationally recognized framework that opened doors for entrepreneurs, business, and innovation across the state.
I sponsored the Utah Office of Regulatory Relief and have run licensing reform bills across multiple sessions.
The families and small business owners here are the backbone of this local economy.
My job is making sure state policy helps them grow instead of burying them in mandates.
No wage floors set by government, and no mandates that price a small shop out of hiring.
There is a second half to this work that gets less attention and matters more to a family.
I have spent years on bills that stop people from getting taken advantage of.
Consumer review fairness.
Collection agency rules.
Credit reporting notification.
Earned wage access.
Financial institution disclosure.
And towing.
Getting your car towed out of a lot with no sign on it should not cost a family $400.
I have run five towing bills: the signage, the fees, and the practices that make it possible.
Nobody expects a legislator to have spent this long on it.
I have, because it happens to a working family once and they never forget it.

Every income tax rate cut that reached the House floor got my vote.
So did every bill reducing what seniors owe on Social Security and retirement income.
The goal, and I am not alone in it, is to get seniors on a fixed income off the income tax entirely.
The other half is voting no.
I voted no on the taxpayer-funded Delta Center arena subsidy.
It passed anyway.
I was still a no, because that spending was not ours to approve.
I voted no on the HTRZ expansion and we killed it in committee before it reached the floor.
I have also run the bill three times to take the sales tax off prescription glasses and contact lenses.
Utah exempts medical devices from sales tax.
The law lists them out, and then it specifically excludes prescription glasses and contact lenses from that exemption.
A family of six should not pay sales tax on seeing clearly, and neither should a senior on a fixed income.
I am running it again.
SB 59 and HB 54 · Income tax rate cuts · Voted Yes SB 272 · Arena subsidy · Voted No HB 99 · Eyewear sales tax · Run three times Public Safety The Pool in Your Neighborhood Was a Gap in the Law.
Lehi Police Chief Darren Paul came to me with a problem.
Utah law kept registered child sex offenders out of public parks, public pools, public playgrounds, schools, and day cares.
It said nothing about the pool owned by a homeowners association, a condominium project, or an apartment complex.
HOA neighborhoods and apartment communities have multiplied across this district in recent years, and those are exactly the facilities the old law missed.
The pools and playgrounds our children use every summer.
We worked it out together, I ran the bill, and HB 146 closed it.
Violating it is a class A misdemeanor.
Police now have the teeth to enforce what parents were asking them to enforce all along.
Our officers wanted to act, and now the law backs them up.
I wrote the law that stiffened penalties for assaulting a peace officer.
I ran the bill on interference with public servants and the murder offense amendments in the last session.
Our officers show up for us.
I show up for them.
Protection first.
Accountability always.
Redemption where it is earned.

Fixing them has been some of my most consistent work at the Capitol.
I sponsored HB 295 , creating a grant program to expand medical residency programs.
I floor-sponsored SB 24 , expanding the scope of practice for physician assistants so more Utahns can access care.

Healthcare workforce is not a campaign topic for me.

One Vote.
Election integrity has been a consistent focus across my time in the Legislature.
I have sponsored or floor-sponsored nine election integrity bills: SB 69 (2017, Ballot Proposal Notification), HB 319 (2019, Political Advertising Amendments), SB 151 (2019, Initiative Procedure Amendments), SB 170 (2022, Election Schedule Amendments, implementing 17 of 20 recommendations from a state election audit), SB 19 (2022, Election Revisions), SB 18 (2022, Election Modifications), HB 448 (2023, Election Changes), SB 63 (2023, Election Candidate Replacement Amendments), and HB 209 (2026, Voting Amendments confirming only U.S. citizens may vote).
I also voted for SB 153 , which restored meaningful standards to voter registration privacy, protecting domestic violence survivors and law enforcement families while ensuring the rest of the voter roll is public as it should be in our republic.
On redistricting: that entire fight was about congressional maps.
District 52 was not part of the lawsuit.
Here is what happened.
The Legislature negotiated directly with the people who wrote Prop 4.
A Democrat carried the bill on the House floor.
It passed 67 to 4.
The Utah Supreme Court threw it out anyway, and a district court then adopted a map submitted by the plaintiffs.
House Speaker Schultz and Senate President Adams said jointly and publicly that the map was drawn to produce a specific partisan outcome.
A party that got the map it wanted from a judge does not get to lecture anyone about gerrymandering.
I voted for SB 200 , I am proud of that vote, and I have written the full history in The 52 Report .

Utah has had years of severe drought, a growing population drawing on the same supply, and infrastructure that is getting old.
The lake most Utahns are worried about is the Great Salt Lake, and the answer there is the same as it is everywhere: conservation, stewardship, and real investment in the systems that move and store water.
I voted for the data center water reporting bill on the same principle I apply to most things.
Know what is being used before deciding what to do about it.
Conservation and infrastructure fix this.
Panic does not, and neither does a federal mandate written by people who do not have to live with it.
