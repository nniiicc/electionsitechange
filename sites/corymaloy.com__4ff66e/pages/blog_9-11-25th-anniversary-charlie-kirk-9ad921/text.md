Today marks 25 years since Sept. 11, 2001.
Yesterday marked one year since we lost Charlie Kirk at Utah Valley University.
With the two anniversaries now a day apart, I've been thinking about them together.
A Day of Infamy
I've called 9/11 a day of infamy before, and I'll keep calling it that.
Nineteen hijackers took four passenger jets and killed 2,977 people in New York, at the Pentagon, and in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania.
Twenty-five years hasn't softened that for me.
What stays with me is firefighters climbing the stairwells of the Twin Towers while everyone else was coming down.
Thousands of people made it out of those buildings alive because firefighters, police officers, and EMTs went in after them.
The Fire Department of New York lost 343 members that morning.
The NYPD lost 23 officers, and the Port Authority Police Department lost 37.
When the towers came down, the work didn't stop.
Rescuers dug through twisted steel and passed debris hand to hand in bucket lines.
They pulled survivors out of the rubble, the last one more than a day after the collapse, and then kept searching into the next spring so families could bring someone home.
The Toll Keeps Growing
Here's the part I don't think most people realize.
The air at Ground Zero was thick with pulverized concrete, glass, asbestos, and smoke, and the crews on the pile breathed it for months.
We're still losing them.
Three years ago, the number of FDNY members who had died from 9/11 illnesses matched the 343 lost on the day of the attacks.
The department's new 25-year report puts it past 600.
FDNY Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore said 9/11 "was not a one-day event for our department.
It is a day that continues to sicken our members." On Sept. 2, they added 36 more names to their memorial wall.
Police families have lived it too.
This week the NYPD added more than 450 names to its memorial wall at One Police Plaza, each one an officer lost to illness from the recovery.
Between those two departments alone, that's more than 1,000 people.
In the past year alone, the federal 9/11 health program certified more than 10,000 new cancers.
That works out to about one every 50 minutes.
I've gotten to know a lot of Utah firefighters and police officers.
They don't talk much about what the job costs them.
Ground Zero showed all of us what it can cost.
The Savior taught that "greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends." Hundreds of responders did exactly that on 9/11.
Many more have been doing it slowly ever since, one diagnosis at a time.
Where Did That Feeling Go?
I remember how the country came together in the weeks after the attacks.
That night, members of Congress stood on the Capitol steps and sang "God Bless America." Flags went up everywhere.
Walmart sold 450,000 of them in three days, compared with 26,000 in the same stretch the year before.
By October, nearly 8 in 10 adults were flying one.
Churches were full.
I wish I could say that feeling stuck around.
It didn't.
In 2003, 70% of Americans told Gallup they were extremely proud to be American.
This June, that number dropped to 33%, the lowest Gallup has recorded.
Among adults under 35, it's 14%.
I don't think patriotism can live on the memory of a tragedy.
Grief pulls people together for a while.
After that, every generation has to learn to love this country from the one before it, or that love slips away.
A Year Without Charlie
That brings me to Charlie Kirk.
He started Turning Point USA when he was 18 and spent the next 13 years on college campuses, making the case for faith and freedom to students who didn't hear it much anywhere else.
He'd set up a tent, hand the microphone to anyone who disagreed with him, and invite them to prove him wrong.
He listened, and he answered.
That's what he was doing on Sept. 10, 2025, in Orem when we lost him, in front of the crowd that had come to hear him.
He was 31.
He left behind his wife and two little children.
What happened that afternoon hit Utah hard, and a year later we're still feeling the repercussions of that tragedy.
Those firefighters and a young man with a microphone on a Utah campus had different callings.
But both went where they felt needed and accepted the risk.
So today, fly your flag.
If you know a firefighter, a police officer, or an EMT, thank them.
And if there's a young person in your life, take a minute to tell them why that flag means something to you.
Representative Cory Maloy represents Utah House District 52, covering western Lehi, a portion of American Fork, and Saratoga Springs.
He serves as Chair of the House Business, Labor, and Commerce Committee.
