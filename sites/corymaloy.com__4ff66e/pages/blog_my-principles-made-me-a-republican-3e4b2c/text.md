I am honored to be the Republican nominee for Utah House District 52, and even more than that, I am honored to serve the people who live here.
My loyalty runs first to them, and then to the party that best reflects the values we share.
That loyalty was not handed to me.
It was built through a lifetime of conviction that started, as most things in my life do, with faith and family.
My parents gave me two things before I was old enough to understand their full weight.
The first was faith in God, rooted and real, the kind that shapes everything else.
The second was love of country.
I remember standing as a small boy, hand over heart, reciting the Pledge of Allegiance and meaning every word, because something in those words connected to what I was already being taught about duty, about God, and about the extraordinary place I was lucky enough to call home.
I did not know it yet, but they were handing me the foundation of everything I would come to believe as a Utah Republican and a constitutional conservative.
Born From Moral Conviction
That love of country sent me straight into the history books.
The Revolutionary War captured me early.
The Founding Fathers were not abstract figures to me.
George Washington, Paul Revere, the men who pledged everything for an idea that had never been tried at scale: I was fascinated by what drove them and what they built.
That fascination did not fade.
It deepened.
The Republican Party was born from that same tradition.
Founded in 1854 in a schoolhouse in Ripon, Wisconsin, not from political convenience but from moral necessity.
The men who gathered there believed the expansion of slavery had to stop.
That position was not popular.
It was right.
They built a party on the conviction that some things are not negotiable regardless of political cost.
Abraham Lincoln carried that spirit into the White House and held it there at an almost unimaginable price.
Lincoln understood that the Declaration of Independence was not ceremonial language.
It was the philosophical ground of the republic.
Government exists to secure individual liberty, not to bestow it.
That founding conviction has never left the party at its best.
God-Given Rights and the Document That Protects Them
Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.
Those words from the Declaration are not just poetic.
They are a statement of political theology.
Our rights do not come from the Constitution.
They come from God, and the Constitution exists to protect them from being taken away by those in power.
We the People opens the Constitution for a reason.
The authority of this republic flows upward from citizens, not downward from government.
That is the architecture of self-governance the Founders fought and died to establish, and it is the architecture the Republican Party has always been built to defend.
I believe God had a hand in building this republic.
I believe it, with all its struggles and contradictions, has mostly pointed toward what is right.
This country, this state, this community are the shining light upon the hill for the entire world.
As I got older I began to understand the difference between conservative and liberal, between right and left.
When I looked at it seriously, I did not feel like I was choosing a side.
I felt like I was reading a description of something I had already been for years.
I believed in God, in the sanctity of life, in free markets, in personal responsibility.
The alternative held none of those things.
That was not a close call.
The Lineage of Republican Leadership
I remember the 1968 presidential race.
I was in fifth grade, paying attention to the names and the conversations in our home.
I did not yet understand every policy difference, but I understood that people I respected cared deeply about who led this country, and I began to care too.
That interest never left.
From George Washington's model of servant leadership to Lincoln's moral courage to Ronald Reagan's unapologetic belief that freedom is not free, the Republican Party has produced leaders who understood that governing is not a career to be managed but a calling to be answered.
Reagan reportedly told his own staff: "The person who agrees with you 80 percent of the time is a friend and an ally, not a 20 percent traitor." That is the wisdom of someone who understood how durable coalitions and lasting governance actually work.
That lineage shapes how I approach my own service.
I debate within my party.
I disagree.
I push back.
I voted against the Delta Center arena deal when that was not the easy vote.
I killed an HTRZ expansion in committee.
Nobody handed me those votes.
Accountability within a party is not suppression.
It is integrity.
The Party Works for Me.
Not the Other Way Around.
Growing up in Arizona, my father and I spent a lot of time hunting and fishing.
That is where my respect for firearms took hold, out in the field with my dad, learning what the Second Amendment actually represented in the life of an ordinary American family.
It was never abstract.
It was personal.
That is how I have always related to the Republican Party.
Not as an institution handing me a script, but as a reflection of things I already believed.
The party does not dictate my convictions.
It provides a home and a structure for principles I already hold, shared by millions of Americans who love this country, believe in God-given rights, and want government to serve the people rather than rule them.
I do not stay Republican because the party tells me to.
I stay because the party reflects what I already believe.
Real conservatives debate, push back, lose some fights, and show up again the next session.
When someone's convictions evaporate the moment the party asks something of them, that reveals more about the depth of the convictions than about the party.
Two Parties, Two Visions
The Democratic Party today is not the party of John F.
Kennedy.
It has moved well past policy disagreement into a fundamentally different answer to the question of what human beings are and what government owes them.
DEI mandates displace individual merit with group identity.
Federal expansion displaces local decision-making with bureaucratic dependency.
Regulatory pressure on speech and conscience displaces open civic life with ideological enforcement.
When government decides who deserves what based on group membership, individual liberty is not just inconvenienced.
It is rejected as a governing principle.
When the federal government systematically expands into education, energy, medicine, and land use, it does not leave room for the self-governing communities that Utah has spent generations building.
These are not abstract threats.
They show up in school curricula, in business licensing, in how a family decides to raise its children.
Liberalism does not anchor itself to fixed moral truth.
It shifts with the political moment.
It offers no settled account of human dignity, no consistent framework for right and wrong, no real reverence for this country's founding or for the God who blessed its creation.
I looked into it seriously years ago, trying to understand it clearly.
What I found was not a competing vision.
It was the absence of one.
What It Has Cost
Living these principles has not been without cost.
I have lost relationships because of them.
Family.
Friends.
People I cared about.
Not because I forced my views on anyone, but because of how I have lived, how I have voted, how I have conducted my public service and my personal life.
I do not say that to generate sympathy.
I say it because anyone who thinks conservatism is just a political preference has not lived it seriously.
These are convictions, and they carry weight.
Why I'm a Republican
The party has imperfections.
So does every institution worth keeping.
I know them, argue about them, and stay anyway.
Not because the party shapes me, but because it represents me.
My faith in Jesus Christ, my reverence for the founding, my belief in God-given rights protected by the Constitution, my love of this republic and its people: all of that was already in me.
The Republican Party is where I found millions of Americans who share it.
I am staying.
I am fighting.
Because that is what principled Republicans do.
Cory Maloy represents House District 52 in the Utah State Legislature, where he chairs the House Business, Labor, and Commerce Committee.
