SUBSCRIBE Log In All Articles Search Will There Be a Future for Them Here? edarriolacnmi Sep 14 5 min read September 14, 2026 Like many parents in the CNMI, my wife and I raised our children hoping they would get a good education, find meaningful careers, build families of their own, and someday contribute to the islands that raised them.
But there is a difficult question we don't talk about enough: What are we asking them to come home to?
One of my daughters earned her degree in medical administration.
She wanted to stay close to her family, so she applied for a position with a government agency here at home.
She waited seven months before receiving an answer.
During that time, she received multiple job offers in the States.
She turned them down because she wanted to remain close to her family and build her life here.
But love of home can only ask someone to wait so long.
Eventually, she left.
I don't share her story to criticize one agency or one person.
I share it because I suspect many families in the CNMI have a similar story.
Maybe it's a son who went away to college and never came back.
Maybe it's a daughter who wanted to return but couldn't find a job in her field.
Maybe it's a young couple who realized they could earn more and build a more secure future somewhere else.
These are not always people who wanted to leave.
Sometimes they simply reached a point where staying, or coming home, no longer made sense.
This past month, my wife and I took our son to college.
In one of those quiet moments that parents remember, my son and I were walking together through a new city when he asked me a simple question: "Dad, will there be a job for me when I come home?" I wish I could tell you that I immediately said yes.
I couldn't.
I told him that I hoped this year our people would choose representatives based not simply on names, friendships, political alliances or promises, but on what those candidates can contribute toward building the CNMI's future.
Then I told my son the truth: "I don't know." That answer bothered me.
A father should be able to tell his child that if he works hard, gets an education and wants to come home, there will be an opportunity for him.
We tell our young people to study hard.
We encourage them to go to college, Start Smart at NMC, learn a trade at NMTI, earn certifications, develop skills and prepare themselves for the future.
But what happens when they do exactly what we ask of them?
Are we creating enough opportunities for them to build that future here?
And this challenge isn't only about bringing our children home after college.
It is also about keeping the talented young people we educate and train right here in the CNMI.
When employers in Guam, Hawaii, the mainland and elsewhere can offer better pay, relocation assistance and a clearer path forward, we should not be surprised when our young people take those opportunities.
We have to give them compelling reasons to stay and to build their careers, raise their families and invest their futures here at home.
At the same time, we should recognize the local businesses that continue to invest in the CNMI, keep their doors open through difficult economic times, and provide opportunities for our people.
Many of these businesses are doing everything they can to hire, train and retain local workers while facing the daily challenge of simply keeping their doors open and their employees working.
We cannot talk about creating jobs for our young people without also talking about helping the businesses that create those jobs.
Government cannot guarantee the success of every business, nor should it try.
But government can help create an environment where businesses have a fighting chance to succeed.
That means listening to the people who sign paychecks.
It means removing unnecessary barriers, making permitting and government processes faster and more predictable, encouraging investment, supporting entrepreneurs, and finding practical ways to reduce the cost and difficulty of doing business in the CNMI.
Because when our local businesses grow, they can hire more people.
They can offer better wages.
They can provide training and advancement.
And they can create more reasons for our young people to stay.
A healthy CNMI needs a strong private sector.
We need an economy where existing businesses can expand, entrepreneurs are encouraged rather than discouraged, new investment is welcomed, and industries that create professional, vocational and technical careers have a reason to establish themselves here.
Government must also do better itself.
When a qualified young person must wait seven months just to learn whether there is a place for them here, while employers elsewhere are actively recruiting them, we should not be surprised when they leave.The mainland doesn't have to convince our children to leave.
Too often, we are making that decision easier for them.
And when one of our young people leaves, we lose more than another resident.
We may lose a future nurse who would have cared for our elderly, an accountant who would have helped a local business grow, an engineer who would have helped build our infrastructure, a teacher who would have taught the next generation, a skilled tradesperson our community desperately needs, or an entrepreneur who would have opened a business and created jobs.
And eventually, we may lose the generation after them.
Their children grow up thousands of miles away from their grandparents, extended families and the islands their parents once called home.
That is how a community slowly loses its next generation.
If we want our children to come home, and if we want those who are already here to stay, we have to give them something real to build their lives around: good jobs, competitive wages, affordable places to live, a growing private sector, opportunities based on ability and merit, and a government that respects people's time and understands that economic growth must be more than a campaign talking point.
I am running for the House of Representatives because I believe government should be judged, in part, by a very simple measure: Are we making it easier or harder for our children to build their futures here?
I want the day to come when another CNMI mother or father is walking with their son or daughter on a college campus and hears the same question my son asked me: "Will there be a job for me when I come home?" And I want that parent to be able to answer without hesitation: "Yes.
Come home.
There is a future for you here." That is the CNMI we should be working to build.
Not simply a place our children are proud to call home, but a place where they can afford to choose home .
