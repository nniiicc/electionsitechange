1.
What happened at Lake Tahoe In March 2026 Liberty Utilities, which serves about 49,000 customers around South Lake Tahoe, told California regulators that NV Energy — the source of roughly 75% of its power — would stop supplying it after May 2027, citing "its own resource needs." 7 NV Energy's own resource needs are data centers: they were 22% of Nevada's electricity in 2024 and are projected to reach 35% by 2030, with data-center requests driving about three-quarters of the utility's load growth and a plan to nearly triple its peak capacity.
8 Building a direct connection over the Sierra to replace the lost supply would cost "hundreds of millions of dollars," according to Liberty's president; Liberty's rates had already roughly doubled between 2020 and 2025 and sit near 34 cents a kilowatt-hour.
8 9 A local energy leader put it plainly: "We're 49,000 customers.
We have no leverage." 8 10 Two honest notes.
NV Energy says "there will be no disruptions in power" and that "data centers did not influence this decision"; California's regulator says supply continues until replacement transmission is built, and the deadline has since slipped to 2028.
11 12 Nobody's lights went out.
What happened is quieter and fully documented: a small community's contract was not renewed by a utility whose growth is data centers, and the cost of replacing it lands on the community.
That is what "outbid" looks like.
How to read these numbers · Section 1 Wholesale supply is power one utility sells to another to resell to its own customers; Liberty owns the wires around Tahoe but buys most of the electricity.
A load is anything that uses power; a data center is a "large load" the way a steel mill is.
Peak capacity is the most power a utility can deliver at the worst hour of the year — the number that decides whether new customers can be served. " Hundreds of millions " is Liberty's estimate for a new transmission line, not a regulator's finding.
The 34¢/kWh figure is Liberty's residential rate in April 2026; RG&E customers pay roughly half that today, which is the point — we are early in the curve Tahoe is late in.

The bill comes first — before a single server is switched on $9.3B of the 2025/26 PJM capacity-auction increase attributed to data centers by the market's own monitor — 63% of the jump 1 $16–18 a month added to a residential bill in Ohio and western Maryland from that one auction 1 $29.4B data-center-related capacity costs across four PJM auctions — "a paradigm shift," says the monitor 13 14 12,000 MW of large-load requests in New York's grid queue, with gigawatt-scale data centers "particularly in upstate zones" 2 The mechanism is the capacity market: every year the grid buys enough standby power to cover the worst hour, and when data centers announce they are coming, the price of standby power jumps for everyone — years before the data center runs.
That is how families in Ohio saw $16 a month appear on their bills for computers in Virginia.

How to read these numbers · Section 2 A capacity auction buys a promise to be available at the peak hour, paid per megawatt per day; its cost is spread across every customer's bill as a "capacity" or "supply" charge.
PJM is the grid market for 13 states from New Jersey to Illinois; New York's is NYISO , which runs a similar auction.
The market monitor is an independent firm PJM is required to have; its numbers are the ones the utilities argue with.
A megawatt (MW) is capacity — Ginna is about 580 MW; 12,000 MW of requests does not mean 12,000 MW will be built, only that it is asked for.
Permanent jobs at a data center are the ongoing headcount after construction — 125 for a $19 billion campus is typical of the industry, and it is the reason the tax incentive per job is so high.

4.
What the "stop on AI" actually is — and where the hole is There are three things in Albany people mean when they say New York "stopped AI," and only one of them is law.
Executive Order 62 , signed July 14, 2026, tells DEC to hold every permit application for the "construction or expansion" of a data center of 50 megawatts or more until a statewide environmental study is done — about a year.
36 37 The Governor said in the Finger Lakes two weeks later: "we're not letting residents foot the bill while data centers come in." 38 The Responsible Data Center Development Act (S.10642 / A.11560) passed both houses in June — a one-year moratorium at 20 megawatts, a separate rate class so data centers pay their own grid costs, renewable-power requirements, prevailing wage and host-community benefits — and has been sitting on the Governor's desk since, with reporting that she "believes data center decisions should be left to municipalities." 39 40 41 42 And the RAISE Act , signed in December 2025, is an AI-safety law for the companies that build frontier models — it says nothing about data centers or power.
43 Here is the hole.
The executive order covers "construction or expansion," and the Act exempts the "modification, renewal, reissuance, or recertification of previously issued approvals." Greenidge already has the building, the gas plant, the water permit and — after the November settlement — the air permit.
Its position is that switching the computers from Bitcoin to AI is not construction, and that it is an existing data center the moratorium "does not apply to." 6 DEC's answer, so far, is that no application has been filed.
33 If that reading holds, the first AI data center in the Finger Lakes will open through the side door while every new project waits at the front.
And every retired or half-used gas plant in the state — there are dozens — becomes a candidate to do the same.
What the rules cover today New data centers of 50 MW+ (EO 62) or 20 MW+ (the Act): permits paused.
"Expansion" of a data center: paused under EO 62 — but a gas plant changing its computers argues it is not one.
Renewals and modifications of existing approvals: exempt under the Act.
Who pays for the wires: under study at the PSC (Case 26-E-0045), decision due 2027.
15 What has to be added Change of use is a new use.
Any generating plant that redirects more than 20 MW to computing — crypto, AI, anything — needs a fresh permit and a full environmental review, no matter what it was licensed for before.
The grid connection is the trigger.
A request to draw or deliver 200 MW more is an expansion by definition; write that into the order and the Act.
The lake is not a cooling pond.
Water-intake permits for computing loads reviewed on their own, with Seneca Lake and Lake Ontario protected the way the platform already promises.
Sign the bill.
Then fix it.
How to read this section An executive order binds state agencies and expires; a law binds everyone and stays. " Hold in abeyance " means the agency will not act on an application, not that it is denied.
50 MW / 20 MW thresholds are measured by the data center's electrical demand; Greenidge's proposed 260 MW clears both by a wide margin, which is why the "existing facility" argument matters.
A separate rate class means large data centers pay a tariff built on their own costs instead of sharing the residential one — the single most important protection for your bill.
PSC Case 26-E-0045 is the docket where that will be decided; anyone can file a comment.

5.
The fair, defensible version — say it this way An opponent will try to make this an argument about scare stories, so the campaign does not tell one.
The documented reality is that residents are being outbid, displaced and charged more — not that utilities are deliberately blacking out homes so AI can run.
In fact the rules are moving the other way on that narrow point: federal regulators ordered PJM in December 2025 to create "non-firm" service so co-located data centers are the first thing curtailed when supply is tight, Texas gave its grid operator the power to disconnect large loads in an emergency, and at least a dozen states are moving to charge data centers a higher rate.
44 45 46 47 Protection exists.
It is uneven, it is new, and it depends entirely on regulators acting for residents against the best-funded lobby in the country.
Lake Tahoe shows what "uneven" means: the contract simply was not renewed.
The other honest word is about AI itself.
This brief is not against computing, research or the Empire AI consortium the state exempts from its own order; it is against a business model in which the public pays for the plant, the lake and the wires, and a private ledger — Bitcoin yesterday, AI today — takes the power.
A data center that pays its own grid costs, runs on power it builds, hires at prevailing wage and returns full taxes to the school district is a factory.
One that does none of that is a toll booth on the grid.
The law should be able to tell the difference.
"AI brings jobs." Greenidge promised 60 to 100, employs about 50, and paid the county and schools 15 cents on the dollar of what the old plant paid.
The STAMP data center wants $801 million in incentives for 125 permanent jobs — $6.4 million per job.
Ask for the number, then divide.

The fix is to say so in the statute.

6.
The hedge: own it, and make it here Power the community owns and generates locally cannot be outbid for and cannot be redirected to a distant buyer.
A district that produces its own solar, wind, biomass, geothermal, hydro and storage — and owns it together, town by town, school by school, on the distributed model in the energy brief — converts "hope the regulator protects us" into "we control our own supply." 51 That is the whole argument, and it does not depend on any forecast being right: whatever AI demand turns out to be, a megawatt-hour made on a Wayne County barn roof is one no data center can buy out from under you.
The campaign's community energy model puts rough numbers on the exposure.
If a data-center-driven rate shock like the one already on bills in Ohio and Maryland — about $17 a month — hit the roughly 58,000 households of Webster and Wayne County, it would drain about $11.8 million a year out of the district, about $118 million over a decade, and roughly double that in a severe case.
At about 90% local self-supply, roughly $10.7 million a year of that exposure disappears, because the power is not for sale on the market that sets the shock.
52 1 Over thirty years the model's all-in cost to the community — build, financing, fuel, maintenance, grid rebuild, investor profit, subsidy and administration — comes to about $4.4 billion for a privatized, centralized system, $2.6 billion for privatized distributed generation, and $1.2 billion for public, distributed — the shortest bar because it carries no shareholder profit, no subsidy feeding profit, lower administration and municipal-rate financing, and it keeps about $13 million a year circulating in local wages, contracts and farm feedstock.
52 The resilience is the bonus.
About 80% of major U.S. outages are weather-related, and the last decade had roughly double the weather outages of the one before; when the lights go out it is almost never a shortage of power, it is downed lines — and a plant two hundred miles away runs while your house sits dark.
53 Power made and stored locally can island and keep the fire station, the water plant and the shelter on.
A data center in Dresden cannot do that for Sodus.
How to read these numbers · Section 6 The $11.8 million is simple arithmetic: $17 a month × 12 months × about 58,000 households; the "$17" is the Ohio/Maryland figure from the PJM auction, applied here as a what-if. $10.7 million is 90% of that — the share of a household's supply that, if locally owned, is not exposed to the market shock.
The 30-year totals are the campaign's own planning model, built from public cost sources (Lazard, NREL, EIA, EEI, NRC, the American Public Power Association); they are illustrative estimates, not engineering quotes , every input is adjustable in the accompanying spreadsheet, and if the local mix needed much more winter storage or captured fewer local dollars the gaps narrow.
The brief says so because a number you cannot defend is worse than no number.
What does not change under any input is the direction: locally owned power cannot be outbid.
