Paid twice for the same fuel Here is how the waste bill lands on taxpayers.
The industry supported the Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, a bipartisan bill signed by President Reagan, which made the federal government responsible for taking every utility's spent fuel starting January 31, 1998.
In return, utilities collected a fee from their customers and paid it into a federal Nuclear Waste Fund — which now holds about $49.5 billion of ratepayer money.
125 126 When 1998 came and no repository existed, the same companies sued the government for breach of contract and won.
But the courts also ruled that the Waste Fund can be spent only on building a repository, so the damages come instead from the U.S.
Treasury's Judgment Fund — ordinary taxpayer money: $12.2 billion paid so far, $500–800 million more every year, and $38–45 billion projected as long as the fuel sits where it is.
44 127 Ratepayers paid once into a fund that can't be touched; taxpayers pay again, every year, for the same casks — and every new reactor adds to a pile that has no destination.
That is the contract the nuclear industry wrote, and it is why "the government will take the waste" should never again be accepted as an answer.

How to read these numbers · Section 5 — what the money buys MW (megawatts) is capacity — the size of the tap.
GWh (gigawatt-hours, 1,000 MWh) is energy actually delivered over a year — how much came out.
The two connect through capacity factor: MW × 8,760 hours × capacity factor = MWh/yr.
“Homes” assumes 7,500 kWh per home per year.
For storage , MW is how fast it can discharge and MWh is how long: 750 MW / 3,000 MWh is four hours at full power.
Every “$1B buys” figure is the sum of money ÷ the cost to build one megawatt (for example, $4B ÷ $1.6M per MW ≈ 2,500 MW) — hardware and construction only, before land, grid connection, permitting and operating costs — so treat it as a rule of thumb for scale, not a budget.
Job-years are one person employed for one year: 2,000 job-years could be 1,000 people for two years.
Permanent jobs are ongoing positions.
Peak construction headcount (Vogtle's 9,000) is the busiest month, not the average.
Job figures per MW come from national and international studies and are ranges for a reason — local wages, union rules and project size all move them.
The $526B storage market is a private forecaster's projection; the deployment figures from BloombergNEF and IEA are measured.

Where Butch stands: a distributed energy solution Public energy development is already on the platform.
What it means in practice is a distributed energy solution : instead of one $20-billion plant owned by a corporation or a state authority two hundred miles away, hundreds of smaller generators owned right here — solar on school roofs, town halls, barns and water plants; community solar arrays our neighbors subscribe to; digesters on our dairy farms; batteries at the substation, in our homes, on our farms, in our schools and in our town and county buildings; heat pumps in our basements.

In parallel: buy the wires.
Generation the public owns still pays RG&E to deliver it unless the public owns the poles and wires too.
Massena did exactly that: after three referendums (2-to-1, then 3-to-1, then 5-to-1), a $10 million bond and seven years of court fights with Niagara Mohawk, the town took over its distribution system in May 1981.

Let every resident produce — and get paid Right now a Wayne County homeowner, farm or school that wants to make more power than it uses runs into a wall of rules the utilities asked for.
New York caps a home system at 25 kW and a farm system on a residential meter at 100 kW.

Put solar over the parking lots The 130th has thousands of acres of asphalt — school lots, plazas along Ridge Road and Route 104, county and town buildings, the Webster and Wayne Central campuses, church lots, park-and-rides.
A canopy over a parking lot makes power, shades cars in July, keeps snow off them in January, carries the EV chargers, and uses no farmland .
New York has already proved it: JFK's new carport is 12 MW with a 7.5 MW battery, feeding 6 MW of community solar to low-income Queens households; the Rochester airport put a 75,000-square-foot canopy over its lot in 2017; White Plains has 6.8 MW over four garages; Maryknoll in Ossining runs 874 kW over 12 canopies.
108 109 110 Elsewhere, Rutgers covers its lots with 23 MW and calls the projects "cash-positive from the get-go"; Michigan State's 10.5 MW over 5,000 spaces saves more than $10 million over 25 years and was built specifically to keep panels off farmland; the Cincinnati Zoo's two canopies save it $300,000 a year.
111 112 113 France now requires canopies on every lot over about 1,500 square meters — 7 to 11 GW of new capacity — and a Yale study found Connecticut's parking lots alone could supply 37% of that state's electricity.
114 115 Canopies cost more per watt than a field ($3.62/W versus $2.96, per EnergySage) but they pay for something a field can't: grid capacity where the load is.
116 Con Ed's Brooklyn-Queens program spent $200 million on local solar, storage and efficiency to defer a billion-dollar substation; NYSEG and RG&E's own Stillwater battery put off a transformer upgrade by ten years.
117 118 A canopy with a battery at a Webster plaza or a Wayne County school is exactly that kind of "non-wires alternative" — it adds capacity to our end of the grid without a rate case.
Albany's job: pass S8696 (canopies on 75% of SUNY parking by 2040) and A4701 (canopies on new state-funded lots), extend NY-Sun's canopy adder — currently offered only downstate — to upstate, and let NYPA finance canopies on school and municipal lots owned by the districts and towns that host them.

120 They matter here because a conventional turbine shuts down at about 56 mph — right at the gale-to-storm line off Lake Ontario — while these designs are engineered to keep running through weather that idles blades.
