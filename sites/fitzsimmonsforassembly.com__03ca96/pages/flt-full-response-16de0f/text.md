Duplicate departments in Albany Closing thoughts Question 1 What do you think is the number one problem that New York State is facing as 2027 approaches, and stepping away from partisan politics, what would you propose to do to begin to solve it?
Cost of living, and the reason behind it: too much of what working families and small businesses pay goes out of our communities to a few large corporations that set the price.
Health insurance, electricity and food all follow the same pattern.
When I knock on doors, Republicans, Democrats and independents tell me the same thing: follow the money.
My answer has no party label.
Keep more of our money here.
Pay less and get more.
Pass the New York Health Act so families, employers, school districts and towns stop paying insurance-company overhead.
Build locally owned, distributed energy so our towns, schools, farms and co-ops produce their own power and keep the savings.
And make sure farmers get a fair price for what they grow.
I do not take corporate money, so I can work on these problems without owing anyone who profits from them.

Back to top ↑ Question 2 Many people complain because they say New York is overly regulated, which discourages business from either locating or expanding here.
What do you see the government being able to do, or change, to make New York more business-friendly?
Please be specific.
I own a small business and hold several patents and software innovations, so I know the paperwork side of this firsthand.
At the same time, my family and I have had the opportunity over many years to travel across our nation, and we have seen firsthand the value of what New York State has built: schools, colleges, libraries, parks, sidewalks and much more that truly make a difference.
That is part of why people want to live and do business here.
The goal is to cut the costs that hold businesses back without giving up what makes New York worth investing in.
Here are specific changes I would push for: Take health insurance off the employer's back.
Health care is one of the largest and least predictable costs a small employer carries.
Under the New York Health Act, a small business no longer has to shop for, negotiate and administer a health plan every year, which frees up valuable resources for what it does best: running the business.
That holds true for businesses of every size, and for the unions that fill those businesses with skilled labor.
It also puts small firms on equal footing with large ones when hiring.
And because coverage no longer depends on a job, people who are afraid of losing their health insurance will no longer be kept from starting a business of their own or taking a job they believe is better for them.
That is real freedom.
Remove the rules that stop homes, farms, schools and businesses from producing more power than they use.
Today Albany's rules hold them back.
I would support no usage cap at all, and pay surplus power at the retail rate minus only the community's actual cost of metering and billing.
A barn roof or a parking lot canopy becomes a second income.
This works best through local nonprofit or municipal utilities, where the profits return to the community instead of showing up as a corporate markup on every monthly bill.
More at fitzsimmonsforassembly.com/energy/ .
Right to Repair for farm equipment.
Farmers should be allowed to fix their own very expensive machines.
I would put farm equipment back into New York's Digital Fair Repair Act.
Fair pricing for farms.
Distributors and packers control what our onion and apple growers are paid.
Some growers are paid about what they were paid decades ago.
I would work for fair prices on what farms sell and fair costs on what they buy.
Make data centers pay their own way and close the loophole for existing facilities, so local businesses and households are not outbid for their own power.
Being business-friendly should mean being friendly to the businesses that already live here and spend here, not just to the largest out-of-state players.
Back to top ↑ Question 3 What do you see as the government's role in making health care more affordable for New Yorkers?
How do you ensure that services are offered equally, or is that even the job of government?
Yes, it is the job of government, the same way roads, water, police, fire protection and Medicare are, to name a few.
Our current system is not a free market.
Here in Wayne County, "the market has spoken": the Wolcott hospital closed decades ago, Myers in Sodus was sold and then closed, and now Newark is losing services the same way.
Pregnant mothers have to drive to Rochester or Canandaigua to give birth.
That is not equal access.
All it does is create uncertainty.
I support the New York Health Act, a single-payer plan that covers every New Yorker.
Coverage goes with you from job to job.
It removes premiums, deductibles and surprise bills, and it cuts the paperwork that doctors and hospitals spend so much on.
A RAND analysis found it would lower long-run costs.
To be fair about that study, RAND assumes every needed federal waiver is granted.
Many unions now support the bill.
Perhaps just as important, it takes away the leading cause of personal bankruptcy.
Studies have found that medical bills are the top cause of bankruptcy in this country, and roughly three out of four of those families had health insurance.
That insurance most certainly did not work for them.
Those families' savings were wiped out at the same time their loved ones were suffering.
That is not the mark of a great society.
It also helps property taxpayers.
The NYHA reduces what your school district and town pay for employee health coverage, and every dollar of that comes straight off the property-tax levy.
In the near term, I will work with any local nonprofit group that wants to buy the Newark hospital and the empty Myers campus and reopen them as community nonprofit hospitals.
By putting our heads together, we can create solutions.
Back to top ↑ Question 4 What about energy costs?
New York has paused implementation of its All-Electric Buildings Act and electric school buses.
Do you think the act is realistic; and if you don't, what do you propose as an alternative?
The goals are right, but a mandate that arrives before families, builders and school districts can afford it is not realistic.
One of my platform priorities is realistic state mandates.
Albany should not hand rural districts a bill for buses, chargers and grid upgrades they cannot pay for and then blame them when they fall behind.
My alternative, which already has many working examples across the state and the country, is to lower the cost of power first, then let electrification follow because it saves people money: A distributed energy plan: locally owned, public energy run by towns, schools, co-ops and municipal utilities like Fairport and Spencerport, with NYPA acting as builder and financier at public bond rates, well below bank rates.
Upstate farmers taught us this lesson nearly a century ago.
When private utilities would not run lines to rural areas, Congress created the Rural Electrification Administration and farmers formed co-ops, so electricity finally reached our farms and villages.
Let homes, farms, schools and businesses produce as much as their roofs, barns and parking lots allow, with no cap, and get paid fairly for the surplus.
Solar canopies over parking lots, which keep the sun off your car in the summer and the snow off it in the winter.
Small farming between and under solar rows so young families can stay on the land.
Hydropower, pilots of the new propeller-less wind units, and renewable natural gas from our farms and public wastewater treatment plants, all creating revenue streams for our communities.
Batteries are the next big growth market for the world, and we have a world-class battery facility in the district.
Investing there can help us lead in a global market expected to reach hundreds of billions of dollars a year within the next decade.
For municipal ownership, follow the example of Massena and buy back the wires.
Make data centers and crypto miners pay their own way.
Greenidge on Seneca Lake shows what happens when public money is spent on a power plant and the power goes elsewhere.
Compare that with paying for private nuclear plants through ratepayer subsidies and state subsidies, which are taxpayer money as well.
A new nuclear plant can take 10 to 15 years before it produces any electricity.
After a plant is decommissioned, we keep paying millions of dollars a year to store and monitor the spent-fuel casks for a century or more.
That means our children's children's children will pay for the nuclear decisions we make today, without getting any electricity from them, and hoping none of those nuclear casks ever fails.
That model gives us privatized gains and socialized losses.
Wind, solar, hydro, geothermal, biomass and battery storage, by contrast, can be up and running within a year or two, with no radioactive waste left for future generations.
Read the full plan at fitzsimmonsforassembly.com/energy/ .
Back to top ↑ Question 5 The interests of Upstate and Downstate, of urban and rural areas, of agriculture and the environment, can be divisive.
How would you, as a legislator, work to find compromise, to possibly break the gridlock that prevents real progress from being accomplished?
I start where people already agree.
I have served on our Planning Board, as an assistant Boy Scout leader, as a youth sports coach and as a Science Olympiad coach and regional judge.
In none of those roles did anyone ask my party first.
I am also a U.S.
Army veteran of the 82nd Airborne, where you work beside whoever is next to you.
Many of the divides in the question are false choices.
Agrivoltaics, small farming under solar panel rows, is good for farmers and the environment.
Protecting Lake Ontario (yes, Lake Ontario) protects tourism, fishing and property values, not to mention our fresh water.
A single-payer health plan helps a farmer in Wayne County, teachers in Webster and a nurse in the Bronx the same way.
When a proposal saves money for both sides, the solutions should be easy to find.
Gridlock is often about who funds the campaigns, not what voters want or need.
I take no corporate money, and many Republican voters in this district have told me they will vote for me.
My job is to represent all of them, and I will work with any legislator from any region who will move these solutions forward for the benefit of our communities.
Back to top ↑ Question 6 School districts in this region largely fall at the bottom of the rankings for competency in English and Math.
Why do you think that is, and how could state policies change to improve our test scores?
Test scores follow what is happening at home and in the district budget.
Our rural districts carry high costs per student, long bus routes, and families under financial strain.
When health insurance premiums rise every year, as they have for Webster CSD and are coming to Wayne County, that money comes out of teachers, classrooms and programs, or out of taxpayers' pockets, or both.
These money pressures are pushing school districts to look at merging, which only creates longer bus rides.
Many people who see the problem have approached me, and we have discussed a county school district that could significantly reduce costs without losing our existing local schools.
There is certainly more to discuss here.
As with health care, I will join these discussions so we can preserve the fabric of the 130th and reduce costs without giving up vital educational needs, while seeking to expand them.
State policy can help by lowering the costs that crowd out learning.
The New York Health Act removes employee health premiums from school budgets.
Locally owned solar on school roofs and parking lots cuts energy bills, and surplus power can become a revenue source.
Those savings can go into reading and math support, smaller classes, and programs that keep kids engaged.
As a youth sports coach and Science Olympiad mentor, coach and regional judge, I have seen what our students can do when they are challenged and get hands-on learning from adults who invest time in them.
I would push for improved state support for STEM programs, tutoring and after-school activities in rural districts, including bringing the Science Olympiad curriculum into daily school activities, and for school funding that reflects the real cost of educating students across a large rural area.
Years ago I was fortunate to join the Sodus Central junior high Science Olympiad effort when two science teachers, both named Bill, had a good group of students.
I volunteered to run the technology projects when one of our sons entered the sixth grade.
What happened over the next couple of years was remarkable: the Sodus team was ranked among the top 20 schools in the state.
So I know firsthand what good leadership, challenge and proper support can do.
There are solutions.
One of the largest factors working its way through our schools now is early intervention.
Funding for early intervention and preschool programs for our youngest children has been cut in half over the past several years.
Many of those children live here in Wayne County, and the staff who gave them that early help, most of them women, have also been cut in half, while the need keeps rising.
Without that help, too many of these children are heading toward what has been called the school-to-prison pipeline.
That is not the mark of a great society.
The health care savings I have described can be used to restore these programs.
Combine that with the county district solution, and we can make even more changes that help students learn and grow.
The State Education Department has outlined a shift to less teaching to the test and more hands-on learning, and it is starting to phase in this year.
My experience as a youth sports coach and with Science Olympiad tells me this is the right direction.
Together, these changes will help bring our schools back, and New York can show the country once again why we are the Empire State.
Back to top ↑ Question 7 Democrats and Republicans in the Assembly and Senate have their own separate research, human resources and IT departments that do not overlap.
What do you think about this practice?
Is it necessary; could it save money and would Albany be more bipartisan and transparent without it?
Or would chaos ensue?
If that is the case, duplicating HR and IT does not make sense.
Albany is the one place where all of our state's elected officials meet and work.
Unless someone can convince me otherwise, HR and IT are the same services whether they are delivered to the Assembly, the Senate or the Governor.
I work in IT and have consulted for much larger organizations, including work through AT&T for the Administrative Office of the U.S.
Courts.
AT&T brought me in because our software innovations, which installed every phone and voicemail account for the federal courts from the Supreme Court to the Courts of Appeals, significantly reduced errors, saving time and money.
The Administrative Office consolidated many IT and HR departments into one.
Each court location across the country had local staff, but all of them pointed back to that one HR and one IT department, and it worked.
I can see the need for sub-branches of HR or IT because of the number of people and offices, but not two separate entities.
Having seen that model work for the federal courts, I know it can work for New York.
If these really are separate operations, combining them should save money without hurting their effectiveness, provided they are staffed properly.
It is the same concept as a county school district.
Research is not much different.
Each conference may want its own policy staff, and that may be fair.
But the basic facts, like what a bill costs and who it affects, should come from one shared, nonpartisan source that both parties and the public can see.
It is through the exchange of ideas and data that we find solutions.
That is how you get transparency: everyone argues from the same numbers and facts, not opinions or ideologies.
We are supposed to be working toward the greater good, and that means the residents and taxpayers.
So if there are in fact separate entities, I would support a careful review, done in the open, to find where Albany can share services and where it should not.
Back to top ↑ In closing Closing thoughts I will close with this: an opportunity not provided is a loss for our future.
I have seen this in youth sports over the past two years, when we were fortunate to be invited to Cooperstown Dreams Park to watch Webster compete against teams from all across our nation.
Like Science Olympiad, it brought hundreds of young people together in one place, all going for it, and it was great to see.
To all the boys and girls I have had the chance to coach, and to the countless others playing today, I say this: once I am elected, I will get together with the players I coached and ask them to help create a Wayne County team that will travel to the Cooperstown Dreams Park this coming summer.
It will be built on the same fundamentals I taught years ago: hitting, running, throwing, pitching and catching.
And most important of all: team.
Not "me," but team.
When we work together as a team, that is when things become possible.
And there will always be room for someone with energy.
I know I can count on help from Webster to make this happen.
After all, we are in the same district.
There is a cost to this, and I believe we can find a solution for that as well: one where money does not decide who makes the team, and every player who earns a spot can go.
The whole point is to provide opportunity.
Too often, opportunity like this depends on your zip code, and where opportunity is missing, complacency and problems take root.
When we break down those barriers, we all benefit.
Then these young players can come home, share their experiences, create dreams and bring hope to others.
As I have told my players: you may never be the ball player you dream of being, but if you keep trying, you will most certainly get closer, and who knows, you just may make it.
