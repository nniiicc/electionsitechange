News / 2026 Legislative Update - Post Crossover Recap 22 Mar Sunday, 1:55 PM · 2026 2026 Legislative Update - Post Crossover Recap Last week, the House returned to the Gold Dome on Monday, March 9, following a long legislative day the friday before that stretched into the early hours of Saturday morning.
By the end of the week, the Georgia General Assembly reached Legislative Day 31, meaning that only nine legislative days remain in the 2026 legislative session.
Following a very busy Crossover Day, the House turned its attention to reviewing Senate bills that our counterparts passed out of their chamber, with some of those measures reaching the House floor for a vote.
As we move closer to Sine Die on April 2, my House colleagues and I have continued working diligently during this ninth week of the legislative session to pass several measures, most importantly the Fiscal Year 2027 (FY 2027) budget.
Below, I will highlight several key components of the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget, as well as other important pieces of legislation that the House passed this week.
My colleagues and I hit a major milestone in our legislative work this week with the bipartisan passage of the FY 2027 budget .
Passing a balanced budget is the General Assembly’s sole constitutional responsibility, and the FY 2027 budget appropriates funding for state operations for the upcoming fiscal year, beginning July 1, 2026.
House Bill 974, the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget, is set by a revenue estimate of $38.5 billion, an increase of $738 million, or 1.95 percent, over the Fiscal Year 2026 (FY 2026) budget.
HB 974 makes significant investments in education, including fully funding the Quality Basic Education (QBE) program at a total of $14.9 billion in state funds, providing $5.5 million for the Special Needs Scholarship and allocating more than $60 million to a statewide literacy initiative.
The House’s version of the FY 2027 budget also addresses key areas such as Medicaid, providing funding for the state’s correctional system, fully funding the state’s pension obligations, supporting economic development and continuing to make noteworthy investments for Georgia’s transportation needs.
Education represents the largest area of state spending each year, and the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget reflects this priority through targeted investments, particularly in early education.
A central focus in our budget is early childhood literacy, with appropriations that would go toward implementing K-3 literacy coaches in Georgia schools—an approach supported by research showing that reading proficiency by the end of third grade is strongly linked to future academic achievement and life success.
To advance this goal, the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget includes more than $60 million to support a statewide literacy initiative aimed at improving reading outcomes for students in kindergarten through third grade.
This includes $31.2 million in newly appropriated QBE funds to provide school systems with the resources needed to implement elementary-based literacy coaches in schools across the state.
The remaining funds needed to support the newly hired coaches will be distributed to school systems through the amended budget.
The House’s version of the FY 2027 budget also includes $18.4 million to repurpose Regional Education Service Agencies (RESA)-based literacy coaches.
Together, these investments support the implementation and objectives of House Bill 1193, the Georgia Early Literacy Act of 2026, passed by the House earlier this legislative session.
Recognizing that a child cannot learn to read if they cannot see or hear properly, HB 974 includes $2.7 million for the Department of Education to provide a one-time grant of $15,000 to each school system for the purchase of vision and auditory screening equipment to support annual screenings for students in kindergarten through third grade.
This initiative is also outlined in House Bill 1402, legislation passed by the House to provide these screenings at school.
To further support this effort, the House also unanimously adopted House Resolution 145 this week, which would urge the Department of Education and the Department of Public Health (DPH) to collaborate and prioritize the expansion of vision and hearing screenings at various grade levels to further assist in identifying students with undiagnosed or untreated vision or hearing problems.
HB 974 includes $7.9 million for the Governor’s Office of Student Achievement (GOSA) to enable GOSA to review and provide recommendations on universal reading screeners, recommend high-quality instructional literacy materials for pre-K through third grade, offer professional learning development for teachers, literacy coaches and administrators and establish a new director of literacy—all to support the implementation of the Georgia Early Literacy Act of 2026.
Additionally, HB 974 provides $527,966 for the Professional Standards Commission (PSC) to hire four program specialists, funding that is also tied to our work on the Georgia Early Literacy Act of 2026.
When children enter kindergarten already behind in early language and literacy development, catching up becomes significantly more difficult, making those first years of learning especially critical.
To help ensure more children begin school on track and ready to learn, the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget provides $9.7 million to the Department of Early Care and Learning (DECAL) for the expansion of the extended day program for the pre-K priority group.
This funding would align with House Bill 1123, legislation the House passed earlier this session, which would expand access to after-school programs for pre-K students to better support working families.
This appropriation would fully fund extended day services with lottery funds, providing aftercare and school holiday care for 2,448 pre-K students.
By shifting the funding source to lottery funds, $9.7 million in federal funds would be freed up to expand the Child and Parent Services (CAPS) program, which would assist low-income families with the cost of childcare, to an additional 1,288 children.
In addition, HB 974 includes $700,000 for DECAL to expand the rising pre-K program by adding 25 new classes during the 2026-2027 school year and nearly $4 million to provide five days of leave for pre-K lead and assistant teachers.
Together, these investments reflect the House’s priorities to strengthen early learning opportunities and help ensure that more children begin kindergarten prepared to learn to read, setting them on a path toward long-term academic success.
The House’s commitment to education extends beyond literacy, with additional appropriations aimed at supporting Georgia’s economically disadvantaged students.
Last year’s FY 2026 budget included $15.2 million for a poverty grant program that provided assistance to students in 59 school systems.
However, while children experiencing poverty live in every school system across the state, not all systems received additional resources through the program.
In response, the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget replaces the FY 2026 poverty grant with targeted investments that would provide support to every school system, while addressing many of the same underlying needs.
The original poverty grant was intended to help address essential areas of support that impact a student’s ability to learn, such as nutrition and transportation.
To help meet these needs, HB 974 includes $1 million for the Department of Education’s Nutrition Program and an additional $1 million for the Department of Human Services (DHS) to begin implementation of SUN Bucks, a new summer nutrition program for economically disadvantaged students.
This combined $2 million investment would allow the state to draw down significant matching funds from the U.S.
Department of Agriculture to help cover administrative costs of the program.
The House’s FY 2027 budget also addresses transportation challenges for economically disadvantaged students by providing $41.1 million in additional funding for school transportation based on updated bus and mileage data, representing an 11 percent increase over FY 2026 funding.
In addition, HB 974 increases funding for school social worker grants with $2.6 million to help support underserved districts that currently do not have access to a social worker, as well as $1.3 million for mental health support grants, bringing the total funding for the program to $5 million.
The House’s version of the FY 2027 budget also adds an additional $2.5 million for the BOOST 2.0 program for statewide and community grantees to expand afterschool and summer programming opportunities for students, bringing the total appropriation for the program to $15 million.
With respect to school personnel and the State Health Benefit Plan (SHBP), the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget reduces the governor’s recommended increase to the SHBP employer contribution per-member-per-month (PMPM) rate from $2,028 to $1,935.
Over the past four years, the SHBP employer contribution for non-certified school personnel has increased from $945 to $1,885 PMPM, placing added pressure on local school systems’ budgets.
The governor’s proposal for FY 2027 would increase the SHBP employer contribution for teachers and other school personnel by $143 PMPM, generating approximately $207 million to help address the unfunded liability associated with future retiree health insurance benefits through the Other Post-Employment Benefit (OPEB) Plan.
Under the QBE formula, the PMPM increase for teachers would be largely funded by the state, whereas the increase for non-certified school personnel would be borne entirely by local school systems.
While the House recognizes the need for continued, measured contributions to the OPEB Plan, the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget recommends a smaller increase of $50 PMPM, rather than $143 PMPM.
This approach would still contribute to addressing long-term retiree liabilities, while reducing the financial impact on local school system budgets.
HB 974 also reflects the House’s strong commitment to supporting higher education with investments that would strengthen Georgia’s public universities, expand research capacity and develop the state’s workforce.
To that end, the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget includes $4.8 million in additional funds for existing member institutions and to extend Georgia Research Alliance (GRA) membership to Kennesaw State University and Georgia Southern University.
GRA, a nonprofit public-private partnership, plays a critical role in recruiting Eminent Scholars to Georgia’s universities, bringing world-class talent that drives innovation and economic growth in our state.
In support of this mission, HB 974 provides $3 million in one-time state matching funds for three new Eminent Scholar endowments and allocates $15.7 million in capital funds for specialty lab equipment and infrastructure, ensuring Georgia’s researchers would have the tools necessary to advance cutting-edge discoveries.
The House’s budget also invests $9.5 million to fund start-up costs for the University of Georgia School of Medicine, established in 2024, with its inaugural class of 60 students expected to begin in Fall 2026.
These investments are part of a broader strategy to grow Georgia’s healthcare workforce and expand access to medical education across the state.
Additionally, HB 974 provides $6 million for continued investment in the Behavioral Health Professions Service Cancelable Loan program, which was created through House Bill 1013, the Mental Health Parity Act, that was passed during the 2022 legislative session.
The goal of this program is to attract and retain behavioral health professionals in Georgia and has already awarded $10 million to these students since its first disbursement in 2023.
Lastly in higher education, HB 974 includes an additional $450,000 to enhance security at five state technical colleges in Georgia, reaffirming the House’s commitment to making school safety a top priority, even among our higher education institutions.
Another vital area of the state budget each year addresses Georgia’s healthcare needs.
In the House’s FY 2027 budget, we prioritize improved access to care by allocating approximately $45.3 million to increase reimbursement rates for providers who serve Medicaid patients across the state.
These Medicaid reimbursement investments include $11.8 million for dental care, $7.6 million for primary care physicians and $3.9 million for psychiatric residential treatment.
Additional investments would support autism services by directing $709,000 to help train parents of children with autism and $7.9 million to ensure parity between in-home and in-office reimbursement for autism care.
Furthermore, the House allocates $1.6 million for adult heart and lung transplants, $1.4 million to increase the prospective payment rate for federally qualified health centers and $1.3 million to establish a rate floor for durable medical equipment, prosthetics, orthotics and supplies.
Additionally, HB 974 provides $600,000 for the Elderly and Disabled Waiver program, $574,000 for air ambulance reimbursement, $512,000 to increase the nursing home ventilators reimbursement rate, $352,000 for privately owned intermediate care facilities serving individuals with intellectual disabilities and $276,000 for registered dietitians.
Together, these investments aim to ensure that providers who care for Medicaid patients across the state have the resources needed to continue serving their patients.
As part of additional Medicaid investments outlined in HB 974, the House’s budget supports the governor’s recommendation to provide $4.6 million to the Department of Behavioral Health and Developmental Disabilities to add 100 New Option Waiver Program and Comprehensive Support Waiver Programs—Medicaid waiver programs for eligible Georgians with intellectual and/or developmental disabilities—as well as $513,558 for waiver staffing.
The House also allocates $6.69 million to DHS for memory care and treatment, home delivered meals, respite, caregiver support and other services for older adults and their families.
Furthermore, the House agrees with the governor’s recommendation to address rising costs in foster care services and appropriates an additional $27 million to the Division of Family & Children Services, bringing the total appropriations to $48.3 million.
Last year, my colleagues and I received testimony from DHS where we learned of the rising cost of foster care services, and HB 974 would ensure that the state can continue supporting these critical services for children and families across Georgia.
Continuing our investments in healthcare, the House’s budget allocates $18.2 million in new funding for graduate medical education, including $4.7 million to create 147 slots in primary care medicine and $1.9 million to create 13 new fellowship slots.
These investments aim to strengthen Georgia’s healthcare workforce and help meet the growing demand for physicians across the state.
Recognizing the critical need for healthcare access in rural Georgia, HB 974 provides $3 million for one-time grants for emergency preparedness in rural areas, $3 million for rural hospital stabilization grants to include rural emergency hospitals and $2 million to develop and implement a solvency evaluation process for rural hospitals.
By directing these funds to rural healthcare initiatives, the House’s budget would help bridge gaps in access to care for underserved communities.
Our FY 2027 budget also includes $6.9 million to expand the DPH maternal home visiting program to provide coverage to an additional 62 counties, which would guarantee that every rural county has access to this program that supports Georgia families living in communities at-risk for poor maternal and child health outcomes.
In further support of maternal health, the House allocates $2 million to the Office of Cardiac Care to expand access to cardiac care services statewide as it is a leading cause of maternal mortality.
The House is focused on ensuring that all citizens have access to quality care and support services in every corner of our state, and these funds would help accomplish that goal.
In the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget, we also make substantial investments to strengthen public safety across Georgia.
The House provides the Department of Corrections with a total increase of $101 million over the agency’s prior year budget.
Since Fiscal Year 2025, the General Assembly has worked to provide funds to improve recruitment and retention of correctional officers after the state lost nearly half of its workforce during the pandemic.
As a result of these contributions, the department now has 300 more correctional officers than it did in June 2024.
Building on this recent progress to improve safety within our prison system, the House budget includes $34.9 million to support continued hiring and reduce the inmate-to-correctional officer ratio to 1:12 in FY 2027, with a goal of reaching 1:11 by the Fiscal Year 2028 budget cycle.
HB 974 also appropriates $70 million in additional funds to ensure that the department can continue providing inmates with necessary physical, mental, dental and pharmaceutical care.
With the state’s prison population now exceeding 50,000 inmates, demand for these services has increased significantly.
In further support of public safety and victims’ services, the House directs $20.7 million to the Criminal Justice Coordinating Council to expand grants and offset the loss of federal funding for victim service providers, including child advocacy centers, domestic violence shelters and sexual assault centers that serve some of Georgia’s most vulnerable citizens.
Last year alone, more than 21,000 children were served through child advocacy centers, while domestic violence shelters assisted more than 40,000 victims, with more than 15,000 individuals turned away because shelters reached capacity.
Sexual assault centers served 9,018 adult victims, performed 1,649 forensic medical exams and responded to 7,724 crisis calls last year.
To strengthen Georgia’s judicial system, the House’s budget would establish pay parity between assistant district attorneys and assistant public defenders by allocating $12.7 million to the Prosecuting Attorneys’ Council and the Georgia Public Defender Council to ensure salaries remain competitive and encourage talented individuals to pursue these critical roles.
Under this plan, both positions would have a starting salary of $78,000 effective January 1, 2027.
Additionally, HB 974 appropriates approximately $3.1 million to increase the salaries of district attorneys and public defenders to $175,000 beginning January 1, 2027.
Through these investments, the House remains committed to improving Georgia’s correctional system, supporting victims of crime and ensuring our state’s public safety and justice systems remain strong and able to effectively serve the citizens of our state.
To ensure that Georgia’s transportation network and critical infrastructure remain properly funded, the House’s FY 2027 budget includes an increase of $166 million over the original FY 2026 budget for various transportation initiatives.
This funding directs an additional $112.8 million for the Capital Construction program to support transportation construction projects, $33.5 million for Routine Maintenance to address rising maintenance costs and $13.6 million for Local Maintenance and Improvement Grants to assist local governments with resurfacing projects.
The House also provides an additional $10 million in airport aid, bringing the total base funding to nearly $40 million.
Currently, the list of state-funded airport aid requests exceeds $96 million, highlighting the continued demand for infrastructure improvements across the state and the need for these targeted investments.
By strategically funding our roads, bridges and airports, Georgia can maintain a safe, reliable and capable transportation network that drives economic growth and connects communities across the state.
Additional notable investments in the House’s version of the FY 2027 budget include $1 million to the Department of Veterans Affairs for the treatment of traumatic brain injuries and mental health conditions for veterans and active-duty military members.
This builds on a $1 million investment included in House Bill 973, the Amended Fiscal Year 2026 (AFY 2026) budget, demonstrating our ongoing commitment to those who have courageously and selflessly served our country.
Continuing the House’s work this session to protect Georgia’s farmlands through the passage of House Bill 946—which would allow hunters to trap and kill feral hogs without a hunting or trapping license—the House’s version of the budget provides $500,000 to the Department of Natural Resources (DNR) for a public-private feral hog management pilot program, along with $407,500 to the Soil and Water Conservation Commission to initiate a feral hog eradication incentive program.
As feral hogs continue to cause significant damage to agriculture—Georgia’s number one industry—this provision of the budget seeks to more effectively control feral hog populations, an invasive species that causes significant damage to agriculture and native wildlife, with estimated losses to Georgia farms totaling approximately $150 million annually.
Aside from our budget work this week, the House also overwhelmingly passed Senate Bill 220, the Putting Georgia’s Patients First Act, which would overhaul and modernize the state’s medical cannabis framework.
The legislation builds upon the foundation established by House Bill 324 in 2019, which authorized the Georgia Access to Medical Cannabis Commission to oversee the regulated licensing of in-state cultivation, production, manufacturing and sale of low-THC oil—which SB 220 would rename to medical cannabis to reflect its broader use and recognition as a treatment option for registered patients.
The House Blue-Ribbon Study Committee on Georgia’s Medical Marijuana and Hemp Policies played a central role in shaping this legislation, holding four bipartisan meetings across the state over several months in 2025.
During these meetings, the committee heard from more than 60 witnesses—including physicians, patients, law enforcement officials, representatives from the Georgia Access to Medical Cannabis Commission, the Georgia Department of Agriculture, DPH, industry leaders and the Georgia Composite Medical Board—which helped inform the recommendations ultimately reflected in SB 220.
Specifically, the bill would expand the forms of medical cannabis available to qualified patients to include vaporization, oils and raw plant materials.
Importantly, the legislation would clarify that smoking or combustion of medical cannabis is illegal but would permit vaporization as an alternative.
The legislation would also remove the five percent cap on THC oil products, which was the only permitted form of medical cannabis for many years, by shifting from percentage-based dosing to measured milligrams.
As medical understanding and treatment options have evolved, additional delivery methods have emerged that use milligram-based dosing to more closely aligns with how medications are typically administered in other areas of medicine.
SB 220 would also reduce the per-package cap from 1,400 milligrams to 1,200 milligrams to help ensure an adequate supply is available for patients statewide.
The bill would also add several new qualifying conditions for medical cannabis use, reflecting the growing recognition of patients who could benefit from this treatment.
Specifically, cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, HIV, lupus, severe arthritis and severe insomnia would be added to the current list of eligible conditions under this bill.
SB 220 would also require DPH to issue electronic or physical patient registration cards with a five-year expiration, helping to modernize access and documentation for patients across Georgia.
These changes would make medical cannabis more accessible and practical for patients across Georgia, helping ensure those suffering from serious health conditions can receive the treatment they need safely.
The House also focused on addressing the rising challenges in prescription drug costs and accessibility.
As these issues continue to affect patients, providers and pharmacies across Georgia, the House took steps to further examine the role of pharmacy benefits managers in the prescription drug marketplace with the adoption of House Resolution 948.
HR 948 would create the House Study Committee on Pharmacy Benefits Managers and Consumer Access to Prescription Medications to investigate rising drug costs and how pharmacy benefits managers impact costs.
Pharmacy benefits managers administer prescription drug insurance benefits, negotiate with drug manufacturers and pharmacies and determine which medications are covered through drug formularies and pharmacy networks.
However, the cost of prescription drugs has increased at rates exceeding inflation each year since 2016, raising concerns about affordability and transparency.
In 2019, Georgia passed the first pharmacy benefits manager steering law in the nation to prevent these entities from directing patients to their own affiliated pharmacies, but despite these efforts, steering practices still occur within the state.
Current Georgia law also requires pharmacy benefits managers to publicly report when drug prices exceed 10 percent above the national average drug acquisition cost.
These reports have revealed significant markups on certain medications for both commercial and government payers, including instances where medications used to treat serious conditions, such as multiple sclerosis, cancer and kidney disease, were priced much higher than the national average acquisition cost.
These concerns are particularly significant for rural communities across Georgia.
Many rural areas rely on a single independent pharmacy to serve an entire county or region, and in some cases, that pharmacy may be the only local access point for prescription medications and basic health services.
For these reasons, my House colleagues and I felt it was necessary to examine this issue more closely through the creation of this study committee.
Study committees play a crucial role in the legislative process by allowing us to dedicate time outside of session to study specific issues, providing an opportunity to examine facts and seek legislative solutions in the next legislative session.
The committee would meet throughout the 2026 interim in communities across the state to hear from stakeholders and members of the public and ultimately develop findings and recommendations to help address these concerns and ensure Georgians have reliable and affordable access to the medications they need.
Recognizing the urgent need to address the growing opioid crisis in Georgia, the House unanimously adopted House Resolution 1049, which would designate the last Wednesday in October each year as Fentanyl and Opioid Awareness Day in Georgia, acknowledging the growing opioid crisis in our state.
The resolution would align Georgia with the national Red Ribbon Campaign, held each October and recognized as the nation’s oldest and largest drug prevention initiative in the United States.
DPH reports opioid-related overdose deaths have more than doubled from 853 in 2019 to 1,718 in 2021, and more than half were attributed to fentanyl.
Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid that is 50 times more potent than heroin and 100 times more potent than morphine and is inexpensive, widely available and highly addictive.
This growing threat is particularly evident in Georgia, where fentanyl overdoses are rising among middle and high school students.
This resolution seeks to promote awareness, encourage schools to provide naloxone and overdose prevention resources and ensures steps are being taken to reduce opioid-related deaths, especially in younger populations.
The House also gave final passage to Senate Bill 420 to support Georgia’s coastal fishing industry, a bill similar to another piece of legislation the House passed during our third week of session, House Bill 443.
SB 420 would establish an annual fee for reef or migratory fish endorsements for Georgia recreational fishing licenses.
Requested by coastal fishermen, the measure is designed to improve oversight and management of both recreational and commercial offshore fishing.
Currently, Georgia relies primarily on federal surveys to assess fish populations.
These surveys evaluate fish stocks across the entire Atlantic coast and often do not accurately reflect conditions in Georgia waters.
SB 420 would set an annual fee of $10 for Georgia residents and $20 for non-residents who participate in offshore fishing, and all funds would be retained by DNR.
Revenue generated through the endorsement fees would allow the state to conduct its own fishery surveys in order to collect more precise data.
Overall, this legislation seeks to protect our coastal fishing industry and allow for more accurate data collection.
Now that the legislation has received final passage, it has now been sent to Governor Brian Kemp for consideration.
Lastly, Senate Bill 239 passed in the House this week and is designed to strengthen oversight and professional standards within Georgia’s funeral services industry.
The bill would give the Board of Funeral Services the authority to subpoena individuals to superior court if they are acting as unlicensed funeral directors and subpoena any witnesses necessary to carry out the board’s duties.
It would also expand the board to seven members, ensuring a mix of industry expertise with three licensed funeral directors, three licensed embalmers and one member with no connection to the industry, while also allowing an individual who is both a licensed embalmer and funeral director to serve on the board.
Additionally, SB 239 would raise the standards for licensure by requiring both embalmers and funeral directors to hold an associate’s degree, obtain a passing score on an examination approved by the board and complete 3,120 hours of apprenticeship training under the direct supervision of a licensed embalmer.
The legislation would also prohibit holding both a license as an embalmer and as a funeral director simultaneously, which would help to further professionalize the field and safeguard the public by ensuring oversight and penalties for misconduct.

Notable Legislation Passed During Crossover Day: House Bill 1000 - unanimously passed, returns money to hardworking Georgians and puts dollars directly back into the pockets of families across the state.
As part of Gov.
Kemp’s priorities funded in the AFY 2026 budget, this one-time income tax refund would be available to qualified taxpayers who filed individual income tax returns for both the 2024 and 2025 tax years by the applicable filing deadlines, including any granted extensions.
House Bill 1402 - passed overwhelmingly with bipartisan support and requires all public-school students in pre-K through third grade to receive annual vision and hearing screenings at school.
House Bill 1413 - passed with bipartisan support and would establish the Dedicating Resources to Educationally Advance More Students (DREAMS) Scholarship, a need-based scholarship program administered by the Georgia Student Finance Authority.
With $325 million appropriated for state-matching funds to support the need-based DREAMS scholarship program already included in the AFY 2026 budget, this scholarship would provide up to $3,000 per academic year, with a duration maximum of up to eight semesters or 12 quarters, to eligible undergraduate students enrolled in institutions within the University System of Georgia or the Technical College System of Georgia who demonstrate unmet financial need.
House Bill 1187, also known as Trey’s Law, which would prohibit the enforcement of any nondisclosure agreements (NDAs) or confidentiality agreements that seek to silence victims of childhood sexual abuse, passed out of the House.
The bill would void NDAs—including employment agreements, settlement agreements and other contracts—in situations where these agreements would prevent someone from discussing an act of childhood sexual abuse and any related facts about the abuse.
House Bill 1290, legislation aimed at strengthening protections for some of Georgia’s most vulnerable citizens—older adults and individuals with disabilities.
The bill passed the House unanimously and would revise the definition of sexual abuse as it relates to older adults by clarifying that the offense occurs when conduct is committed without consent, rather than requiring proof of coercion, while also ensuring that serious sexual offenders are properly classified and monitored.
House Bill 1283, also known as the Family Justice Center Act, passed unanimously.
The legislation would authorize the creation of Family Justice Centers—multiagency facilities that would provide coordinated services to victims of family violence, sexual assault, child abuse, elder abuse, human trafficking and related crimes.
House Bill 1230, passed with bipartisan support in an effort to strengthen security at Georgia’s correctional facilities by addressing unauthorized drone activity.
HB 1230 would prohibit the operation of unmanned aircraft systems over these facilities, and it would be illegal to allow another individual to do so from one’s property.
House Bill 1138, or the Increasing Access to Contraceptives Act, was passed with bipartisan support.
HB 1138 would increase access to contraception by making various birth control methods more readily available and financially accessible through a pharmacy.
HB 1118 would build upon legislation passed during the 2024 legislative session—House Bill 1010—which expanded paid parental leave for state employees.
HB 1118 would continue the House’s work to support working families by ensuring new mothers have meaningful time to recuperate following birth and care for their newborn.
The bill would provide eligible state employees with 120 hours of paid maternal birth leave to be used within the first three weeks following birth.
House Bill 1045, also known as the You Are Not Alone Awareness Act.
HB 1045 would require any local school system or public school that issues student identification badges to students in grades six through 12 to include the following message on badges: “9-8-8—You are not alone.
Anyone experiencing a suicidal crisis, emotional distress or challenges with substance abuse should call or text 9-8-8.
The hotline is available 24 hours a day, seven days a week, 365 days a year.” House Bill 1114, requires the State Board of Education to adopt age-appropriate financial literacy standards for students in kindergarten through grade 12.
Under the legislation, students in grades nine through 12 would receive instruction in personal budgeting and money management; banking, credit and debt; saving and investing principles; risk assessment and long-term financial planning; digital finance, fraud prevention and consumer protection; as well as the risks associated with gambling and an overview of the state’s gambling laws.
HB 1112 would establish a standardized cash-rounding system for purchases made using legal tender, requiring that the total cost of goods and services—including sales tax and any other applicable taxes—be rounded to the nearest five cents.
Under the bill, totals ending in one, two, six or seven cents would be rounded down to the nearest five cents, while totals ending in three, four, eight or nine cents would be rounded up to the nearest five cents.
These rounding adjustments would not affect the actual sales price of a purchase or the calculation of any taxes owed.
House Bill 1122 passed with bipartisan support, which would address peripheral artery disease (PAD) and expand access to preventative vascular care.
HB 1122 would expand access to screening PAD, a serious circulatory condition that affects blood flow to the lower extremities and can lead to amputations if left undetected.
The bill would require insurance coverage for PAD screenings for at-risk individuals between the ages of 50 and 64, helping ensure more Georgians have access to early detection and preventative care.
House Resolution 1000, setting the stage for Georgia’s voters to consider a constitutional amendment that would support the state’s timber industry.
The resolution comes at a necessary time, as the state continues to rebuild in the wake of Hurricane Helene and recent impacts of several pulp and paper mill closures in the Southeast.
HR 1000 would allow standing timber to be assessed at a zero percent ad valorem tax, provided the timberland is placed under a qualifying-use covenant.
House Bill 668, a measure designed to strengthen protections for service dogs and the Georgians who rely on them.
HB 668 would update state law to formally recognize service dogs and establish criminal penalties and fines for individuals who interfere with or cause physical harm to a service dog, while also extending protections to their trainers and owners.
