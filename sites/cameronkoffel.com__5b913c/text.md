Both parties raise money off the fight between them, so neither has much reason to end it.
Running without one changes who I answer to — and it changes how I have to see you.
You're a person, not a data point Campaigns buy voter files that score you — party lean, turnout likelihood, which issue is supposed to move you.
I don't have a party file to work from, so the only way I find out what you think is to knock on your door and ask.
That's slower.
It's also the entire point.
Nobody hands you a label Pick a party and you get sorted, and from then on everything you believe gets read through that sort — by campaigns, by your neighbors, sometimes by your own family.
There's no label next to my name, so there's nothing to sort you into for voting for me.
Most people don't fit in one box You can want your property taxes capped and your schools fully funded.
You can think the state gives too much away to data centers and that a police camera needs a warrant.
Those aren't contradictions.
They're just what people actually think.
A party asks you to trade some of it away to stay on the team.
Nobody tells me how to vote There's no leadership deciding which bills I'm allowed to support or how I vote on your property taxes.
On every bill, the only people I answer to are the ones who live in District 7.
I can work with anyone I'm not on either team, so I don't need permission to sign onto a good bill from either side.
The license plate privacy bill is moving right now because a Republican and a Democrat are pushing it together.
That's the job.
You have to look for the name Most voters scan a ballot for a party label.
Next to my name there isn't one.
On November 3, you're looking for Cameron Koffel — that's the whole trick to it.
