About Travis
Hi, I'm Travis
I'm Travis Jack Stevens, and I'm running for U.S.
Senate in Delaware as a write-in.
My name won't be printed on your ballot.
On November 3, I'm asking you to write it in yourself.
I live paycheck to paycheck in a one-bedroom apartment with my dog, Aurora, and my kittens, Leo and Luna.
I recorded my campaign introduction video on my lunch break, and that's still how this campaign runs: on a phone, with no Super PAC and no party machine.
Where I come from
I was born in Hagerstown, Maryland, and grew up on a farmhouse porch just over the line in Welsh Run, Pennsylvania.
I'm the middle of three boys in a working-class family.
My parents met in the Navy.
My dad spent twenty years as a correctional officer at the Hagerstown prison, and my mom was a freight dispatcher.
At seven I got a piano for Christmas and found out I could play by ear.
When I was eight, a bad case of mono led to lesions on my brain.
During a biopsy at the University of Maryland, I had a stroke.
I spent weeks in a coma and woke up unable to speak, then spent months relearning to walk and talk.
It took about three years to get my speech back, but I didn't quit.
A nurse who cared for me in that hospital is the reason I became a nurse.
I wanted to pay it forward.
I came out when I was thirteen.
I paid for it.
I was kicked out of more than one school, and opportunities were taken from me.
I kept going anyway.
As a teenager I bused tables at a crab house in Halfway, Maryland, to help my mom with the bills.
I learned early that an institution can decide you're inconvenient, and that you can outlast it.
Years at the bedside
Massage therapy was my first career out of high school, meant to pay for medical school, until the Great Recession took that work away.
So I became a nurse.
I worked my way up the ladder the long way: floor nursing first, then step-down, then critical care.
At Meritus Medical Center in Hagerstown, a patient's family nominated me for the DAISY Award for extraordinary nurses, and I won.
"Travis has a wonderful bedside manner and is very professional.
He stood out among many of the nurses we have come across over the years."
"He definitely is a role model… He was a blessing through our experience."
I spent years as a travel ICU nurse, four years in all.
My first year was in ICUs at several hospitals in Las Vegas.
Then I spent three years in the ICU at David Grant Medical Center on Travis Air Force Base in California, a Department of Defense hospital.
I cared for service members and their families, people who had put themselves on the line for this country.
Serving them was an honor.
In 2019 the base commander awarded me a commander's coin for reporting a mistake I had made, even though speaking up put me at risk.
Owning my errors is how I was trained to keep patients safe, and it's the same accountability I'll demand in the Senate, starting with my own.
After that, a travel assignment brought me to Delaware, as a recovery-room (PACU) nurse at Beebe Hospital.
I'd loved Delaware since childhood vacations, and I made my home here.
When the pandemic hit, I kept my Delaware home and spent about eight months in the COVID unit at Cooper University Hospital in Camden, New Jersey.
Nurses held up phones so families could say goodbye to patients they weren't allowed to visit.
COVID broke something in me, and I left the bedside.
Leaving felt like failing.
It wasn't.
Good nurses around me left too, because the system asked more than people can give.
At the bedside, I watched people skip medications, put off a diagnosis, and go into debt over one ER visit.
That's a big part of why I'm running.

Starting over
This wasn't my first time rebuilding.
The recession took my first career, and the pandemic nearly ended my second.
So I went back to massage therapy here in Rehoboth, and that's my work now.
I know you can build again.
My neighbors
I live near Rehoboth Beach, where I put myself back together.
I sing baritone with CAMP Rehoboth Chorus, and I still play piano.
I've volunteered with local nonprofits and helped organize a rally in Rehoboth Beach for International Women's Day, and I kept showing up.
I help guide Benevolent Community Initiative circles with my mentor Lori Ann — neighbors building communities worthy of our shared humanity.
Why I'm running
This campaign started in a room where I wasn't allowed to ask a question.
Senator Chris Coons was speaking.
I wanted to ask him about the Iran war and the billions in military aid to Israel he voted for in 2024, and about the money his campaign has raised from AIPAC's PAC and its donors, and from defense-contractor PACs.
I was told I couldn't.
The message was that we have to be nice to those in power.
I don't believe that.
Standing up to people in power, politely and without fear, is the whole point of a democracy.
If a constituent can't ask a senator a hard question, somebody needs to ask it from the ballot.
So I decided to challenge him myself.
Getting your name printed on the ballot for U.S.
Senate here costs a $10,000 filing fee.
For someone living paycheck to paycheck, that's out of reach.
I didn't think anyone should have to pay it for the right to run.
So I filed as a declared write-in.
The Delaware Department of Elections lists me as a Qualified write-in candidate for U.S.
Senator, which means your write-in vote for me is counted and reported.
Since then I've written more than a hundred daily letters to Delaware.
What I'll fight for
- 1 Justice and accountability.
No one is above the law, including a president.
I want full, independent investigations into possible crimes and self-enrichment in this administration.
Then the facts and the courts decide.
- 2 Ending the wars.
Congress, not the president, decides when this country goes to war.
I'll vote to end U.S. involvement in the Iran war and to stop sending weapons that kill civilians in Gaza.
- 3 Regulating AI.
AI is being rolled out with almost no rules.
David Robinson, who led the writing of OpenAI's safety reports, wrote on October 3: "If this is the situation, then the time for trial and error is over." I'll fight for independent safety testing before release, legal liability when products cause harm, notice and retraining when jobs are replaced, and power bills paid by the companies rather than families.
I won't take a dime from AI or tech companies or their executives.
- 4 Health care and housing for everyone.
The richest Americans and the biggest corporations should pay their fair share to help pay for it.
Who pays for this campaign
This campaign is people-funded.
I don't take Super PAC money.
I've also made a pledge: I won't take a dime from AI or tech companies or their executives.
When I vote on how to regulate AI, I won't owe them anything.
