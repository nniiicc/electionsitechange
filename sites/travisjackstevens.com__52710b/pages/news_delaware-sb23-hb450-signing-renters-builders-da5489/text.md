Now Delaware Has to Build the Homes.
T Travis Jack Stevens • Thursday, October 8, 2026 • 4 min read News · Thursday, October 8, 2026 · Travis Jack Stevens, write-in candidate for U.S.
Senate, Delaware · 26 days to November 3 On Wednesday, Governor Matt Meyer held a ceremony at Legislative Hall for two housing laws he first signed on July 13: Senate Bill 23, the Housing for Every Delawarean Act, and House Bill 450, the ROAD-DE Act.
Nothing in the laws changed this week.
What changed is that the state is putting its name on them again, out loud, while counties and towns work out how to comply.
I walked through both laws in detail on Tuesday in Delaware Just Rewrote the Rules for Building Homes .
Here is the short version for the two groups who will feel them first: people who rent, and people who build.
What was said Wednesday “You cannot solve the housing shortage without building more housing,” Meyer said, according to WMDT.
He also said the approach is not one-size-fits-all and that the state will work with local and county governments “step by step, to get this right for every community.” DelDOT Secretary Shanté Hastings said HB 450 gives the state tools to coordinate transportation and development, including “a statewide approach to transportation impact fees.” The bill’s lead Senate sponsor, Sen.
Jack Walsh, said it cuts “red tape,” and Rep.
Bill Bush said it trims delays “while making sure developers help pay for the impact of that growth,” according to CoastTV.
The governor’s office says permitting for new development can take more than two years.
If you rent Nothing about your rent changes this month because of these laws.
What changes is what your town has to plan for.
Under SB 23, every county, and every city or town with more than 2,000 people, has to write an affordable housing plan into its comprehensive plan.
Those plans work toward a goal of 20 percent of homes being affordable, which for rentals means affordable to a household at or below 80 percent of the area’s median income.
The state’s own policy director has called that 20 percent figure a stretch goal.
Under HB 450, housing financed by the Delaware State Housing Authority can get its transportation impact fees reduced or waived, which can lower the cost of building homes meant for working families.
The deadlines are long.
Local zoning in growth areas has to allow higher density by June 1, 2027, and the state has promised a public progress report on each community by May 2028.
If you build Traffic studies are now triggered by the busiest hour of the day, with a statewide threshold of 500 peak-hour trips, instead of total daily trips.
Downtown Development Districts are exempt from traffic impact study requirements, CoastTV reports.
DelDOT is setting statewide transportation impact fees, due by January 1, 2027, paid before a building permit.
According to CoastTV, the fees are calibrated by county, and the money generally stays in the county where it is collected.
Projects that already meet every local zoning rule now go through administrative review under SB 23, without a formal public hearing.
That is the part some residents and local officials in Sussex have pushed back on hardest.
Where I stand I live paycheck to paycheck in a one-bedroom with a dog and two kittens.
I believe housing is a human right, and a ceremony is not a home.
The measure of these laws is how many homes a nurse’s aide, a line cook, or a retiree can actually afford in 2027 and 2028, and whether the roads, schools, clinics, water, and sewer come with them.
Impact fees that stay in the county are a start.
They are not a hospital or a school.
As your Senator, I will fight for federal housing and infrastructure dollars that come to Delaware with affordability strings attached, so towns are not forced to choose between growth and the services people already wait for.
Your town is writing its plan now.
Ask to see it.
