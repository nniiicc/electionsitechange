Who Is Protecting Your Power Bill?
T Travis Jack Stevens • Friday, October 9, 2026 • 7 min read News · Friday, October 9, 2026 · Travis Jack Stevens, write-in candidate for U.S.
Senate, Delaware · 25 days to November 3 Nobody voted against House Bill 393.
Not in the House.
Not in the Senate.
It passed both chambers unanimously, and on September 30, the last day he could act, Governor Matt Meyer vetoed it, according to the governor's own legislative advisory.
Spotlight Delaware published a closer look this morning.
It deserves more than a headline, because it is about the thing every Delaware household opens every month: the electric bill.
What the bill was about Most of us buy our electricity through Delmarva Power's standard offer service.
You are also allowed to buy it from a third-party supplier instead.
Some of those companies send salespeople or run ads that come with a gift card or a rebate.
Rep.
Debra Heffernan, the bill's sponsor, says constituents came to her after exactly that.
Salespeople promised a discount.
Customers did not realize it was temporary.
Then the rate went up, and cancelling cost money.
"Their power bill was more expensive than it had been before," she told Spotlight.
The Delaware Public Advocate, the state office that speaks for utility customers, supported the bill and called the competitive supply market one that "has suffered from consistent problems with supplier behavior and unfair pricing." What HB 393 would have done Under the bill as passed, for residential customers third-party suppliers would have been: Capped at the trailing 12-month average of Delmarva's standard offer rate for most non-renewable plans.
Limited to contract terms of 12 months.
Barred from auto-renewing a contract without the customer agreeing, in writing or electronically.
Barred from charging early termination, cancellation, enrollment and late fees.
Barred from paying sales commissions to the people who sign you up.
Required to give 15 days' written notice before a rate change, and 90- and 30-day notices before a contract ends.
Required to report their prices to the Public Service Commission every month, compared against Delmarva's rate, with the Commission publishing findings.
Required to put sales agents through Public Service Commission training and certification.
Why the governor vetoed it Meyer says he supports the consumer protections but not the price cap.
His statement says the cap would drive competitors out of the market and "strengthen Delmarva's monopoly." He points to Maryland, which passed a similar cap in 2024.
Spotlight reports many third-party suppliers stopped serving Maryland homes almost immediately.
Maryland lawmakers have since raised the cap to 10 percent above the regulated utility's rate, which took effect at the start of this month.
The suppliers have not come back yet.
He also argued that fixed-rate contracts can protect people on fixed incomes, and he asked the Public Service Commission to adopt similar protections without the cap: notice and renewal rules, limits on auto-renewals and variable rates, limits on sales commissions, and a ban on early-termination fees, according to EnergyChoiceMatters, an industry trade outlet that published his statement.
That outlet also reports the Commission recently adopted new notice and renewal requirements.
That is a real argument.
I am not going to wave it away.
A fixed rate that costs a bit more can be worth it to someone who cannot afford a surprise.
What is still missing Heffernan says the bill was written with the governor's office, the Public Advocate and the Public Service Commission, and she was "shocked" by the veto.
She also says that if suppliers leave because they can no longer overcharge, "good riddance." The numbers are contested, and I will tell you which is which.
Laurel Peltier, who chairs the Maryland Energy Advocates Coalition, says her analysis shows Delaware residents on third-party suppliers paid 34 percent more on average.
That is one advocate's analysis, reported by Spotlight, not a state finding.
The industry's spokesperson, Frank Caliva of the Retail Energy Supply Association, says no price cap can work because the products are not the same.
Both are on the record.
Here is what is true today.
The Public Advocate's own guidance to customers is that third-party supplier rates are not regulated by the Public Service Commission.
The monthly price reporting that HB 393 would have required is not law, because the bill is vetoed.
And the governor's alternative is a request to a commission.
That is a different thing from a statute.
The legislature overrode two of this governor's vetoes last year, according to WHYY.
Whether it can or will do the same on this one is unclear.
EnergyChoiceMatters reports an override would likely take a special session.
Heffernan is retiring and says she does not know whether another lawmaker will carry the bill next session.
Where I stand I live paycheck to paycheck in a one-bedroom with a dog and two kittens.
I know what it means when a bill is the difference.
When I was a travel nurse, informed consent meant the patient understood what they were signing.
A gift card and a fast talker is not understanding.
Consumer protection and consumer choice are not opposites.
A choice you cannot compare is not a choice.
If a company wants your business, it should be able to show you its price next to the regulated rate, in plain words, before you sign.
If rules this basic live only in a commission's guidance, a different commission can change them.
Put them in law.
And do not lose sight of why your power bill is climbing.
Spotlight's reporting is that electricity costs are rising across the country and in Delaware, primarily because of growing demand from data centers.
Heffernan says the legislature acted this term so that Delawareans do not pay the costs of large-use energy facilities, such as data centers, connecting to the grid.
In Washington on September 30, a Senate vote to take up a House-passed bill, H.R.
9340, got 57 votes, three short of the 60 needed.
The bill would have asked states to consider making large data centers cover the cost of the grid upgrades they require.
I wrote about that here .
It says "consider," not "require," and even that stalled.
I am running against unregulated AI, and this is what that looks like at the kitchen table.
Whoever builds the machine should pay for the power it needs.
Not the family in the one-bedroom, and not the retiree who signed a paper at the door because a stranger was friendly and a gift card was on offer.
As your Senator, I will fight for a federal rule that makes large data centers pay the full cost of the grid they use, by law and not by suggestion, and I will fight for utility customers over the people who sell to them.
