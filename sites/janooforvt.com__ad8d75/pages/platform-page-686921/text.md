Our Platform
I love Vermont.
I love that we don’t have any billboards but our VTtrans highway signs say things like: “camp in the woods, not the left lane”.
I love that we’ve got the only state capital without a McDonald’s but more farmers markets, breweries and coops per capita than any other state.
I love our town meeting tradition and the fact that there is never more than one degree of separation between you and anyone else in the state.
These aren’t just charming facts about Vermont.
They reflect something much deeper about who we are.
We’ve always believed that communities matter more than corporations.
That neighbors should know one another.
That local businesses deserve to thrive.
That our working lands should be protected.
That democracy works best when ordinary people have a real voice in shaping the places they call home.
I believe Vermont already has everything we need to thrive.
We have incredibly skilled workers, innovative entrepreneurs, family farmers, extraordinary teachers, healthcare providers, artists, local businesses, and communities that know how to care for one another.
Our greatest challenge isn’t that we lack talent, resources, or ideas.
It’s that too much of the wealth we create leaves our communities instead of strengthening them.
For too long, we’ve been using the wrong economic playbook.
We’ve been told that prosperity comes from attracting more people, more corporations, or more investment from somewhere else.
I see it differently.
The future of Vermont won’t be built by waiting for prosperity to trickle down.
It will be built by investing in the people, businesses, farms, and communities that are already here—creating an economy where more wealth is created here, stays here, and is shared more broadly.
This is not about isolating ourselves but investing in ourselves, our strengths and our communities so that we can weather the storms ahead and lay the foundations for a more prosperous future.
That’s what this platform is about.
It’s one strategy to make Vermont more affordable, more resilient, and more democratic by putting more wealth, power, and decision-making back into the hands of Vermonters.
Because I really believe that together, we have everything we need to lead.
Local Businesses, Strong Communities
Strengthening Vermont's economy from the bottom up by investing in local businesses, farmers, workers, and cooperatives, keeping more wealth circulating in our communities instead of flowing to distant corporations and shareholders.
Together, we can strengthen Vermont’s economy from the bottom up by investing in local businesses, workers, farmers, entrepreneurs, and the industries of the future—creating good jobs, producing more of what we need here at home and keeping more wealth circulating in our communities instead of flowing to distant corporations and shareholders.
For too long, we’ve been using the wrong economic playbook.
We’ve been told that Vermont’s future depends on attracting outside corporations, outside investment, and outside wealth.
But Vermont isn’t struggling because we lack talented people, innovative businesses, or hardworking communities.
We’re rich in entrepreneurs, farmers, skilled workers, researchers, artists, creatives and communities that know how to solve problems together.
In fact, 99% of Vermont businesses are small businesses and about three-quarters are sole proprietors, yet too often our economic policies are designed around the needs of large corporations rather than the businesses that actually make up Vermont’s economy.
That’s backwards.
We should build a system where small businesses succeed because of our policies—not in spite of them.
The challenge is that too much of the wealth we create leaves Vermont.
Every day, money flows out of our communities through profit oriented healthcare systems, corporate monopolies, national retail chains, online marketplaces, financial speculation, and distant shareholders.
As wealth leaves, our small businesses struggle, wages stagnate, downtowns decline, and communities lose the power to shape their own future.
We can choose a different path.
Instead of competing to attract investment from somewhere else, we can invest in the people, businesses, industries, and communities that already make Vermont strong.
By helping Vermont businesses grow, connecting local producers with local buyers, expanding local ownership, and producing more of the food, energy, housing, healthcare, building materials, and other essential goods we need here at home, we can create an economy that’s more prosperous, more affordable, and far more resilient.
When Vermont businesses buy from other Vermont businesses, the benefits multiply.
A local farm that purchases equipment from a Vermont manufacturer, banks with a Vermont credit union, and hires local workers keeps that same dollar circulating through our communities again and again before it ever leaves the state.
Strengthening these local supply chains is one of the most effective ways to create jobs, support small businesses, and build long-term prosperity.
This isn’t simply an economic development strategy.
It’s a strategy for strengthening Vermont’s economic self-determination.
- Level the playing field for Vermont businesses by redesigning state procurement, financing, regulations, technical assistance, and tax policy around the needs of small businesses rather than large corporations—making it easier for local businesses to compete, collaborate, innovate, and grow while keeping ownership and wealth rooted in Vermont.
- Help Vermont businesses start, grow, and stay local by expanding patient capital, reducing unnecessary administrative burdens, creating one-stop business support services, providing shared legal, accounting, marketing, etc. services and supporting cooperative business networks, and helping businesses scale without selling out to distant corporations.
- Reward businesses that create public value by aligning grants, financing, tax incentives, and procurement preferences with outcomes that benefit Vermonters—including local ownership, living wages, apprenticeship programs, environmental stewardship, worker participation, Vermont sourcing, and long-term investment in our communities.
- Keep more Vermont dollars in Vermont by helping state agencies, municipalities, schools, hospitals, colleges, and other public institutions purchase more goods and services from Vermont businesses, farms, cooperatives, and manufacturers, breaking large contracts into sizes local businesses can compete for, and strengthening Vermont supply chains so local businesses buy from one another whenever possible.
- Produce more of what Vermont needs by investing in local food systems, community-owned renewable energy, housing construction, forest products, sustainable building materials, healthcare, childcare, manufacturing, repair industries, and other essential sectors that create good jobs while making Vermont more resilient, self-reliant, and less dependent on fragile national supply chains and corporate giants.
- Build the industries of the future by making Vermont a national leader in regenerative agriculture, circular manufacturing, repair and reuse industries, clean technologies and high-quality products built to last—supporting businesses that create long-term value while reducing waste and environmental impact.
- Strengthen Vermont’s workforce and innovation economy by expanding apprenticeships, career and technical education, workforce partnerships, and research collaborations between Vermont colleges, universities, technical centers, unions, employers, entrepreneurs, and manufacturers—aligning public research with Vermont’s greatest needs so innovations in areas like Lyme disease, climate resilience, sustainable agriculture, affordable housing, clean energy, and rural healthcare improve people’s lives while creating new Vermont businesses and good local jobs.
- Create a Vermont Public Bank to keep more of Vermont’s capital working in Vermont by partnering with local credit unions and community lenders to expand affordable, long-term financing for small businesses, farms, manufacturers, cooperatives, housing, renewable energy, community infrastructure, and other mission-driven investments that strengthen Vermont’s economy.
- Expand local ownership by helping retiring business owners transition to employees, cooperatives, family members, or local buyers instead of out-of-state corporations, while supporting worker-owned cooperatives, employee-owned businesses, community-owned enterprises, and Public Benefit Corporations through financing, technical assistance, and targeted tax incentives for businesses that create long-term public value.
- Support community-led economic development by helping towns, regions, and local organizations develop economic strategies rooted in their own strengths, assets, and priorities—whether that’s food, forestry, outdoor recreation, the arts, or holistic care—so every region of Vermont can build an economy that reflects its own people, skills, and resources.
- Coordinate economic development around one shared strategy by creating an Office of Community Wealth Building and Economic Resilience that aligns state investments, strengthens regional partnerships, expands local procurement, builds resilient Vermont supply chains, supports community-led economic development, and helps create stronger, more self-reliant local economies across the state.
How will we pay for it?
This strategy isn’t about creating an entirely new economic development system.
It’s about investing the resources we already spend more strategically.
We’ll redirect existing economic development subsidies away from expensive recruitment incentives for large corporations and toward investments that strengthen Vermont businesses, workers, farmers, and communities for the long term.
Public financing, partnerships with Vermont credit unions, community lenders, and mission-driven investors, together with returns from community-owned investments, will help multiply these dollars while keeping capital rooted in Vermont.
Because locally owned businesses recirculate significantly more of every dollar they earn than absentee-owned corporations, these investments will strengthen local tax bases, create better jobs, expand entrepreneurship, and generate lasting returns for Vermont communities.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
When more businesses are locally owned, more money stays in Vermont.
That means better jobs, thriving downtowns, stronger family farms, lower energy costs, healthier local food systems, more opportunities for young people, and communities that are better prepared for economic disruptions, natural disasters, and political uncertainty.
Instead of depending on distant corporations to determine Vermont’s future, we’ll build an economy that gives Vermonters greater control over the things that matter most.
Together, we can create an economy where wealth is created here, stays here, and is shared more broadly building stronger communities, greater economic security, and a Vermont where every region has the opportunity to thrive.
Homes for Vermonters, Not Investors
Together, we can build a housing system where every Vermonter can afford to rent, buy, or build a home in the community they love.
By investing in permanently affordable housing, helping Vermonters build and own homes, and asking large corporate investors, private equity, luxury second homes, and commercial vacation rentals to contribute their fair share, we can lower housing costs, reduce property taxes, and put more homes back in the hands of the people and communities they were meant to serve.
Vermont communities are being hollowed out because too many Vermonters can no longer afford housing in the communities they love.
Housing costs have skyrocketed, property taxes continue to rise, young families are struggling to buy their first home, seniors can’t find affordable places to downsize, and too many homes are being used as investments rather than places to live.
For decades, we’ve treated housing as an investment rather than a home.
More homes are being purchased by investors, second-home owners, and corporations while working Vermonters are priced out of the communities they grew up in.
At the same time, we haven’t invested enough in permanently affordable housing or made it easy enough for ordinary Vermonters to build, renovate, and own homes themselves.
We can choose a different path.
A resilient Vermont is one that has the capacity to house itself—investing in local materials, local workers, community ownership, and permanently affordable homes that remain affordable for generations to come.
Across Vermont, organizations like the Champlain Housing Trust, one of the nation’s most successful community land trusts, have shown that limited-equity homeownership works.
Communities are leading the way through housing cooperatives, cohousing developments and innovative local projects like Weathervane United in Lincoln, which has provided affordable senior housing through community leadership for decades, while in Brandon, VT local builders are transforming an abandoned high school into co-housing while training volunteers in basic carpentry and reclaiming building materials.
Instead of treating these as isolated success stories, we can make them the foundation of Vermont’s housing strategy.
Together, we can put homes back in the hands of Vermonters by investing in permanently affordable housing, rebuilding our capacity to build and renovate homes locally, reducing property taxes, and ensuring more homes are owned by the people and communities they were meant to serve.
- Modernize Vermont’s property tax and housing classification system by recognizing the different roles housing plays in our communities.
Primary homes, workforce housing, permanently affordable housing, small Vermont landlords, and modest family camps would receive greater protections, while luxury second homes, commercial vacation rentals, vacant investment properties, and large corporate or private-equity ownership would contribute more.
We’ll also close ownership loopholes that allow large investors to hide behind multiple LLCs, ensuring our tax system rewards homes that strengthen Vermont communities—not big investors and rich out of staters.
- Expand permanently affordable housing by investing in community land trusts, housing cooperatives, cohousing, public housing, and shared-equity homeownership so homes remain affordable for generations—not just a few years.
- Help working Vermonters build, buy and renovate homes by expanding affordable financing, technical assistance, shared-equity programs, and financial support for owner-builders, first-time homebuyers, multigenerational housing, small-scale builders, and accessory dwelling units.
- Rebuild Vermont’s housing workforce through expanded vocational education, apprenticeships, technical centers, and a Community Builder Program that trains the next generation of carpenters, electricians, plumbers, and builders while restoring homes and public buildings across the state.
- Build a circular housing economy by expanding adaptive reuse of vacant properties and old schools, churches, barns, mills, motels, and other underused buildings, supporting renovation and deconstruction instead of demolition, reclaiming building materials, and growing local businesses that repair, remanufacture, and recycle construction materials.
- Expand Vermont’s production of building materials by supporting local lumber, wood products, modular housing, insulation, windows, heat pumps, and other essential building components that strengthen local supply chains and create good-paying jobs.
- Invest in senior housing and universal accessibility by expanding accessible housing, aging-in-place programs, and incentives that allow older Vermonters to remain in their communities while freeing up homes for younger families.
New publicly supported housing should be designed using universal design principles wherever practical so Vermonters can remain in their homes throughout every stage of life.
- End homelessness through a Housing First approach, ensuring every Vermonter has rapid access to safe, permanent housing alongside voluntary mental health care, substance use treatment, employment assistance, and other wraparound services that help people rebuild their lives.
- Support locally driven housing solutions by simplifying permitting for permanently affordable housing, adaptive reuse projects, accessory dwelling units, and small-scale infill while preserving strong environmental protections and giving towns greater flexibility to meet local housing needs.
- Measure success differently by judging our housing system by the percentage of Vermonters living in quality, affordable housing—not simply the number of housing units built.
How will we pay for it?
Revenue from taxes on private equity, large corporate housing, second homes, vacation rentals and luxury estates will be reinvested to reduce property taxes for Vermont residents and expand permanently affordable housing.
We’ll also leverage state and federal housing programs, revolving loan funds, municipal partnerships, and mission-driven investment to help communities create housing that remains affordable for generations.
By taxing digital advertisements, data monetization and large online marketplaces (see Ban AI Data Centers & Retake Control from Big Tech Policy)
By investing in local builders, technical education, Vermont-made building materials, adaptive reuse, and community ownership, we’ll keep more construction dollars circulating in Vermont’s economy while reducing long-term housing costs.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
We can build a Vermont where homes are owned by people, neighborhoods still know their neighbors, and every community has the opportunity to thrive.
By making it easier for Vermonters to rent, buy, build, and remain in their homes, we’ll lower housing costs and property taxes, help young families put down roots, enable older Vermonters to age with dignity in their communities, reduce homelessness, and create good-paying jobs in the skilled trades.
Just as importantly, this plan will rebuild Vermont’s capacity to house itself—strengthening local builders, supporting Vermont-made building materials, expanding community ownership, and keeping more wealth circulating in our towns instead of flowing to distant investors.
Housing should create community wealth, strengthen local economies, and give every Vermonter the freedom and security of a place to call home.
Protect & Invest in Community Schools
Together, we can protect and strengthen Vermont's schools by investing in them as the heart of every community—bringing essential services closer to home, strengthening local economies, and giving communities more power to shape their own future.
For generations, Vermont’s schools have been more than places where children go to class, they are the heart of our communities.
Yet instead of building on this strength, Vermont’s current education strategy is focused on consolidating districts, closing rural schools, and sending children farther from their communities.
Consolidation changes where children attend school, but it does little to address the core drivers of rising education costs, including rising healthcare costs, growing student mental-health needs, and the increasing expectation that schools respond to challenges created far beyond the classroom.
We can choose a different path.
A resilient Vermont is one where every community has the power and capacity to meet more of its own needs.
Schools are one of the few public institutions that still exist in nearly every town.
Instead of concentrating more services, resources, and decision-making farther away, we can strengthen the institutions that already bring people together.
Building on the Vermont community school pilots in 2021, community schools integrate primary care, social workers, mental-health support, childcare, food access, continuing adult education and other nonprofit and state services into our schools as a way to improve educational outcomes while investing in the social, economic, and civic infrastructure that makes communities stronger.
This isn’t just about bringing more services into schools.
It’s about redistributing power back to communities.
Community schools give towns more capacity to solve problems locally.
They make it easier for parents to work by expanding childcare, improve workforce participation through continuing adult education and job training, reduce healthcare costs through prevention and create stable local jobs.
Instead of dozens of community service organizations operating separately, schools become shared civic infrastructure where people, ideas, and resources come together.
They also make Vermont more resilient.
Strong communities are built on strong relationships and trusted public institutions.
Community schools create year-round places where neighbors learn together, support one another, organize, and respond during floods, public health emergencies, and other crises.
They become the foundation of stronger local economies, healthier communities, and more resilient democracy.
Vermont is already showing what’s possible.
Winooski has connected schools with health services, food access, and family support.
Newport has expanded health, wellness, and early-childhood services through the state’s Community Schools initiative.
Small rural schools like Peacham Elementary have integrated childcare directly into the school building, helping young families remain in their communities.
Instead of treating these as isolated success stories, we can make them the foundation of Vermont’s education strategy.
Research shows this approach works.
Well-designed community schools improve attendance, academic achievement, graduation rates, family engagement, student health, and school climate because they address the barriers to learning before they become crises.
- Build on Vermont’s community school approach by supporting schools to become locally designed hubs for education, healthcare, mental-health support, childcare, food access, adult learning, recreation, and community services.
Each school will determine its own priorities through collaboration among students, families, educators, municipalities, nonprofits, and local residents.
- Protect small and rural schools as essential community institutions by making community-school investment an alternative to forced consolidation and closure.
Schools will be evaluated not only by enrollment or cost per pupil, but by the full value they provide to children, families, local economies, and community life.
- Place community-school coordinators in participating schools to build partnerships, identify unmet needs, coordinate services, pursue outside funding, and connect families with assistance.
This will ensure teachers are not expected to act simultaneously as educators, social workers, case managers, mental-health providers, and healthcare navigators.
- Integrate primary and preventive healthcare into schools (See universal primary care policy) and expand school-based mental-health and social-work support by placing more counselors, social workers, behavioral-health specialists, substance-use prevention providers, and family-support workers in schools.
These services will help address challenges early rather than waiting until children and families reach a crisis.
- Create childcare and early-learning hubs in underused school space by helping schools partner with local childcare providers.
Building on models like Peacham, schools could host infant care, preschool, afterschool programs, summer programs, and childcare so parents can work and know their children are in good hands.
- Expand food access and community nutrition programs by partnering with the Vermont Foodbank, local farms, food shelves, community-action agencies, and mutual-aid organizations.
Schools could provide community meals, food distribution, school gardens, cooking programs, clothing, diapers, hygiene products, and emergency necessities without stigma.
- Open schools for continuing adult education and lifelong learning by offering evening, weekend, and summer programs such as GED preparation, English-language classes, digital skills, financial literacy, parenting support, arts, recreation, civic education, continuing education.
- Connect schools with workforce development and technical education through partnerships with technical centers, Vermont State University, community colleges, labor unions, local employers, and tradespeople.
Schools could host apprenticeships, vocational programs, repair workshops, maker spaces, agricultural education, and training in the skills our communities urgently need.
- Strengthen intergenerational connection by offering senior meals, exercise programs, technology assistance, volunteer opportunities, mentoring, oral-history projects, and shared activities between students and elders.
This will reduce isolation while allowing children to learn from the knowledge and experience of their communities.
- Bring government services closer to people by coordinating state agencies, nonprofit providers, and local organizations through community schools.
Instead of building larger bureaucracies, we’ll make existing services easier to access while giving communities more power to coordinate solutions locally.
- Invest upstream rather than paying for crises downstream.
By providing children and families with healthcare, counseling, childcare, food assistance, housing support, and other services before problems become emergencies, we can improve educational outcomes while reducing long-term costs in healthcare, corrections, child welfare, homelessness, and special education.
Research consistently shows that community schools improve attendance, behavior, graduation rates, and academic achievement because they remove barriers to learning before they become crises.
- Rebuild Vermont’s school infrastructure for the 21st century by renovating aging buildings, improving energy efficiency, modernizing classrooms, and creating space for childcare, healthcare, workforce development, arts, recreation, and community services.
We’re already going to have to rebuild many of our schools.
Let’s rebuild them once—and rebuild them to solve multiple challenges at the same time.
How will we pay for it?
Community schools aren’t about asking education property taxpayers to fund more services.
They’re about making better use of the public investments we’re already making.
Today, healthcare, childcare, workforce development, mental health, nutrition, family services, and education are often funded separately, delivered separately, and located separately—even when they serve the same children and families.
Community schools bring these investments together, allowing every public dollar to accomplish more.
The Agency of Education, Department of Health, Department for Children and Families, Department of Mental Health, Department of Labor, Agency of Commerce and Community Development, community health centers, designated agencies, nonprofits, and philanthropic partners will each continue funding the services connected to their missions.
The difference is that those services will increasingly be delivered together through trusted community institutions rather than fragmented across multiple buildings and bureaucracies.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
A child cannot learn when they are hungry, sick, frightened, unhoused, or unable to access the support they need.
A parent cannot fully participate in the workforce when there is no childcare or don’t have access to the social services they need.
An elder cannot remain connected to the community when every gathering place disappears.
And a rural town cannot attract young families or build a thriving local economy after losing its school.
Community Schools allow us to address these challenges together.
By bringing services to the places families already know and trust, we can improve educational outcomes, reduce pressure on teachers, make health care and childcare easier to access, support local jobs and businesses, reduce isolation, and diversify the funding that sustains our public schools.
The answer to rising costs and struggling communities is not to withdraw more public institutions from rural Vermont.
It is to make those institutions work better for all of us.
Instead of consolidating our public schools out of existence, we will protect and invest in them as the foundations every child, family, and community needs to thrive.
Ban AI Data Centers & Retake Control from Big Tech
The concentration of power in the hands of a few AI companies and tech giants is a threat to our democracy, economy and planet.
Vermont can be a national leader in advancing the regulations and protections needed to ensure everyone has control of their data, their privacy and their lives.
Artificial intelligence is one of the greatest threats of our time, left unchecked it risks concentrating extraordinary wealth and power in the hands of a small number of billionaires while accelerating mass layoffs, expanding surveillance, increasing energy demand, spreading misinformation, and weakening democracy itself.
A handful of corporations now control the computing infrastructure, cloud services, digital marketplaces, communications platforms, advertising networks, personal data, and AI systems that increasingly shape our economy and public life.
Their decisions influence what information people see, how businesses compete, who gets hired, how workers are managed, and even how governments function.
No democracy can remain truly free when so much of its communications infrastructure and economic activity depends upon corporations accountable primarily to shareholders rather than the public.
Technology is not an inevitable force acting upon us.
It is the result of human choices, public investment and political rules.
We can choose an economy in which technology gives people more time, security, knowledge and democratic power—not one in which it concentrates wealth, replaces human relationships and places our communities under permanent surveillance
Vermont has already demonstrated national leadership by strengthening consumer privacy protections and even advanced some of the most ambitious data privacy and AI data center bills before they were vetoed by our current Governor.
Just as previous generations confronted monopolies in railroads, oil, banking, and telecommunications, our generation must take on the concentration of wealth and power by artificial intelligence and big tech before it’s too late.
Vermont can be a leader and work with other states to build common standards, coordinate enforcement, restore competition, and reclaim democratic control over the technologies shaping our future.
There is a reason that the federal government keeps trying to prevent states from regulating AI- it’s because we have real power and it’s critical that we use it now.
- Place a moratorium on AI data centers and make clear that Vermont is not for sale to the AI industry.
We will oppose the development of energy-intensive AI data centers and the new nuclear power infrastructure proposed to support them.
Vermont’s land, water, and affordable electricity should strengthen our communities—not subsidize the growing concentration of wealth and power in a handful of global technology corporations.
- Strengthen Vermont’s Digital Bill of Rights, guaranteeing meaningful control over personal information, including the right to know how data is collected and used; access, correct, transfer, and delete personal data; opt out of targeted advertising and algorithmic profiling; challenge consequential automated decisions; and obtain meaningful remedies when companies violate these rights.
- Protect Vermonters from mass surveillance by prohibiting the sale of sensitive personal information, banning surveillance technologies such as Flock cameras, and preventing AI from being used to monitor lawful political activity, labor organizing, immigration enforcement, reproductive healthcare, or religious practice.
- Ensure Big Tech pays its fair share by placing taxes on large digital advertising platforms, data monetization, dominant online marketplaces, and AI infrastructure—both to discourage business models that extract wealth, data, and economic activity from Vermont communities and to level the playing field for local businesses.
Revenue generated will be reinvested in community broadband, local journalism, public-interest technology and programs that help Vermonters buy local, support Vermont businesses, and keep more of our economy rooted here at home.
- Protect workers in the age of AI by prohibiting invasive workplace surveillance, protecting collective bargaining over automation, and ensuring productivity gains supported by public investment are shared through higher wages, shorter working hours where appropriate, improved public services, or broader community ownership.
- Establish the nation’s strongest public standards for AI, requiring independent testing, transparency, human oversight, ongoing audits, and public accountability while prohibiting predictive policing, social scoring, automated benefit termination, emotion recognition, and AI systems designed for mass political or immigration surveillance.
- Put people back in control of the algorithms shaping their lives by requiring greater transparency and independent audits of large recommendation systems, giving users meaningful control over algorithmic feeds where legally feasible, and restricting manipulative design practices that maximize addiction, outrage, and compulsive engagement—especially for children.
- Strengthen digital resilience and public-interest technology by investing in community broadband, open-source government software, secure public communications infrastructure, privacy-enhancing technologies, cybersecurity for towns and schools, cooperative online marketplaces, independent local journalism, and Vermont technology businesses developing public-purpose digital solutions.
- Support public-purpose innovation by partnering with Vermont colleges, universities, entrepreneurs, and mission-driven technology companies to develop open-source software, privacy-protecting technologies, cybersecurity, AI for healthcare and scientific research, agricultural innovation, accessibility tools, and other technologies that solve real problems while keeping knowledge, jobs, and innovation rooted in Vermont.
- Create a Vermont Cyber Defense Corps to protect critical infrastructure, elections, hospitals, schools, municipalities, nonprofits, and small businesses from cyberattacks while training the next generation of cybersecurity professionals.
- Use Vermont’s purchasing power to strengthen democratic technology by prioritizing secure, interoperable, privacy-protecting, and open-source technologies wherever practical, reducing dependence on proprietary software while supporting mission-driven technology companies that serve the public interest.
- Partner with other states to coordinate AI governance, privacy protections, cybersecurity, taxation of dominant technology companies, public procurement standards, antitrust advocacy, open-source development, and enforcement so states can act together rather than individually against corporations whose power increasingly rivals that of governments.
- Use public investment and collective economic power to defend democracy by coordinating with other states, public pension funds, universities, and public institutions to review investments in companies that enable mass surveillance, political repression, algorithmic discrimination, or other practices that undermine democratic rights.
Where appropriate and legally supported, pursue coordinated divestment and procurement restrictions while directing public investment toward mission-driven, privacy-protecting, democratic technology companies.
How will we pay for it?
Many of these reforms establish new rules and protections rather than creating significant new spending.
Investments in cybersecurity, digital resilience, community broadband, local journalism, and public-interest technology will be financed by ensuring that the technology companies extracting enormous value from Vermonters finally contribute their fair share.
Taxes on digital advertising, data monetization, dominant online marketplaces, and AI infrastructure will serve two purposes: they will help discourage business models that extract wealth, data, and economic activity from Vermont communities while generating revenue to invest in the digital infrastructure and public institutions that strengthen our democracy.
Those revenues will be reinvested in community broadband, cybersecurity, local journalism, open-source technologies, public-interest innovation, and programs that help Vermont businesses compete and encourage Vermonters to buy local whenever possible.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
Technology should give people more freedom, security, and opportunity—not less.
This plan will protect Vermonters’ privacy, strengthen democracy, create good-paying jobs in cybersecurity and public-interest technology, expand access to secure digital infrastructure, support independent local journalism, and help local businesses compete more fairly against global technology monopolies.
By investing in digital resilience, public-purpose innovation, and democratic technology, we’ll keep more wealth, knowledge, and innovation rooted in Vermont while reducing our dependence on corporations whose business models often undermine local communities, workers, and democratic institutions.
Together, we’ll build a future where technology strengthens our local economy, expands freedom, protects our rights, and gives Vermonters greater control over their own data, digital infrastructure, and economic future—ensuring that technology serves people and communities, not the other way around.
Free, Universal Primary & Preventive Care
Reduce the overall cost of healthcare by implementing universal primary care, and emphasize preventive medicine by making sure there is a community doctor for every Vermonter.
Together, we can rebuild small-town medicine by making primary care free, strengthening community healthcare, and ensuring every Vermonter has access to trusted care close to home while laying the foundation for a universal healthcare system.
For generations, healthcare in Vermont was rooted in relationships.
Families knew their doctor, local hospitals and independent pharmacies served as anchors of their communities, and people could receive care close to home from providers who understood their lives.
Today, that system is disappearing.
Rural practices are closing, independent pharmacies are struggling, healthcare workers are burning out, and too many Vermonters delay care because they simply can’t afford it.
At the same time, doctors and nurses spend more time navigating insurance paperwork than caring for patients, while our healthcare system spends billions treating illness after people become sick instead of investing in the care that keeps people healthy.
We can choose a different path.
Across Vermont, communities are already showing us what the future of healthcare can look like.
Towns including Thetford, Tunbridge, Norwich, Hartland, Hartford, Strafford, Reading, and West Windsor have established Community Nurse programs that bring trusted healthcare professionals directly into their communities.
These nurses help people manage chronic illnesses, coordinate care, make home visits, connect families with local services, prevent unnecessary emergency room visits, and help older Vermonters remain healthy and independent in their own homes.
We will build on these successful models by ensuring every Vermont community has access to a locally based Community Health Team—including primary care providers, community nurses, mental health professionals, community health workers, care coordinators, and independent local pharmacies—working together to keep people healthy before small problems become expensive crises.
By making primary and preventive care free and investing in community-based healthcare, Vermont can improve health outcomes while dramatically reducing the need for costly emergency room visits, avoidable hospitalizations, and expensive specialist care.
Every dollar we invest upstream in prevention saves money downstream by reducing the burden of chronic disease, improving mental health, supporting healthy aging, and helping people receive care before conditions become emergencies.
This approach also makes Vermont more resilient.
Nearly one-third of our state budget depends on federal funding, leaving our healthcare system vulnerable to changing federal priorities and corporate consolidation.
By strengthening local healthcare providers, rebuilding small-town medicine, supporting independent pharmacies, and expanding community-based care, we can ensure every Vermonter has access to trusted healthcare close to home regardless of pending federal cuts to Medicaid and attacks on public health by this administration.
This isn’t simply about expanding healthcare.
It’s about rebuilding a healthcare system that rewards keeping people healthy instead of treating illness after it has already become a crisis.
And by creating a universal system of publicly funded primary and preventive care, we’ll lay the foundation for a future where every Vermonter has access to comprehensive universal healthcare—building it step by step on a strong, affordable, community-based foundation.
- Guarantee free primary and preventive healthcare for every Vermonter by eliminating copays, deductibles, and insurance barriers, making primary care the front door to a future universal healthcare system.
- Bring back small-town medicine by ensuring every Vermont community has free primary care providers by replacing fee-for-service primary care with direct public funding that gives doctors, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and mental health providers more time with patients and rewards prevention, continuity of care, and healthier communities.
- Integrate healthcare into the heart of our communities by partnering with Community Schools, expanding school-based healthcare, supporting home visits, telehealth and community outreach so care is available where people already live, learn, and gather.
Ensuring our elders can age in place and our disability community can get access to the care they need close to home.
- Rebuild Vermont’s healthcare workforce by expanding primary and preventive healthcare residency programs, loan repayment programs, and rural recruitment incentives while reducing administrative burdens that contribute to burnout and helping more clinicians build long-term careers in Vermont.
- Invest upstream instead of paying for crises downstream by expanding preventive care, maternal health, mental healthcare, nutrition, chronic disease management, and early intervention—improving health while reducing costly emergency room visits, avoidable hospitalizations, and expensive specialist care.
- Strengthen healthcare resilience by supporting independent pharmacies, expanding regional purchasing partnerships, maintaining reserves of essential medicines and medical supplies, and reducing Vermont’s dependence on fragile national supply chains and changing federal policies.
- Lay the foundation for universal healthcare by creating the nation’s first publicly funded universal primary care system—improving health, lowering overall healthcare costs, rebuilding community-based care, and creating the infrastructure needed to expand toward comprehensive universal healthcare over time by exploring partnerships with neighboring states as a way to achieve this in the years to come.
How will we pay for it?
This plan will be funded through our Fair Share Tax Proposal, which asks the wealthiest Vermonters to contribute the equivalent cuts from the Big Beautiful Bill.
Those who have benefited most from decades of rising wealth and tax cuts should help ensure every Vermonter can access the healthcare they need.
At the same time, we’re not simply spending more—we’re spending smarter.
Today, billions of healthcare dollars are consumed by insurance administration, fragmented funding systems, and treating preventable illnesses after they become expensive crises.
By making primary and preventive care universally available, paying providers directly instead of through fee-for-service insurance billing, and investing in community-based care, Vermont can reduce avoidable emergency room visits, hospitalizations, and the long-term costs of chronic disease with estimated cost savings of 300 million a year for the state.
This approach allows every public dollar to accomplish more: improving health, reducing waste, strengthening local healthcare, and laying the financial foundation for a true universal healthcare system.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
Together, we can build a healthcare system where every Vermonter has a trusted healthcare team close to home and never has to choose between getting the care they need and paying the bills.
This plan will make it easier for families to see a doctor before small problems become serious illnesses, help older Vermonters remain healthy and independent in their communities, improve access to mental healthcare, strengthen independent rural clinics and pharmacies, reduce pressure on emergency rooms and hospitals, and allow healthcare professionals to spend more time caring for patients instead of navigating insurance paperwork.
By rebuilding small-town medicine, expanding successful Community Nurse programs, and making primary and preventive care free for every Vermonter, we’ll create healthier communities, lower long-term healthcare costs, strengthen Vermont’s resilience, and restore the trusted local healthcare relationships that have always been at the heart of our communities.
Just as importantly, we’ll demonstrate that healthcare works best when it’s organized around keeping people healthy—not maximizing profits—and take the first practical step toward a universal healthcare system that guarantees high-quality care for every Vermonter.
Protect Vermont, Deepen Democracy
Together, we can build the most democratic state in America by protecting our freedoms, giving every Vermonter a meaningful voice in the decisions that affect their lives, and returning more power to our communities.
For generations, Vermont has aspired to a form of democracy rooted in community, where neighbors come together to deliberate, solve problems, and shape the future of the places they call home.
That tradition reminds us that democracy is strongest when people meaningful opportunities to listen to one another, work through disagreements, and help shape the decisions that affect their lives.
Our challenge today is to build on those strengths while ensuring that every Vermonter can participate fully.
Democracy should be accessible to everyone, regardless of disability, age, language, work schedule, caregiving responsibilities, transportation, or where they live.
Today, our democracy faces two interconnected crises.
Across America, wealth and power have become increasingly concentrated in Washington, Wall Street, Silicon Valley, and a handful of multinational corporations.
Decisions affecting our lives are increasingly made farther away from the people who live with the consequences.
At the same time, rising authoritarianism, political polarization, mass surveillance, and growing distrust in public institutions are weakening faith in democracy itself.
Here in Vermont, many people also feel they no longer have a meaningful voice in the decisions that shape their communities.
Too often, agencies work in silos, public investment is organized around bureaucracies instead of outcomes, and communities are invited to comment only after major decisions have already been made instead of helping shape them from the beginning.
We can choose a different path.
The promise of our federal system was that states would serve as laboratories of democracy and bulwarks against authoritarianism.
Vermont has an opportunity to demonstrate that the answer isn’t less democracy, it’s better democracy: democracy that is more accessible, more deliberative, more participatory, and more empowering.
Across Vermont, people with very different perspectives are asking for a greater voice in the decisions that affect their lives.
Whether communities are asking for a greater voice over education, land-use planning, economic development, or Vermont’s long-term future, there is a growing recognition that people don’t just want different policies, they want a different relationship with government.
While these movements don’t agree on every issue, they reflect a shared belief that the people most affected by decisions should have a meaningful role in making them.
Communities across the United States and around the world are already demonstrating what this can look like.
Wales adopted a Future Generations Act requiring the government to work toward long-term goals identified through public engagement.
Ireland used Citizens’ Assemblies to break political deadlock on some of its most challenging issues.
Following the financial crisis, cities across Spain expanded participatory budgeting to rebuild public trust by giving residents a direct voice in how scarce public resources were invested.
Washington and other states are adapting deliberative democracy to strengthen public decision-making.
Vermont doesn’t need to copy these models, but we can learn from them while building on our own democratic tradition and becoming a leader in new forms of governance and democracy that challenge the rise of Fascism and centralized control.
Together, we can build a Vermont where democracy isn’t simply about choosing leaders every few years.
It becomes a way of governing that gives every Vermonter a meaningful voice, helps communities solve problems together, strengthens trust across differences, and ensures government is truly of the people, by the people, and for the people.
- Protect Vermont’s democracy and constitutional freedoms by defending civil liberties, privacy, reproductive freedom, workers’ rights, LGBTQ+ Vermonters, immigrants, people with disabilities, freedom of speech, and the rule of law while ensuring Vermont remains a place where everyone can safely participate in public life.
- Advance a Vermont Future Generations initiative by engaging Vermonters in a statewide conversation to define our shared long-term goals for the future of our state, establishing measurable long-term targets, and aligning budgets, investments, and policies across government to achieve those democratically determined priorities while publicly reporting on our progress.
- Create Vermont Citizens’ Assemblies that bring representative groups of Vermonters together to deliberate on major statewide issues and make recommendations to the Legislature and Governor, ensuring every day Vermonters play a meaningful role in shaping public policy.
- Expand participatory budgeting and community decision-making by giving communities greater direct authority over public spending while supporting locally led planning for housing, economic development, healthcare, and other community priorities.
- Build a democracy that is accessible, deliberative, and empowered by ensuring every Vermonter has meaningful opportunities to participate through accessible Town Meetings, Citizens’ Assemblies, participatory budgeting, digital engagement, transportation, language access, universal design, and other modern participation tools.
Democracy works best when everyone can participate and people’s voices have a real impact on decisions.
- Redesign government around people and communities—not bureaucracies—by breaking down silos between agencies, streamlining services, reducing unnecessary red tape, expanding one-stop community services, and empowering public servants to work collaboratively with communities to solve problems rather than simply administer programs.
Government should be organized around the outcomes Vermonters want to achieve, not the structure of government itself.
- Return more power to communities by giving municipalities, regional organizations and local institutions greater flexibility, resources, and authority to solve problems in ways that reflect local priorities and context.
- Build democratic resilience by strengthening local journalism, libraries, community organizations, mutual aid networks, and interstate partnerships while reducing Vermont’s dependence on concentrated corporate power and federal funding.
How will we pay for it?
Most of these reforms improve how the government works rather than creating entirely new programs.
By coordinating existing public investments around shared wellbeing goals, strengthening local decision-making, improving collaboration between agencies, and reducing duplication, Vermont can build a more democratic, transparent, and effective government while making better use of existing resources.
Many of the investments proposed throughout this platform, including Community Schools, universal primary care, community wealth building, and local food and energy systems, also strengthen democracy by giving communities greater capacity to solve problems together and reducing dependence on distant political and economic institutions.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
Democracy is strongest when people have both the opportunity and the power to shape the future of their communities.
This plan will give Vermonters a stronger voice in public decisions, ensure our government is more transparent and accountable, strengthen trust in public institutions, and ensure communities have the resources and power they need to solve more problems locally.
By planning for future generations, measuring what truly matters, and putting more decision-making power into the hands of the people, we can build a government that is more responsive, more resilient, and truly governed by the people and for the people.
Just as importantly, it will help Vermont demonstrate that democracy can be more than a system for choosing leaders.
It can become a way of governing that empowers people, strengthens communities, and ensures that every generation inherits a state that is more free, more resilient, and more democratic than the one before it.
Local Food, Healthy Communities, & A Vermont That Can Feed Itself
Every Vermonter should have access to healthy, nutritious food, and every farmer should be able to earn a good living feeding their community.
Vermont is already showing the country what’s possible.
Through initiatives like Farm to Plate Initiative, Farm to School, Universal School Meals, Crop Cash, Farm Share, Vermonters Feeding Vermonters, our food hubs, cooperatives, and the extraordinary work of farmers across the state, we’ve become a national leader in local food systems.
Over the last decade, Farm to Plate helped double local food consumption, through a networked, integrated supply chain approach.
Proving that when we invest in local agriculture, we strengthen our economy, our communities, and our resilience.
Now it’s time to build on that success.
Today, too much of our food system is controlled by a handful of multinational corporations that dominate food processing, distribution, manufacturing, and retail.
That concentration of power leaves farmers with less bargaining power, sends billions of food dollars out of Vermont every year, and makes our communities more vulnerable to supply chain disruptions.
At the same time, industrial food production and weak national standards around chemicals and contaminants have contributed to growing concerns about the quality of the food we eat and its impact on our health.
Healthy food is one of the most powerful forms of preventive healthcare.
By investing in local and regional food production, regenerative agriculture, and stronger food standards where Vermont has the authority to act, we can improve public health, strengthen our farm economy, keep more wealth circulating locally, and ensure every Vermonter has access to safe, healthy food.
A resilient Vermont is one that has the capacity to feed itself.
- Reduce our dependence on corporate food monopolies by investing in local and regional processing, distribution, cooperative ownership, and regional food infrastructure so farmers have more choices, communities keep more wealth locally, and Vermont is less vulnerable to corporate concentration.
- Build on Vermont’s Farm to Plate success by expanding local and regional food processing, milling, slaughter, storage, cold-chain infrastructure, and distribution so more food grown in Vermont is processed, sold, and eaten here.
- Buy Vermont first by expanding local purchasing through schools, colleges, hospitals, correctional facilities, and state government while strengthening Farm to School and Universal School Meals so more Vermont-grown food reaches our children and schools.
- Expand Community Schools as community food hubs, partnering with organizations like the Vermont Foodbank to provide healthy meals, community kitchens, nutrition education, food recovery, emergency food support, and connections to local farmers.
- Strengthen Vermont farms by protecting farmland, rebuilding soil health, expanding access to land for beginning and working farmers, supporting farm succession, and helping family farms remain economically viable across generations.
- Keep more of the food dollar in Vermont by expanding local food manufacturing and value-added production, including dairy processing, grain milling, meat processing, canning, freezing, fermentation, and other locally owned food businesses.
- Invest in the next generation of farmers through apprenticeships, technical education, affordable farmworker housing, and pathways that help young Vermonters build careers in agriculture.
- Support healthy food as preventive healthcare by expanding Food is Medicine initiatives, strengthening connections between local farms, schools, hospitals, and community health centers, supporting regenerative agriculture, and reducing Vermonters’ exposure to harmful chemicals and contaminants wherever Vermont has the authority to act.
- Build a climate-resilient food system by investing in regenerative farming, diversified crops, seed breeding and seed-saving networks, water management, and farming practices that strengthen resilience to climate change.
- Prepare for future disruptions by establishing a Vermont Food Security Reserve and working with neighboring states to strengthen regional food security so New England can continue feeding itself during supply chain disruptions, climate emergencies, or national crises.
How will we pay for it?
We’ll build on successful investments like Farm to Plate by directing more public purchasing toward Vermont farms and food producers, strengthening state agricultural partnerships, expanding cooperatives, and investing in food infrastructure that creates lasting economic returns.
Our broader Community Wealth Building strategy will keep more of the food dollar circulating in Vermont by supporting local processing, distribution, manufacturing, and cooperative businesses instead of relying on distant corporations.
As more food is grown, processed, purchased, and consumed locally, we strengthen our farm economy, create good-paying jobs, improve public health, and reduce our dependence on fragile national supply chains.
How will this improve Vermonters' quality of life?
Food is one of life’s greatest joys.
It’s how we celebrate, care for one another, share our cultures, and build community.
Every Vermonter deserves access to fresh, healthy, delicious food, and every farmer deserves the opportunity to make a good living producing it.
By investing in local food systems, we’ll make nutritious food more affordable and accessible, improve public health, and reduce diet-related disease by making healthy food the easy choice.
We’ll strengthen our rural communities by creating good-paying jobs across the food economy, keeping more food dollars circulating locally, and ensuring family farms can thrive for generations.
Just as importantly, agriculture has the power to heal our landscapes.
By supporting regenerative farming, healthy soils, clean water, and biodiversity, we can leave Vermont’s land healthier than we found it while strengthening our resilience to climate change.
Together, we can build a Vermont where healthy food nourishes our people, farming sustains our communities, and our land continues to flourish for generations to come.
