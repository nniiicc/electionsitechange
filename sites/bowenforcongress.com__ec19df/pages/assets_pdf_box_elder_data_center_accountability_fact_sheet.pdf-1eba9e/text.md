Carlton Bowen is calling on every public official who voted for or approved the Box Elder Data Center framework to make a simple public pledge: the decision was made for the public interest, and they will not seek or accept private financial benefit from the project or its future phases.
The pledge I will not seek, accept, or benefit from any contract, commission, consulting fee, employment, land transaction, water-right transaction, development opportunity, equity interest, board seat, vendor relationship, or other financial benefit connected to the Box Elder Data Center or its future phases.
The disclosure standard If any such interest already exists - directly, through an immediate family member, through a company, trust, client, affiliated LLC, or other entity - it should be disclosed fully and publicly now.
Why this matters What the pledge is not A 40,000-acre project can create enormous private value.
It is not an accusation of unlawful conduct.
Land, water rights, roads, utilities, contracts, and future development can all become more valuable.
It is not anti-growth or anti-technology.
The public should not have to wonder whether public decisions create private rewards for decision-makers.
It is a public-trust standard for a project this large.
The public-trust test An honest project should be able to survive sunlight.
A responsible public official should be willing to say: I approved this for the public interest, and I will not profit from it privately.

They do not replace an open, live, accountable public hearing before a decision this consequential.
Box Elder County says it reviewed more than 2,500 comments, including about 300 from county residents, before granting authorization.
That is part of the record.
It is not the same as a live public hearing where residents can look decision-makers in the eye, make their case, and hear answers before the vote. [2] The people showed up.
The process didn’t.
Self-government met a closed door.
Public reporting on the May 4 meeting said hundreds of residents gathered at the fairgrounds.
KUTV reported that after commissioners announced there would be no public comment, the crowd booed and yelled "shame," and commissioners later left the meeting room. [12] Hear people first Show the record Hold Officials Accountable Residents should not learn at the decisive meeting that live public comment is off the table.
Water, power, air, land, tax, and financial-interest records should be public before trust is demanded.
Officials who approved this project should disclose every financial interest — and put it in writing that they won't profit privately.
The Public-Trust Test • Did residents receive a real hearing? • Is there a complete water accounting? • Do voters know who benefits? • Are the limits enforceable? • Can the people still change the outcome? • Do taxpayers know what custom tax rates and rebates are being offered — and whether ordinary Utah businesses are offered the same terms?
The public-trust test has not been met Promises don't substitute for answers.
Utahns deserve both.
The Box Elder Data Center — formally known as the Stratos Project — is not a zoning question.
It is a 40,000-acre energy and technology campus that will bind Box Elder County for 50 years, approved by appointed authorities and a three-member commission before residents had a complete public record on water, power, land use, financial interests, or long-term accountability. [1][2][7] Supporters say it will strengthen national security, attract investment, and create jobs.
Those claims may have merit.
But where will the water come from?
Did the approvers serve the public interest, or their own?
And will the jobs pan out?
Box Elder County residents and Utahns didn't get a vote or even a hearing.
They got a fait accompli — one that hands over life-sustaining water and hard-earned tax dollars to a billionaire's project — for 50 years.
Utah doesn't have a data center problem.
It has a self-government problem.
Utah can have data centers.
What it cannot afford is a political class that treats citizens as irrelevant, refuses transparency, and views public trust as an opportunity for private gain.
This process does not pass the public-trust test until these questions are answered.
