That is not being anti-growth.
That is asking for responsible growth.
Utah needs more housing.
Families need places to live, and our state's affordability crisis is real.
The question is not whether to keep building.
The question is whether housing and the infrastructure to support it can move at the same pace, so families end up with both.
Right now, in many fast-growing communities, they do not.
And residents are left trying to navigate a maze of agencies, acronyms, timelines, and funding processes while living with the daily consequences.
City leaders may say they are coordinating with MAG.
MAG may say projects are moving through the regional transportation process.
UDOT may control certain roads.
The Legislature writes the rules.
Developers move projects forward once approvals are in place.
Everyone may be technically correct about their role.
But residents experience the outcome as one thing: more growth without enough roads, schools, water planning, and emergency access to support it.
That is the gap Utah needs to fix.
Not with more meetings.
With a law.
What cities can do now, and where the limits are Cities are not powerless.
They plan through general plans, zoning ordinances, subdivision rules, development agreements, impact fees, capital facilities plans, transportation planning, and public hearings.
They can also adopt a temporary land-use regulation, sometimes called a moratorium, when there is a compelling public interest.
But under Utah law, that temporary regulation can last no more than 180 days, and it cannot impose a new impact fee or financial requirement on development.
(Utah Code §§ 10-9a-504 and 17-27a-504.) Cities can charge impact fees to help pay for the public facilities needed because of new growth.
But those fees are limited by state law to the cost of serving new development.
They cannot be used to fix existing deficiencies in facilities that already serve current residents.
(Utah Impact Fees Act, Title 11-36a.) Utah also has strong vested-rights protections for developers.
Once a complete land-use application is submitted and complies with the ordinances in place at that time, the applicant is generally entitled to have that application reviewed under those rules.
(Utah Code §§ 10-9a-509 and 17-27a-508.) That means cities may have flexibility before applications are filed, but much less once projects are already in the pipeline.
Where the system breaks down This is where the problem shows up for fast-growing communities.
A city may know roads are strained.
Residents may know traffic is unsafe.
Families may see school capacity, water, emergency access, and transportation infrastructure falling behind.
But once development applications are complete, vested, or already approved, the city may not have a simple legal tool to say: pause, the infrastructure is not ready.
A 180-day moratorium may help a city study a problem or update an ordinance.
It is not long enough for a fast-growth community to complete an infrastructure capacity review, coordinate with UDOT, MAG, Utah County, school districts, water providers, and developers, and create a real funding and phasing plan.
Impact fees help, but they are tied to the impact of new development.
They are not designed to solve every existing road, school, water, or emergency access deficiency.
Vested rights protect developers from constantly changing rules, but they can also make it harder for cities to respond when the real-world impact of growth becomes clear after approvals are already moving forward.
So the system can leave cities stuck between two realities.
They are expected to manage growth responsibly.
But they may not have strong enough tools to slow, phase, or condition major new growth when infrastructure is already behind.
That is not a problem more meetings can fix.
It is a problem the law has created, and one the law can correct.
Other states have tried versions of this Utah would not have to invent this idea from scratch.
Maryland counties have used "Adequate Public Facilities Ordinances" since 1973.
Florida's 1985 Growth Management Act used a similar concurrency framework.
Communities in Montana, New York, California, and Colorado have used variations.
The basic idea is straightforward: development should be timed with the roads, schools, water, sewer, emergency services, and public facilities needed to support it.
The track record of these tools is genuinely mixed.
Research on Maryland's experience has found that, in some cases, they have correlated with higher housing prices and have deflected growth to less-regulated areas.
(See the University of Maryland's 2025 review of Maryland APFOs.) That matters.
Any tool designed to slow approvals when infrastructure is over capacity has to be carefully designed so it does not make Utah's housing affordability problem worse.
But the fact that other states have struggled to get the design right is not a reason for Utah to avoid the question.
It is a reason to study what has worked, what has not, and build a version that fits Utah.
What a real law would have to do A Growth-With-Infrastructure law for Utah would need to meet a clear set of requirements.
It is not enough to declare good intentions.
The law would have to actually change how decisions get made.
At minimum, any real version would have to: Require an infrastructure readiness finding before major residential development is approved.
Instead of approving growth first and answering infrastructure questions later, cities would have to publicly show whether roads, schools, water, emergency access, and transportation funding are ready to support what they are about to approve.
Plain-English findings.
Not buried in a packet.
Give fast-growth cities stronger authority to phase or condition major residential approvals when infrastructure is demonstrably over capacity.
Not a vague anti-growth tool.
A targeted authority tied to measurable concerns: road capacity, crash risk, emergency access, school capacity, water availability, or unfunded transportation improvements.
Extend or modify the current 180-day temporary land-use tool for fast-growth infrastructure emergencies.
If a city meets specific growth or infrastructure stress thresholds, state law should allow a longer infrastructure capacity pause while the city completes a real public plan with the agencies and providers involved.
Require real phasing.
Approvals tied to infrastructure milestones.
A certain number of homes can move forward before a road improvement is complete.
The next phase pauses until the signal, the road widening, the secondary access point, or the emergency access requirement is funded or built.
Housing does not stop.
It sequences with what supports it.
Require a public dashboard for fast-growing communities.
Residents should not have to dig through city agendas, UDOT pages, MAG documents, planning packets, and legislative bills to understand what is happening in their own community.
A dashboard should show what is planned, who is responsible, whether it is funded, when it is expected, what is delayed, and how the public can weigh in.
A public process is not truly public if only insiders understand how it works.
These are not optional features.
They are what makes the difference between a real law and a press release.
What this would mean for cities, residents, and developers A well-designed law would give cities clearer legal authority to manage growth when infrastructure is already behind.
It would give residents a process they can actually follow.
It would give developers clearer expectations upfront, instead of letting conflict erupt later.
It would help the state by reducing the pattern of approving housing first and trying to solve infrastructure afterward.
Most importantly, it could help restore public trust.
People are not frustrated because they hate growth.
They are frustrated because they do not know who is responsible, what is funded, what is delayed, what can be accelerated, or when their input actually matters.
That disconnect creates anger and distrust.
A real law would bring the public in earlier and make accountability clear.
Growth should not leave residents in the dark This is not about blaming one person, one city, or one agency.
It is about recognizing that the system is not keeping up with reality, and the only thing that fixes a broken system is changing the rules it runs on.
The old approach may have worked when growth was slower.
It is not working for communities like Eagle Mountain, where families are living with the consequences of growth every day.
Utah can do better.
Growth should not mean residents are left in the dark.
Growth should not mean infrastructure comes years later.
Growth should not mean private profit now and public burden later.
Responsibility can be shared.
Accountability cannot be vague.
Utah needs a Growth-With-Infrastructure law that gives cities better tools, gives residents better information, and makes sure development does not outpace roads, schools, water, emergency access, and public safety.
Growth is not the problem.
Growth without infrastructure, transparency, and accountability is.
