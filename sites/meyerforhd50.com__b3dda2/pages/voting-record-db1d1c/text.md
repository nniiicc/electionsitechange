HB 575 · 2026 · Roads & Growth Verified Verify roll call HB 575 – Utah Legislature Gas Tax Cut Paid From the General Fund HB 575 reduced Utah's state gas tax by 15% (about 6 cents per gallon) from July 1, 2026 through December 31, 2026.
The original proposal would have paid for the cut by taxing refineries or oil exports.
The final version of the bill removed those provisions and instead pays for the tax cut with approximately $12 million from the state's general fund.
The bill also includes provisions to streamline pipeline permitting and an agreement with refineries to increase fuel production by 12.5%.
Rep.
Gricius Voted Yes Voted YES on HB 575.
Roll call available at le.utah.gov.
Kristin Meyer Would Vote No "I am not opposed to gas tax relief.
Working families in Eagle Mountain are feeling the pinch at the pump, and a temporary cut is real money in their pockets.
My concern is how the cut is paid for.
Taking $12 million from the general fund means roads, schools, and public safety compete for what's left.
The original proposal would have asked refineries to contribute.
The final version put the cost on the rest of the state budget instead.
That tradeoff deserves a closer look." What this means for Eagle Mountain Eagle Mountain consistently struggles to secure its fair share of UDOT transportation funding while absorbing the impact of rapid growth.
Pulling $12 million from the general fund to pay for gas tax relief reduces what's available for road projects, school funding, and other shared priorities.
Residents deserve to see the full picture, not just the savings at the pump.
HB 60 · 2026 · Water & Environment Verified Verify roll call HB 60 – Utah Legislature Water Rights Amendments Narrowed the public's ability to participate in Utah water rights decisions.
Removed the "public welfare" standard that previously allowed the State Engineer to reject water applications harmful to recreation, ecosystems, or community interests.
Limited who can formally protest a water rights application.
Narrowed who counts as an "aggrieved person" with standing to sue.
Took effect May 6, 2026, one day after Box Elder County approved the 60-square-mile Stratos data center project, nullifying nearly 4,000 resident protests.
Rep.
Gricius Voted Yes Voted YES on February 3, 2026.
House passed HB 60 Substitute 1 by a vote of 54-17-4.
Roll call available at le.utah.gov.
Kristin Meyer Would Vote No "This bill stripped residents of the procedural tools we use to weigh in on water decisions.
In a desert state with a shrinking Great Salt Lake, residents should have more voice in how water gets allocated, not less.
The Box Elder situation is the first real test of what this looks like in practice.
Eagle Mountain could be next." What this means for Eagle Mountain Future water rights conflicts in Utah County will be evaluated under a narrower standard.
Residents who want to raise concerns about how water decisions affect recreation, ecosystems, or our community no longer have the legal grounds to protest that they had before May 6.
This matters in a community already grappling with data center water use.

HB 267 · 2025 · Workers & Families Verified Verify roll call HB 267 – Utah Legislature Public Sector Labor Union Restrictions Prohibited public employers in Utah from collectively bargaining with labor organizations representing teachers, firefighters, police officers, nurses, and other public workers.
Required unions to file extensive new financial reports and forced the Utah Education Association to withdraw from the Utah Retirement System by 2027.
The bill passed the House 42-32 on January 27, 2025, one of the closest major votes of the session.
Utahns responded by gathering more than 320,000 signatures to force a referendum, one of the largest signature drives in modern Utah history.
Facing likely defeat at the ballot box, the legislature repealed HB 267 in a special session on December 9, 2025.
Rep.
Gricius Voted Yes Voted YES on January 27, 2025.
House passed HB 267 by a vote of 42-32-1.
Roll call available at le.utah.gov.
Kristin Meyer Would Vote No "Teachers, firefighters, police officers, and nurses keep our communities running.
They deserve a voice at the table when their wages and working conditions are decided.
More than 320,000 Utahns signed the petition to repeal this bill.
The legislature eventually agreed and repealed it themselves.
I would have voted no the first time." What this means for Eagle Mountain Eagle Mountain teachers, sheriff's deputies, and firefighters were all directly affected by HB 267 during the months it was law.
The bill was ultimately repealed because Utahns made clear they didn't want it.
Residents deserve a representative who listens to that signal before voting, not after.
HB 392 & HB 366 · 2026 · Courts & Accountability Verified HB 392 roll call HB 366 roll call HB 392 – Utah Legislature Judicial Restructuring Package HB 392 created a new three-judge panel system that allows the state, and only the state, to move civil cases out of district courts where unfavorable rulings have occurred, including pending high-profile cases like Utah's redistricting and abortion lawsuits.
HB 366 was added as a backup.
If courts strike down the three-judge panel as unconstitutional, HB 366 automatically creates a new "Constitutional Court" with judges selected by a nominating commission.
The Utah State Bar formally opposed both bills, warning they create instability in the rule of law, give the state "two bites at the apple" in constitutional litigation, and could be used to bypass judges who have issued rulings the legislature dislikes.
The ACLU of Utah, the League of Women Voters, and the Utah judiciary itself raised similar concerns.
Rep.
Gricius Voted Yes Voted YES on HB 392 (House 56-15) and HB 366 (House final passage).
Both roll calls available at le.utah.gov.
Kristin Meyer Would Vote No "When the Utah State Bar, a non-partisan professional association of the lawyers and judges who work in our courts every day, formally opposes a bill, that is a signal worth listening to.
These bills give the state a procedural advantage that ordinary Utahns and small businesses do not have.
A family fighting a state policy in court should be heard by the same judges, under the same rules, as anyone else.
Courts work because they are independent.
I would have voted no." What this means for Eagle Mountain Eagle Mountain residents have the same stake in independent courts as every Utahn.
When residents challenge how a state agency is handling water, growth, or land use decisions in our community, the legal process should not be tilted in favor of the state at the start.
These bills create that tilt.
The Utah State Bar warned about exactly this outcome before the bills passed.
