A few bills made the news.
Most did not.
Life went on.
But two bills passed this session that, taken together, will quietly reshape how Utah handles water decisions for the next decade.
Both passed with limited public attention.
Both connect directly to questions Eagle Mountain residents have been asking for years.
And both deserve a closer look from anyone who cares about how growth happens in our community.
HB 76: The Bill That Got Smaller HB 76, sponsored by Representative Jill Koford, started as a strong water transparency bill.
It would have required all large data centers in Utah to publicly report their water usage.
On January 29, the bill passed the Utah House unanimously, 72 to 0.
Representative Stephanie Gricius, who represents Eagle Mountain in House District 50, voted yes.
That was the original version.
The bill then went to the Utah Senate, where it was amended.
The new version exempted any data center operational before July 2026.
That includes Meta's existing data center campus in Eagle Mountain.
The facility that started our community's data center era is now legally exempt from the very transparency requirements the bill was supposed to create.
When the amended version came back to the House for final approval on March 5, the vote was 62 to 2 with 11 members absent or not voting.
Representative Gricius was among those absent or not voting on the final concurrence.
According to ABC4 News, Eagle Mountain City's Director of Legislative and Strategic Services testified at the House committee hearing on the original bill.
The narrowing happened later, in the Senate, under what the bill's sponsor has described as industry pressure.
The end result: residents still cannot see how much water Meta uses in Eagle Mountain.
HB 60: The Bill That Removed Your Voice While HB 76 was making its way through the legislature, a second bill was moving in parallel.
HB 60, sponsored by Representative David Shallenberger, made three significant changes to how Utah handles water rights applications: It removed the "public welfare" standard that previously allowed the State Engineer to reject water rights applications harmful to recreation, ecosystems, or community interests.
It narrowed who can formally protest a water rights application, requiring protests to match the legal grounds the State Engineer can use to approve or deny.
It made it harder for residents to sue, narrowing who counts as an "aggrieved person" with standing for judicial review.
HB 60 passed the House 54 to 17 on February 3.
Representative Gricius voted yes.
The bill took effect on May 6, 2026.
The Box Elder Test One day before HB 60 took effect, Box Elder County approved a 60-square-mile data center project proposed by investor Kevin O'Leary.
The project, called Stratos, is projected to eventually generate and consume more than twice the electricity now used by the entire state of Utah.
Nearly 4,000 Box Elder residents filed formal protests against the project's water rights application, at $15 per filing.
That is more than $60,000 of residents' own money spent to exercise a public process that, one day after HB 60 took effect, was substantially narrowed.
The water rights application was withdrawn the day before HB 60 took effect, effectively nullifying those protests.
Any new application will be evaluated under a standard that no longer allows residents to raise concerns about public welfare, recreation, or broader community impact.
"It would certainly behoove the companies to file [water rights applications] today, because the state engineer has a much different standard to consider these applications than she did a couple months ago." — Kyle Roerink, Great Basin Water Network (via Axios) Why This Matters in Eagle Mountain The two bills do different things.
HB 76 affects what residents can see.
HB 60 affects what residents can say.
Together, they shift the balance between corporations and communities in a specific direction.
Companies that want to build large water-using facilities will find Utah more accommodating.
Residents who want to ask questions, file protests, or seek information will find Utah more difficult.
This matters in Eagle Mountain because we are not Box Elder, but we could be next.
The state framework that governs how water decisions happen in Eagle Mountain has changed.
The transparency requirements that were supposed to apply to data centers were narrowed before they ever reached our existing facility.
The protest rights that residents in Box Elder used and paid for will not exist in the same form for any future water rights conflict in Utah County.
Residents deserve to know this happened.
They deserve to know who voted for what.
They deserve a representative who will fight for stronger public protections, not weaker ones.
The Honest Question I am running for House District 50 because Eagle Mountain residents deserve more information than they are getting, and the residents who do ask questions deserve answers instead of pushback.
Bills like HB 60 and HB 76 do not have to be partisan questions.
They are governance questions.
They are about whether residents have a real voice in how water gets allocated in a desert state at a moment of record-low Great Salt Lake levels.
I would have voted no on HB 60.
I would have voted no on the amended HB 76.
And I would have shown up for the final vote.
Residents of Eagle Mountain, Cedar Fort, and Fairfield deserve a representative who treats public input as a feature of good governance, not an obstacle to it.
