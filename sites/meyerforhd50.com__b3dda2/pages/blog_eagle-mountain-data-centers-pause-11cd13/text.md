Kristin Meyer for HD 50 About Issues Compare Candidates NEW Blog Get Involved Donate ← Back to all posts Time to hit pause on data centers in Eagle Mountain April 17, 2026 Share: This week, the final beam went up on Eagle Mountain's third data center.
Most of my neighbors found out the same way I did: from a news article shared in our community Facebook group.
That's already a problem.
But once you look at the full picture, it gets bigger.
According to reporting from the Salt Lake Tribune and the Great Salt Lake Collaborative earlier this year, Eagle Mountain has approved five additional data centers beyond the ones already under construction.
That includes a 300-acre site owned by Google and a QTS facility breaking ground west of the Meta campus.
So when you hear "three data centers," the reality is closer to eight, with more likely on the way.
Here's what concerns me the most: Eagle Mountain City has agreed to keep Meta's monthly water consumption data private.
That's not speculation.
That's documented in the city's own agreements, reported publicly in January.
We live in one of the driest states in the country.
Utah is fighting other states in court over Colorado River water.
Our Great Salt Lake is in crisis.
Our families are being asked to cut back on lawns and landscaping.
And the people running our city have agreed not to tell residents how much water our largest data center is using.
That's not okay.
My position: pause new approvals until we actually know what we're approving I'm calling for a moratorium on new data center approvals in Eagle Mountain until two things are true: HB76 takes effect on July 1, 2026.
This bill, sponsored by Rep.
Jill Koford (R-Ogden) and signed by Governor Cox, requires new large data centers in Utah to disclose their water usage publicly.
It's a real step forward.
But it applies to new data centers going forward.
It doesn't override the agreements Eagle Mountain already made for ones already approved.
We understand the true water costs of what's already been approved.
Before we approve another single data center, Eagle Mountain residents deserve to know what the existing ones actually consume.
Not estimates.
Not promises.
Actual monthly data.
If HB76 means future facilities have to disclose, then the city should voluntarily do the same for the ones already here.
Anything less is asking residents to trust "don't worry about it" when the stakes are this high.
That's it.
Not a ban.
Not anti-growth.
Not anti-technology.
A pause on new approvals until the transparency framework catches up with the pace of construction.
This matches what I'm hearing from Eagle Mountain residents I want to share something I saw recently.
A member of the Eagle Mountain City Council posted a video saying he wants our city to be known for its open spaces.
Beautiful trails.
Parks.
Places where kids can still find wild open ground.
That's part of why a lot of us moved here.
It's why we stayed.
I agree with him completely.
And here's what that means in practice: we cannot be a city known for open spaces if we keep approving hundreds of acres of data center development without a clear picture of what those decisions cost the community in water, traffic, power grid strain, and yes, in open land that's gone forever once it's paved.
You don't get to brand yourself as a city of open spaces and the data center capital of the Wasatch Front.
You have to pick.
Being clear about what a State Rep can and can't do I want to be honest about this, because I don't want to promise things I can't deliver.
Data center approvals are made by the Eagle Mountain City Council, not the state legislature.
As your state representative, I would not be able to veto or overturn a local zoning decision.
That's not how the levels of government work, and any candidate who tells you otherwise is bending the truth.
But here's what a state representative can do: Push for stronger state-level transparency laws that apply to all Utah data centers, not just new ones.
HB76 was a good start.
I'd work to expand it to existing facilities.
Advocate publicly for local transparency about water, traffic studies, tax incentives, and grid impact.
The state rep's office has a bigger microphone than most residents realize.
I'd use it.
Push for state-level infrastructure funding to help districts like HD50 handle the growth pressures we're under: roads, water systems, schools.
If data centers are going to be an economic driver for Utah, the state should be helping communities absorb the impact.
Rethink state tax incentives for data centers.
If a company is going to use millions of gallons of our water, it's reasonable to ask whether a state-level tax break still makes sense, or whether that revenue should stay in communities like ours.
Be a bridge between residents and local officials.
When the city makes decisions that affect the whole community, residents deserve a clear channel to weigh in.
I'd make myself that channel.
The bigger pattern Data centers aren't the only thing growing too fast for our leadership to keep up with.
SR-73 is carrying traffic it wasn't built for.
Our schools are past capacity before the paint dries.
Our water supply keeps getting kicked down the road for another year, another project, another approval.
HD50 deserves leadership that will interrogate reality instead of waiting until things get worse.
That means calling for a pause when a pause makes sense.
It means being honest about what government at every level can and can't do.
And it means refusing to pretend that "open spaces" and "bring on the next 400-acre data center campus" are compatible visions.
