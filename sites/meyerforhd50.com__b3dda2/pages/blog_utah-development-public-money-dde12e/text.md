Why Not Working Families?
August 10, 2026 Share: If you're wondering why your taxes keep going up while massive developments keep getting approved, I've been wondering the same thing.
Families in Utah are getting squeezed from every direction.
Groceries cost more.
Housing costs more.
Power bills are going up.
Property taxes are going up.
Our roads are struggling to keep up with growth.
Our schools need resources.
And every time ordinary people ask where the money is supposed to come from, we hear some version of the same answer: There's only so much money to go around.
But I started digging into how Utah pays for major development projects.
And apparently, when a big development needs something, government can get pretty creative.
When developers need infrastructure, Utah finds a way Utah has powerful public development authorities most people have probably never heard of.
One is the Utah Inland Port Authority , or UIPA.
Another is the Military Installation Development Authority, or MIDA.
These authorities have access to tools that can help make massive development projects possible, including financing for roads, water, sewer, power and other infrastructure.
That doesn't automatically make them bad.
Sometimes public investment in infrastructure makes sense.
But here's what bothers me: When millions of dollars in public financing or public resources are involved, who is looking out for the taxpayer?
I started following the approval process.
That's when things got interesting.
Meet Senator Jerry Stevenson Jerry Stevenson is a longtime Utah state senator.
He's also a member of the Utah Inland Port Authority board .
In January 2025, UIPA considered a $10 million infrastructure loan for the Savage Tooele Railroad.
Stevenson was on the board.
He seconded the motion approving the loan.
But there was another safeguard.
The loan couldn't move forward with UIPA's approval alone.
It also needed approval from the Utah Legislature's Executive Appropriations Committee .
Great.
That's what checks and balances are supposed to look like.
One group approves the deal.
Another group checks their work.
Except there's a problem.
Guess who serves as the Senate chair of Executive Appropriations?
Jerry Stevenson.
When that same $10 million loan came before the committee, Stevenson chaired the meeting.
And he voted yes.
He helped approve the deal wearing one government hat.
Then he helped approve the same deal wearing another.
And apparently, that's allowed.
Then it happened again In January 2026, UIPA considered two more infrastructure loans.
One was for $1.6 million.
The other was for $4.66 million.
This time Stevenson didn't just vote for them.
He made the motions to approve them.
Then the loans went to Executive Appropriations for the Legislature's approval.
Stevenson chaired that meeting too.
And voted yes again.
Across those three loans, we're talking about $16.26 million in public financing where the same elected official participated at two separate stages of approval.
I want to be very clear about something.
I have not found evidence that Senator Stevenson broke the law.
I have not found evidence that he took a bribe.
I'm not accusing him of corruption.
In fact, that's exactly why I think we need to talk about this.
Maybe the problem isn't that someone broke the rules.
Maybe the problem is the rules.
Try doing this at your job Imagine submitting a $10 million expense at work.
Your boss tells you: "That's a lot of money.
We're going to need someone else to independently review and approve this." Fair enough.
Then you walk around the desk, sit in another chair and say: "Looks good to me." Approved.
Your boss would think you'd lost your mind.
Yet when millions of dollars in public financing are involved, Utah's system can allow the same public official to participate at more than one stage of approval.
How is that independent oversight?
And this goes beyond three loans Stevenson doesn't just serve on the Inland Port Authority.
He also serves on the board of MIDA, another powerful Utah development authority.
And while serving on these boards, he has sponsored legislation affecting the powers and operations of the very authorities he helps govern.
Think about that.
A legislator can help write the rules.
Serve on the board operating under those rules.
Help approve financing.
Then participate in the Legislature's oversight of that financing.
Again, that may all be legal.
But legal and good government aren't always the same thing.
Here's why you should care I know what some people are thinking.
Who cares about some government board I've never heard of?
You should.
Because eventually all of this comes back to things you deal with every single day.
Roads.
Water.
Sewer.
Electricity.
Taxes.
Development.
Traffic.
The infrastructure your community needs to support explosive growth.
When major development happens, somebody has to pay for the infrastructure that makes it possible.
And families deserve to know exactly where the public's responsibility ends and the developer's responsibility begins.
Because something doesn't sit right with me about this: Government seems incredibly good at finding tools, financing mechanisms and creative solutions when major development needs infrastructure.
But when ordinary Utah families say they're drowning in housing costs, taxes and utility bills?
Suddenly we're told there's only so much government can do.
When teachers need resources, there's a budget.
When families need relief, there's a budget.
When residents say their roads can't handle more growth, there's a budget.
But somehow, when major development is on the table, we discover an entire alphabet soup of authorities, infrastructure banks, financing mechanisms and tax tools most Utahns didn't even know existed.
Maybe every one of those investments makes economic sense.
Then show us.
Show us the money Show us the money.
This shouldn't be complicated.
If public money or public resources are helping make a major private development possible, taxpayers should be able to answer a few basic questions: How much public money is involved?
What are we paying for?
What is the developer paying for?
Who benefits?
Who assumes the financial risk?
Who approved it?
And what happens if the promises don't pan out?
You shouldn't need a lobbyist.
You shouldn't need an attorney.
And you shouldn't have to spend your night digging through government meeting minutes after putting your kids to bed just to understand what your government is doing.
Put it online.
Put it in plain English.
And tell us before the deal is done.
Here's what I would change This isn't complicated either.
First: real independent oversight.
If you approve a financial deal while serving on the board of a public authority, you should recuse yourself when that same transaction comes before the Legislature.
You got your vote.
Someone else provides the check.
Second: tell communities earlier.
If a massive development requires substantial public financing, tax incentives or taxpayer-supported infrastructure, the people who will live with the consequences deserve meaningful notice before the important decisions have already been made.
Not after.
Not when the bulldozers arrive.
Before.
Third: show us the money.
Create a simple public disclosure for major publicly supported development projects.
Public financing.
Tax incentives.
Infrastructure commitments.
Expected tax revenue.
Financial risk.
Private investment.
And the names of every public official who approved it.
One page.
Plain English.
No treasure hunt through government websites required.
Government should work this hard for you Government should work this hard for you.
I'm not against growth.
I'm not against businesses coming to Utah.
And I'm not against investing in infrastructure when taxpayers are getting a good return.
I'm against a system that's easy to navigate if you're powerful enough to know how it works and nearly impossible to understand if you're an ordinary taxpayer trying to figure out why your bills keep going up.
Utah's working families are doing their part.
They go to work.
They pay their taxes.
They raise their kids.
They volunteer in their communities.
And increasingly, they're being asked to pay more just to stay in the communities they helped build.
They deserve a government that fights as hard to make their lives affordable as it fights to make major development possible.
So the next time someone tells you there's nothing government can do about the cost of growth, ask them a simple question: Are you sure?
Because once you follow the money, it becomes pretty clear that Utah government knows how to move mountains when it decides something is a priority.
It's time working families became one. — Kristin Meyer, Candidate for Utah House District 50 Utah's working families deserve a government that works this hard for them.
