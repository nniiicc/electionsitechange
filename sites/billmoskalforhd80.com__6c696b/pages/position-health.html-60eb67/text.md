Most people who talk about health care in a campaign have read about it.
Bill Moskal has worked in it.
For four decades he served as a healthcare executive and a consultant to hospital systems across the country: Henry Ford Health in Detroit, Stanford Health Care, Cedars-Sinai for twelve years, Tampa General for seven, Baptist Health in Miami for seven, Beaumont in Michigan, and fifteen Indian Health Service clinics and tribal hospitals.
That work was never abstract.
At Springfield Mercy Hospital, a facility a new CEO had been sent to close, Bill helped drive nursing turnover from over thirty percent down to less than five and put the hospital in the black for the first time in its history.
The lesson he took from it is the one he brings to this race: care is about people first.
Money follows when people are treated right.
"This is about people." Bill Moskal, on the philosophy that turned a failing hospital around The reality on the ground The squeeze is real, and it is hitting seniors and working families first.
District 80 has one of the largest retiree populations in Southwest Florida, and those residents are being pressed from every direction at once.
Federal insurance subsidies are shrinking, which drives up the cost of coverage for people who buy their own.
More of the cost of Medicaid is being shifted onto the state, which puts pressure on the services that vulnerable patients, nursing home residents, and people with disabilities depend on.
Prescription drug costs keep climbing.
Elder care options are thinning and working families who are still years from Medicare are getting priced out of basic coverage entirely.
Bill’s position is direct: Tallahassee has a responsibility to protect the health and welfare of its residents, but it has not lived up to it.
Medicaid serves a uniquely vulnerable population, and the state has a duty to make sure the money that flows from the federal government reaches the counties and facilities responsible for patient care, and that the entities providing that care are held to the standards they are supposed to meet.
When the state treats health care as a place to cut rather than a promise to keep, the people who paid into these systems their entire working lives are the ones who pay again.
This is also where health care and affordability meet.
People who worked their whole lives to own a home should not be forced out of it by the combined weight of insurance premiums, drug costs, and care they can no longer afford.
For seniors on fixed incomes, these are not separate problems.
They are the same problem, arriving in the same mailbox, in the same month.
"People have spent their whole lives paying into this system.
Seniors will not be taxed out of their homes, and they can’t be priced out of their care. " Bill Moskal What I can do about it A representative who understands the system well enough to fix it.
A state representative does not set Medicare or Social Security benefits; those are federal programs.
But the state decides how Medicaid dollars are spent, how care facilities are regulated, how insurance is overseen, and whether Tallahassee stands up for its residents or stands aside.
Bill knows exactly where those levers are, because he spent his career pulling them from the inside.
As your representative, he will use that knowledge, not to give speeches about health care, but to change how it works for the people of District 80.
His commitment on health care and aging is threefold, and it is specific: 1 Protect the care seniors already earned.
Defend Medicaid funding at the state level so the dollars that flow from Washington actually reach the counties, nursing homes, and facilities responsible for patient care.
Hold care providers to real standards, and make sure the state does not balance its budget on the backs of the people least able to absorb it.
2 Bring down the cost of coverage and prescriptions.
Your insurance company should not be practicing medicine from behind a computer screen.
If we are serious about lower healthcare costs, we must follow the money.
Floridians have the right to know where their healthcare premiums and dollars are going.
You should be able to see how much goes to medical care, how much to administration, executive compensation, marketing and profits including oversite of insurers.
3 Stand up for people who cannot always stand up for themselves.
A lifetime of advocacy for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, alongside organizations like Best Buddies, The Arc, and Big Brothers Big Sisters, shapes how Bill approaches vulnerable patients.
The measure of a health system is how it treats the people with the least power in it.
"I am a problem solver focused on cost, care, and accountability.
I have spent my life inside these systems.
Now I want to make them answer to District 80." Bill Moskal To protect what you earned, lower what you pay, I will defend the people who need it most., and together we will make sure Tallahassee treats the health of District 80 residents with dignity.
