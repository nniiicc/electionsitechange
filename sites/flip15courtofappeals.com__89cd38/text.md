The Legislature created the 15th Court of Appeals in 2023, and it has been hearing cases since September 2024.
It’s a statewide court with a short docket and a long reach: the civil cases that decide what state government can and can’t do.
State cases Suits against the State When Texans challenge the Governor, the Attorney General, or a state agency — over your schools, your healthcare, your utilities, your rights — the appeal lands here first.
It is the exclusive appellate venue for civil cases against the state.
Constitutional challenges Challenges to State Law Any case questioning whether a Texas statute violates the constitution flows through this court.
In under two years its judges have already ruled on marijuana policy, abortion-related funding, and campaign-finance disputes.
Business appeals Business Court Appeals Appeals from Texas’s new business courts — the high-dollar disputes between corporations, workers, and consumers — are decided here too.
The referee for corporate Texas sits on this bench.

It’s about a principle older than the court itself: no statewide court can claim true impartiality if it is insulated from accountability.

Texas has elected its judges for more than 150 years.
It’s not a perfect system, but it rests on a simple promise: the people a court serves get to choose who sits on it.
The 15th Court of Appeals started differently.
The Legislature created it in 2023 and gave it the state’s most consequential civil docket: cases involving the state and its agencies, and challenges to state laws.
In 2024, Governor Abbott appointed all three of its justices.
Not one of those seats has ever appeared on a ballot, until this November.
A court can’t answer to people it doesn’t know.
That’s why we’re not running an ad-only campaign.
We’re on the road instead, on the Texas Justice Tour, in the cities and the small towns and the places that only get remembered every two years.
We ask what’s weighing on you, and we take it back with us.
Whoever wins these races in November serves a six-year term .
Those years should be answerable to the people of Texas.
We’re running as a slate because a three-judge court changes as a whole.
Each of us brings decades of legal experience and years on the bench, and each of us believes the same things: judges should be elected, rulings should be impartial, and the power of the state must always be tempered by the rights of the individual.

Contribute to the Slate Read Our Issues Issues What’s on trial at this court Judges don’t make campaign promises about how they’ll rule — and you should be suspicious of any who do.
But how a court is chosen, funded, and run is fair game.
These are the issues of the 15th Court of Appeals.
01 / An elected court, not an appointed one The core issue of this race is the race itself.
Every seat on this court was filled by one man’s signature, and its judges have spent nearly two years ruling on statewide disputes without ever facing a voter.
November 2026 is the first — and for six years, the last — chance Texans have to decide who judges the state.
Our slate exists to restore the basic Texas principle that judicial power flows up from the people, not down from the Governor’s office.
02 / A real check on state power This court’s docket is, by design, the people versus the state: suits against the Governor, the Attorney General, and every state agency.
The state should win when the law is on its side — and lose when it isn’t.
That only works when the judges owe the state nothing.
Marc Meyer built his legal career defending Texans against state licensing boards; the slate’s shared conviction is that the power of the state must be tempered by the rights of the individual, in every case, for every Texan.
03 / Rulings without a party label We run as Democrats because that’s how Texas judicial elections work — but as Tom Baker has said throughout his career on the bench, party affiliation and political philosophy should not impact any ruling before a court.
Litigants should never be able to predict a ruling by checking who appointed the judge.
On a court that decides cases about marijuana, abortion-related policy, and campaign finance, impartiality isn’t a nicety.
It’s the whole job.
04 / Courts free of corporate money The three incumbents’ campaigns are financed by Texans for Lawsuit Reform’s PAC, energy-company PACs, and big-firm money — the same interests whose cases come before the court.
Our slate is funded by union halls, small donors, and working Texans.
When a business-court appeal or a consumer case reaches this bench, the people of Texas deserve judges whose donor lists don’t read like the appearance docket.
05 / Equal access to justice Appellate justice shouldn’t be a members-only club for the state and Fortune 500 litigants.
That means plain-language opinions, fair treatment of self-represented Texans, and a bench that reflects the people it serves — firefighters and nurses as well as corporate partners.
06 / An efficient, transparent bench Justice delayed is justice denied — especially when the case decides whether a state law stands.
Jerry Zimmerer has made court efficiency a hallmark of his judicial career: timely opinions, clear reasoning, and a docket that moves.
A court this consequential owes Texans answers, in writing, on time.
