Death Penalty Agrees Agrees Differs Church: The Georgia Catholic Conference supports ending the death penalty in Georgia and the United States. ( Georgia Catholic Conference ; Faithful Citizenship - USCCB, paragraph 67 ) Catechism: 2266 to 2267 , the 2018 text.
Kim Jackson: "I picked the death penalty as the political issue that I wanted to fight against." She served on the board of Georgians for Alternatives to the Death Penalty. ( Run For Something podcast, Oct.
20, 2020, at 21:30 ) Scott Holcomb: Co-sponsored a bill to end the death penalty in Georgia, HB 485 (2021).
Wrote the Georgia Second Look Act, HB 894 (2025), which the Georgia Catholic Conference supports.
Jeff Newlin: I support the death penalty as Georgia uses it now: reserved for the most aggravated murders, rarely applied, and reviewed for years.
I do not support expanding it beyond murder, and I will always support the safeguards that keep it rare and just.
From its founding, the Catholic Church taught that the state may put the worst criminals to death.
St.
Thomas Aquinas defended it in the 1200s, the Roman Catechism of 1566 called a lawful execution an act of obedience to the Fifth Commandment, and Pope Pius XII said in 1952 that a criminal, by his crime, gives up his own right to life.
In 1992, Pope John Paul II promulgated the Catechism of the Catholic Church, and its paragraph 2267 kept that teaching: public authority may punish crime with penalties matched to its gravity, not excluding, in cases of extreme gravity, the death penalty.
In 1995 his encyclical Evangelium Vitae narrowed it to cases of absolute necessity, which he called very rare, if not practically non-existent, and in 1997 that limit was written into the Catechism.
In August 2018, Pope Francis went further and rewrote paragraph 2267 to call the death penalty inadmissible, and Pope Leo XIV repeated that teaching in April 2026.
We are in the first decade of this new teaching.
I believe it remains unsettled among many Catholics, and continued discernment is needed.

Health Care Agrees Agrees Agrees Church: The Georgia Catholic Conference supported SB 427, which creates a licensing pathway for internationally trained physicians. ( Georgia Catholic Conference ) Catechism: 2288 .
Kim Jackson: Voted Yes on licenses for doctors trained abroad, SB 427 .
Scott Holcomb: Voted Yes on licenses for doctors trained abroad, SB 427 .
Jeff Newlin: I support SB 427 , which creates a limited provisional license so a doctor trained in another country can practice in Georgia under supervision and earn a full license, after the Composite Medical Board checks the school, the training, and the record.
Rural Georgia is short of doctors, and I support letting a foreign-trained physician practice here after a thorough review of his education and his practice record by the Georgia Composite Medical Board or another recognized accrediting body.
A doctor cannot simply come into the United States and begin practicing medicine in Georgia.
SB 427 does it the right way: the board verifies the medical school, the credentials, the years of practice, the record, and the English, then the doctor works 4 years under an on-site supervisor in a rural county, a hospital, or a community health center before a full license.
Let people work, but let the board verify first.
The General Assembly passed SB 427 and the Governor signed it, but the law works only when a budget funds the Board's new staff by name, and the FY2027 budget does not.
Senator Jackson co-sponsored the bill and voted for the budget that left it unfunded.
The doctors who want to practice here should pay fees that cover the cost of their own review, the way every other licensee does, and the Board should not need a new budget line to start.
