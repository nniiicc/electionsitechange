I’m not going to tell you I’m uniquely qualified for Congress.
Nobody is.
What I’ll tell you instead is the truth: I’ve lived a lot of the lives that make up this district, and when you talk to me, there’s a decent chance I’ve actually been where you’re standing.
Not read about it.
Not toured it with a camera crew.
Been there.
I GREW UP RURAL, POOR, AND RED-STATE I was born and raised in Belle Fourche, South Dakota, and spent 28 years in a rural red state — Belle Fourche, Rapid City, Spearfish.
We were poor.
Free-lunch, government-cheese poor.
My parents worked themselves raw anyway — through debt, through losing the house, through losing the business — and still scraped together church camps and choir trips for me and my sisters.
We didn’t understand what it cost them until years later.
That’s how poverty in this country actually works: the assistance and the fingers-worked-to-the-bone aren’t opposites.
Most families surviving it need both, and I don’t forget being one of them.
I don’t agree with everything rural America believes.
But I understand where farmers, ranchers, and small towns are hurting, and why they don’t trust people who fly in to explain their own lives to them.
Rural communities in South Dakota and rural communities in Arizona suffer the same squeezes: distance from services, disappearing jobs, being talked about instead of talked to.
When someone from Willcox or Pearce tells me what’s going on, I don’t need a translator.
I’VE LIVED BOTH SIDES OF THE DIVIDE I’ve lived for years in Tucson and for years in the Washington D.C. area, where I worked in network operations near Dulles and my wife taught school.
I’ve shared dinner tables with Senate speechwriters, military families, and defense contractors — and I’ve shared break rooms with people making minimum wage.
I know how the culture out there works, from the people who empty the wastebaskets to the people who write the words that come out of powerful mouths.
Most of what’s broken in American politics is two groups of people who have never lived each other’s lives shouting past each other.
I’ve lived both.
I can hear both.
I’VE DONE THE JOBS Nine years in kitchens.
I started as a dishwasher and worked my way up to kitchen manager.
Roughly 197,000 Arizonans work in restaurants and food service — it’s one of the largest employment sectors in the state.
I know what their feet feel like at hour ten.
I know what a slow Tuesday does to a paycheck built on tips, and what a health scare does to a job with no sick leave.
Four years on the retail floor.
K-Mart and Walmart, stocking shelves up to department manager.
More than 320,000 Arizonans work in retail — the fourth-largest workforce sector in the state.
I know what it’s like to have your schedule posted three days out and your life planned around someone else’s spreadsheet.
A couple of years in radio in college, doing studio production and promotion.
If you’ve ever had a job that sounds glamorous and pays in exposure, we’ve met.
Two-plus years driving southern Arizona as a Dell field service technician — Sierra Vista to Nogales to Benson, Ajo to Douglas, thousands of miles into people’s homes and businesses to tear apart their computers and put them back together.
Fixing computers is boring and I’m chatty, so mostly what I did was listen.
Produce importers telling me about a bad grape crop out of Mexico.
People who had to restructure their marriages on paper just to afford the medication keeping them alive.
You want to know this district?
Spend two years fixing its laptops.
I’VE FALLEN DOWN THE SAME STAIRS I’ve been homeless, briefly, and I was lucky enough to have family and friends to lean on, which is more than many people get.
But I know the specific fear of not having a place, and I don’t forget it.
I went to college the first time on a music scholarship and dropped out my junior year to get married.
The marriage failed.
I know what it’s like to abandon a dream, watch a relationship collapse, and rebuild from the rubble.
(I learned.
My second marriage is the best thing in my life.) In 2008 we lost our house in the housing crash, with two small kids, one of them a baby, driven largely by medical debt.
When someone tells me the system chewed them up even though they did everything right, I don’t nod politely.
I remember.
I LIVE INSIDE THE HEALTHCARE SYSTEM I live with a family managing severe medical issues, and I’ve spent years learning to navigate the medical system — the prior authorizations, the phone trees, the appeals — while holding down high-stress work.
I also live with mental illness.
I manage it with medication, therapy, and training, but I didn’t get treated until my thirties.
I know exactly what it costs to spend decades lost because help was out of reach, and I know how many Arizonans are still in that place right now.
I’ll say it plainly because our slogan demands it: I’m medicated, I’m managed, and I’m not ashamed of it.
At least I’ll admit it.
I KNOW TECHNOLOGY, AND I KNOW ITS SALESMEN I’ve worked with computers since 1980.
I’ve done enterprise architecture for an international engineering firm serving the mining industry.
I built an integrated document management system for a company with offices around the world.
I specialize in software licensing and its legal tangles, and I’ve spent the last two years deep in artificial intelligence.
So when I tell you I’m skeptical of the current AI push, it’s not fear of the unknown — it’s familiarity.
I don’t think we need an endless buildout of datacenters.
I think we need to learn to use the tools we already have safely before we race to make them more powerful.
And I understand why parents want things like age verification — I have two kids — while also understanding the danger of technology laws written by people who don’t understand the technology, advised by experts whose main expertise is making money.
I’ve worked in the corporate world and I’ve worked for years in the public sector at a major institution.
I know how both actually operate — not how they operate in press releases.
I’VE SIGNED THE FRONT OF PAYCHECKS TOO I’ve owned several small businesses: a comic store, a computer consultancy, co-ownership of an integrated health clinic, and a multimedia studio.
Arizona has 706,640 small businesses — 99.5% of all businesses in the state — employing 1.2 million people .
I know what it means to make payroll, eat the slow months, and lie awake doing math.
Small business owners aren’t an interest group to me.
They’re my old life.
AND ONE MORE THING I was bullied most of my life.
Made to feel small, unproductive, not smart.
I worked my way up anyway — in every job, every time.
I know what it’s like to be at the bottom of someone else’s opinion of you, and how hard the climb out is.
Today I have pretty much everything I’ve ever wanted.
None of it is luxury.
All of it is right for me.
(I’d like to be out of debt — like most of you.) I’m not running for Congress to get rich, get famous, or get even.
I’m running because I’ve lived enough of this district’s lives to represent them honestly.
I know what I’m doing.
I also know exactly where my limits are, which is its own kind of qualification — maybe the more useful one.
The people who scare me aren’t the ones who admit they might be wrong.
They’re the ones who never do.
NOT UNIQUELY QUALIFIED- JUST QUALIFIED If you read every candidate’s platform and pick someone else, that’s democracy working exactly the way it’s supposed to — and I built you a page to make that comparison easy.
But if you want a representative whose entire pitch is “I’ll tell you the truth and I won’t make it worse” — and who’s self-aware enough to know that shouldn’t be a rare offer — then maybe, on a ballot full of people promising you the world, the guy promising you honesty and a little less wreckage is worth writing in.
No pressure.
Read first.
Then decide.
