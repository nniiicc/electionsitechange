Public Education
Restoring Excellence and Accountability in Indiana Education
Indiana’s education system is changing quickly.
- Graduation rates are at a record high, but college-going remains far below where it was a decade ago.
Indiana’s 2025 state graduation rate reached 91.83%.
For the Class of 2024, 52.2% of graduates went directly to college, compared with 64.8% for the Class of 2014.
- Teacher pay has increased, but it has not kept pace with inflation.
Legislative Services Agency analysis found that inflation-adjusted median teacher pay declined between 2020 and 2025.
- Indiana has dramatically expanded its voucher program.
More than 80,000 students received Choice Scholarships in 2025-26, and beginning in 2026-27, the program no longer has a household-income limit.
As Indiana redesigns graduation pathways and expands school choice, we should make sure greater flexibility does not come at the expense of strong academics, sustainable public-school funding, or accountability for taxpayer dollars.
1.
Meaningful Diplomas
A diploma should prepare a student for what comes next.
I support multiple pathways to graduation, including college, careers, skilled trades, and military service, while maintaining a strong academic foundation in reading, writing, math, science, and civics.
I support
- Strong academic expectations across every graduation pathway.
- Practical financial education including budgeting, credit, taxes, insurance, and household finances.
- Career and technical education, early college programs, career academies, and magnet schools.
- Multiple pathways that recognize students have different goals without lowering expectations.
Indiana already requires personal-finance instruction, and the new Indiana Diploma includes a Personal Finance credit beginning with the Class of 2029.
We should build on that foundation so students leave school with practical skills they can use.
2.
Fund Schools for What Students Actually Need
Public education should remain a state priority, and the funding formula should better reflect what it actually costs to educate students.
Special education
Special education is one area where districts can face substantial expenses.
For 2025-26, state special-education funding ranged from roughly $550 to $11,600 per student depending on the student’s eligibility category.
In addition, districts must absorb at least $30,621.63 per IEP before state Special Education Excess Cost assistance applies to qualifying expenses.
I support
- Funding based on student needs, especially for students requiring intensive services.
- Per-student funding that reflects changing costs over time.
- Greater transparency about what the state requires and how those requirements will be funded.
- Protecting classroom resources when schools face significant enrollment changes.
If the state creates a requirement, it should also be transparent about what it costs and how it will be funded.
3.
Modernize Enrollment Funding
Indiana uses two official Average Daily Membership counts each school year to determine state tuition-support funding: one in the fall and one in the spring.
That creates challenges in both directions.
Growing districts can add students before funding fully catches up.
Declining districts still have buildings, buses, utilities, and staffing costs that cannot disappear overnight.
I support
- More responsive funding so rapidly growing districts do not wait months for dollars to follow new students.
- Gradual, state-backed transitions when enrollment declines.
- Greater funding predictability so districts can plan staffing and budgets responsibly.
- Protecting locally approved referendum dollars for the purposes voters approved.
The goal should be a funding system that responds more quickly to the students actually sitting in classrooms.
4.
Recruit and Keep Great Teachers
Indiana needs to recruit strong new educators while giving experienced teachers reasons to stay.
Teacher salaries have risen in nominal dollars, but Legislative Services Agency analysis found that inflation-adjusted median teacher pay declined between 2020 and 2025.
I support
- Competitive pay that recognizes experience and responsibility.
- Fully funding required training and professional development.
- Reducing unnecessary paperwork so teachers can spend more time teaching.
- Expanding the Next Generation Hoosier Educators Scholarship and other teacher-pipeline programs.
- Targeted incentives for shortage areas such as special education and STEM.
- Creating practical pathways for classroom aides and paraprofessionals to become licensed teachers.
- Giving educators meaningful input on planning time, classroom conditions, and school safety.
We should judge teacher policies by whether they actually help schools recruit and retain good educators.
5.
Accountability for Public Dollars
Public dollars should come with public accountability.
Traditional public schools, charter schools, and private schools participating in the Choice Scholarship program operate under different legal structures, so every requirement will not be identical.
But taxpayers should still be able to see where public money goes, how it is spent, and what results it produces.
I support
- An annual Education Transparency Report showing comparable academic and financial information wherever fair comparisons can be made.
- Strong enforcement of existing audit and financial-reporting requirements.
- Full disclosure of management companies, vendors, and related-party contracts.
- Strong conflict-of-interest protections when public education dollars are involved.
Indiana charter schools are already required to be operated by nonprofit organizations and are subject to audit requirements.
The focus should be on enforcing those rules and improving transparency around management arrangements and related-party transactions.
Fix the Dollar Law
I would also revisit Indiana’s Dollar Law governing certain unused public-school buildings.
Local taxpayers helped pay for those properties.
Communities should have a meaningful financial interest in what happens to them.
I support allowing districts greater ability to sell or lease unused buildings at fair market value while preserving reasonable opportunities for properties to continue serving an educational purpose.
6.
Support the Whole Student
Students have a harder time learning when significant mental-health, behavioral, or physical-health needs go unaddressed.
Teachers should not have to be the only line of support for students in crisis.
I support
- More licensed counselors, social workers, and mental-health professionals in schools.
- Strong services for students with disabilities.
- Support for English learners and students from low-income families.
- Strong protections against bullying and harassment.
Bring More Federal Medicaid Dollars Into Schools
Indiana expanded school-based Medicaid reimbursement in 2023.
Schools can now seek reimbursement for eligible medically necessary services connected to IEPs, 504 plans, behavioral intervention plans, service plans, and individualized health-care plans.
But Indiana could examine whether its program should go further.
As of March 2026, 28 states had taken steps to expand school Medicaid beyond IEP-only services, although the scope of those expansions varies by state.
Indiana should evaluate whether schools can draw additional federal Medicaid reimbursement for medically necessary services provided to Medicaid-enrolled students.
If an eligible service is already being provided, Indiana should make sure available federal dollars are helping pay for it.
7.
School Choice and Fiscal Responsibility
Parents should have meaningful educational options, and taxpayers should be able to evaluate what those options cost and what results they produce.
Indiana’s Choice Scholarship program served 80,548 students and cost approximately $548 million in 2025-26.
Beginning with the 2026-27 school year, the program no longer has a household-income restriction.
I support
- Restoring a reasonable income limit for Choice Scholarships.
- Clear public reporting of program costs, participation, and outcomes.
- Keeping public-school funding predictable when enrollment changes.
- Strong safeguards against conflicts of interest and misuse of education dollars.
- Regular evaluation of whether education spending is producing measurable benefits.
Parents can have choices while the state still requires accountability for public money.
Right Here in District 15
These statewide policies have significant local effects.
Fort Wayne Community Schools
In 2025-26, $46.2 million in Choice Scholarship funding went to students whose legal residence was within Fort Wayne Community Schools, the largest amount associated with residents of any Indiana school district.
That does not mean $46.2 million was literally taken from an FWCS account.
It does show the scale of state education funding now associated with private-school vouchers among students living within the district.
Northwest Allen County Schools
NACS faces a different challenge: rapid growth.
Willow Creek Middle School opened for the 2026-27 school year and was built specifically to help relieve overcrowding in the district.
A school-funding system needs to work for communities facing very different circumstances, including growing districts that are adding students, staff, and buildings.
My Education Priorities
Strong academics
Students should graduate prepared for college, careers, skilled trades, or military service.
Great teachers
Indiana should recruit good educators, reward experience, and give teachers reasons to stay.
Responsible funding
State funding should reflect student needs and respond more quickly to enrollment changes.
Accountability for public dollars
Taxpayers should be able to see where education money goes and what it accomplishes.
Smarter use of federal funding
Indiana should maximize appropriate federal reimbursement, including school-based Medicaid.
Long-term planning
Education policy should consider what decisions mean for students, schools, and taxpayers years from now.
