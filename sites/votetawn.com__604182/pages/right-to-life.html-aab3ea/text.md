Proposal 3 changed the constitution.
It did not repeal Tawn’s duty to the most vulnerable, or the legislature’s remaining power to protect children who can survive outside the womb.
What the law is today In 2022, voters passed Proposal 3 and added Article I, Section 28 to the Michigan Constitution — a “fundamental right to reproductive freedom,” including abortion.
The old 1931 criminal ban was repealed in 2023.
Democratic majorities then passed the Reproductive Health Act, stripping out most of the remaining limits.
As of 2026, Michigan has no week-based ban in force .
Abortion is treated as legal throughout pregnancy.
Courts have used Proposal 3 to strike the 24-hour waiting period and the old state-scripted counseling materials.
That is the landscape Tawn will walk into on day one.
A state senator cannot repeal Proposal 3 by passing a bill.
Changing that amendment takes the voters again.
Tawn is honest about that.
She will not pretend a first-trimester ban can be enacted by statute alone.
She will use every authority the constitution still leaves standing.
The opening Democrats refuse to use Proposal 3 itself says the state “may regulate the provision of abortion care after fetal viability” — so long as it does not prohibit an abortion a clinician says is needed to protect the mother’s life, physical health, or mental health.
That sentence is the unused tool.
It is not a first-trimester ban.
It is the power to stop elective abortions after a baby can survive outside the womb, require a documented medical reason, require hospital-level care, require a child born alive to be treated as a patient, and require reporting of late-term cases.
Four years later, Lansing has never written that statute.
In 2023, when Republicans offered a substitute that tracked the constitution’s own viability language — plus reporting of late-term abortions — Democrats rejected it and passed a repeal package instead.
Bills to define viability and limit post-viability abortion (including House Bill 5012) were left to die.
Democratic leadership has not merely “not gotten around to it.” They have opposed the bills that would have used the only door Proposal 3 left open.
Governor Whitmer signed the repeal of the 1931 ban and the Reproductive Health Act.
The Attorney General agreed in court that the waiting period should fall.
The record is clear: Michigan Democrats have treated any new protection for the unborn as an attack on Proposal 3 — even protections the amendment itself says the state “may” enact.
