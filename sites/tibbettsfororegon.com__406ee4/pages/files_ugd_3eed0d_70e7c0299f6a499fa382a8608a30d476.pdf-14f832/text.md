Legislative Goals HB 3115 Exemption This policy seeks to enable cities and counties from having to adhere to the mandated regulations imposed under HB 3115, which effectively made Martin v.
Boise the law of the land in Oregon.
Cities that invest in homeless services and prevention should also have the right to mitigate against the harmful effects of homelessness to their local businesses and residents.
As someone who has worked in the homeless nonprofit space, I believe nothing is more injurious to the efforts of homeless service providers than to create a system where it is okay to exist on the streets, as you want, doing the things you want (i.e. drugs), without so much as a city’s ability to move you.
Oftentimes, the effect of moving someone’s camp is just enough of a push to eventually influence them to seek services, sobriety, housing, and employment.
Here’s an example: Cities with populations of 100,000+ that spend 0.5% of their budget on homelessness and homeless prevention can use law enforcement and code enforcement to move people and clean up encampments; cities with populations of 25,000 or less that spend.25% of their budget, etc.
The development of this scale would be done with comprehensive input from mayors and city managers to ensure fair and achievable scales.
Cities would not be required to spend money on homeless services, but cities that are should not be expected to invest large sums of money into services without being able to balance the needs of all residents, nor create reasonable accountability among people who take advantage of homeless services.
Expand SB 1013 to Address Affordable Housing SB 1013 was passed to make it legal to rent space for a recreational vehicle, to a permanent tenant, on properties zoned rural residential.
This opened roughly 3,600 available spaces in Lane County alone.
Conversely, some cities and counties are spending $350,000 to $450,000 per unit for affordable housing in the form of taxpayer subsidy, and rents are still higher than $1,000, whereas RV space is commonly much cheaper (e.g. $500 - $600).
This law strikes a nice balance between affordable housing that is semi-permanent, and it is truly affordable.
Moreover, it should reduce our need to spend tens-of-millions of dollars annually in taxpayer subsidies to private developers to build new affordable housing units.
Expanding this existing Tibbetts 2 legislation to F2 and EFU zoning makes sense.
Moreover, many farms on EFU-zoned land would greatly benefit from this form of farmworker housing.
Shift Some Affordable (Rental) Housing Funding to Sustainable, Revolving, Loan Funding that Repays the Taxpayer Shift some of the available funding in the current budget for affordable housing to cheap, simple-interest loans to help working Oregonians become homeowners.
Too much money is being spent to build too few affordable rental housing units.
And, let’s face it, being a renter does nothing to build equity and generational wealth for your family.
Too many working families are unable to purchase a home due to downpayment requirements, coupled with today’s high interest rates.
Increasing available funding for 0% down, 2.6%-3.6% simple interest loans would dramatically increase the number of working families achieving homeownership.
This fund, once seeded, using a portion of existing affordable housing money in the budget, would also regenerate itself overtime, thus removing the need to go back to the taxpayers for more subsidy year after year.

Ensure Oregon Small Oregon Farms Remain Free of CAFO Laws In 2023, the Oregon Department of Agriculture took unilateral action – without legislative vote or involvement – to interpret a standing CAFO law, which stands for confined animal feeding operations, and began serving cease and desist orders to dozens of very small family farms.
These orders directed small family farms to stop operations until they developed a wastewater management plan.
One example is Cast Iron Farm, which has 2 cows, 6 goats, 9 sheep, and 2 ducks.
In Cast Iron’s case, it took over five months of back-and-forth with the department to finally arrive at a wastewater management plan that consisted of trapping their dairy cow’s wastewater in the milking parlor and dispose of it appropriately.
The kicker: this all came down to five, five-gallon buckets worth of wastewater.
Small family farms were outraged, and understandably so.
This was an unbridled example of government overreach.
Currently, the ODA has reversed its decision, but it is withholding the right to re-institute it.
I want to make sure this does not happen.
Most family farms operate with minimal profit, if any at all.
Such bureaucratic overreach completely flies in the face of their freedom to provide locally sourced food for their family and their community.

Restore Oregon’s Commercial Salmon Fishing Industry Commercial fishing in Oregon is big business and is the lifeblood of many small coastal communities.
But even though commercial fishing continues to bring in high dollar catches every year, Oregon fishermen are leaving the space at an alarming rate.
High fuel prices, bait prices, dockyard fees, and insurance rates (if you can get it), are being met with declining fisheries.
Namely, Salmon.
The Salmon fishery used to be a lucrative opportunity for fishermen, but not anymore, due to the extremely limited fishery.
We like to point to damming of streams and toxins in the rivers as a huge factor in Salmon’s decline, but little is mentioned of Oregon (and the West Coast’s) exploding seal population.
In the early 1900’s, there were an estimated 25,000 seals along the Oregon Coast.
Today, that number has grown to more than 600,000.
Oftentimes, these seals can be found at the mouth of major rivers, such as the Columbia, where they grab the salmon during their runs.
When the seal population is high, the salmon population is understandably lower.
We need to work with the federal government and provide supporting legislation that seeks to better manage the seal population, just as we manage other fisheries and wildlife.
I propose that we have a seal season, where the public can purchase tags, with the proceeds funding a bond and insurance pool for the fishermen who are being kicked out of ports and harbors for a lack of insurance; due not to their willingness to buy it, but because their wooden vessels are too old and deemed uninsurable by insurance companies without survey – if at all.
This would enable fishermen to purchase sufficient insurance to meet port requirements, fund vessel removal and fuel spill containment, and ensure a thriving salmon fishery once again.
Tibbetts 7 Corner Crossings Many public lands in Oregon are not accessible to the public, and they should be.
When viewing a map of Oregon, you will find a checkerboard of public land across the state (predominately BLM land), all connected by corners.
However, crossing from one block of public land to another is prohibited, cutting Oregon hunters and recreational enthusiasts off from millions of acres their tax dollars contribute towards.
In the past, this was done because conventional maps made accurate crossings difficult to pinpoint, resulting in trespassing onto private lands, which resulted in private landowners having to spend substantial sums to secure their private lands.
But today, with the advent and abundance of GPS mapping technology, we can know exactly where we are within mere feet of accuracy.
I would like to work with the Bureau of Land Management and other federal agencies, timber companies, and large private land holders to identify a safe and responsible way for the public to access their lands via respectful and accurate corner crossings.
One example that comes to mind is that every corner crossing is allowed within a fifteen-foot public easement extending on either side of a property’s surveyed fulcrum point.
Restore Responsible Antibiotic Use on Farmland Recently, the FDA banned the over-the-counter sale of all livestock antibiotics, resulting in farmers and ranchers having to call a veterinarian every time an animal gets injured or sick and requires antibiotic treatment.
On my own farm, this has caused a substantial increase in the amount of money we are spending on veterinarian care, and it has also caused an increase in the death rate of our livestock.
Before, if we had a calf with scours, we knew what to do: give it electrolytes, vitamin B, and two hits of LA-200.
But now, even though we know what to do, we have to pay for a full veterinarian visit, and we typically pay emergency visit rates.
What once cost us $60 to treat a calf with scours now costs us about $400.
This is a perfect example of one cut in a “death by a thousand cuts” that farmers and ranchers quietly deal with every day.
