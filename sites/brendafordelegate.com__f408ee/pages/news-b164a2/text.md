Maryland GOP lawmakers sue to keep redistricting amendment off November ballot
August 7, 2026
Maryland Republican lawmakers filed a lawsuit on Thursday, suing Gov.
Wes Moore and top Democratic officials over the redistricting constitutional amendment passed during this week's two-day special session.
The Maryland Freedom Caucus - far-right House Republicans - and Senate GOP leadership argue the amendment should not be placed on the November ballot for voter approval.
"Annapolis Democrats spent taxpayer dollars on a special session for one reason: to rewrite the Maryland Constitution and eliminate the last remaining Republican congressional seat," said Chair of the Maryland Freedom Caucus Del.
Matt Morgan (R-St.
Mary's County) in a statement.
"Like a banana republic, they suspended the rules, ignored statutory deadlines, and treated the Constitution as an obstacle to be removed.
Our Declaration of Rights exists to protect the people from politicians who undermine free and fair elections.
We are taking them to court to uphold these principles," the statement reads.
This past legislative session, lawmakers approved a new law that requires the secretary of state to submit ballot questions to the State Board of Elections on or before July 1.
Democrats attached an amendment to the constitutional proposal in order to exempt it from that deadline, which Sen.
Cheryl Kagan (D-Montgomery County) explained during the bill's debate in the Senate on Tuesday.
"The July 1st deadline was not something we could meet because of the date that this special session was convened," Kagan said.
"So the amendment was needed to be sure that the specific words that this body hopefully will be passing soon will be what the voters will see on their ballot in November."
But this change troubles Republican lawmakers, so much so that they think a judge could rule the exemption to be in violation of state law.
"To me, we just passed a new law, specifically about constitutional amendments, that had a deadline that we violated.
They even admitted, 'Well, we didn't get it in time.
We didn't get this done in time.' So we're adding this language into the constitutional amendment.
It's very awkward," Senate Republican Whip Justin Ready (R-Carroll and Frederick Counties) told reporters after the constitutional amendment passed in the Senate.
In the lawsuit, GOP legislators argue the deadline exemption language was not introduced until Monday morning, which deprived the public of the opportunity to fully examine the change.
They also allege that the exemption should be considered an amendment to the Maryland Constitution, not just a technical change, and should be voted on by Marylanders accordingly.
The plaintiffs also take concern with Gov.
Wes Moore's decision to ceremonially sign the constitutional amendment on Wednesday, which is not legally required of the chief executive.
"This is an improper attempt to fuse a constitutional process for putting constitutional-amendment referenda in front of Maryland's voters (a process of question formulation that does not involve the Governor) with the process for passing an ordinary law," the lawsuit reads.
The GOP lawmakers have requested the case be fast-tracked due to the November election timeline.
"This was done at the last minute.
They have to have this ballot language on Aug. 5, which is tomorrow.
And if there's anything that can prevent that from happening, then you know what would happen is exactly what we said.
This did not need to be done this year," Senate Republican Leader Stephen Hershey (R-Caroline, Cecil, Kent and Queen Anne's Counties) said on the Senate floor Tuesday.
House Speaker Joseline Pena-Melnyk (D-Anne Arundel and Prince George's Counties) and Senate President Bill Ferguson (D-Baltimore City) are not named as defendants in the lawsuit, but they both expressed confidence in the legality of the amendment-passing process prior to the lawsuit being filed.
"We did this because it was needed," Pena-Melnyk said during a press conference on Tuesday when asked about a potential lawsuit.
"The bill before you basically gives clarity as to the way the standard should be, the way it has been done historically."
"I think we did everything we could to prepare to make sure that we followed every- dotted every i, crossed every t.
Anytime it's in the courts, things are uncertain, and so I would never guarantee anything, but I think that we took every measure and made the appropriate accommodations to follow the law to make sure that this was on the ballot appropriately," Ferguson told reporters on Tuesday.
If Republicans' legal challenge succeeds, a judge could order the removal of the constitutional amendment from the November ballot.
