Proposed Rulemaking 10-242 is a sweeping overhaul of Pennsylvania's communicable and noncommunicable disease regulations (28 PA.
Code Ch.
27), published by the Department of Health in the Pennsylvania Bulletin on August 8, 2026 .
While the rule's stated goal is to modernize the list of reportable diseases, several provisions dramatically expand government authority — allowing warrantless entry into homes, private meetings between state officials and schoolchildren, and broad access to patient medical records.
Michelle Schellberg opposes 10-242 not because reasonable disease control is unnecessary, but because this rule grants government broad, standing powers without equally clear protections for the citizens subject to those powers.
The Problems Why Michelle Opposes It Proposed §27.60a Property & Warrantless Entry Health authorities could enter an apartment, building, or other location as part of an investigation upon presentation of government identification.
Michelle believes nothing in the rule should waive the requirement for consent, a warrant, or other lawful judicial process where the Constitution requires it.
Proposed §27.60c Parental Rights at School Schools would be required to let health officials meet and speak privately with students and prohibited from interfering.
Michelle holds that government access to a minor at school must have clearly defined limits and preserve parental notice and involvement whenever existing law does not specifically require confidentiality.
Proposed §27.60e Medical Privacy Health authorities would gain access to patient medical records and electronic transmission of those records.
Michelle says such authority must be limited to information actually necessary for a specific investigation, with clear standards for access, retention, security, disclosure, and accountability.
Emergency Authority Open-Ended Government Power Broad phrases like investigating “any case” or “unusual occurrences,” paired with prohibitions on citizens obstructing investigations, create sweeping discretion.
Michelle wants objective thresholds, defined limits, due-process protections, and meaningful oversight.
Michelle's Position In Her Own Words “Government can protect public health while remaining constrained by consent, warrants where required, parental rights, medical privacy, due process, and judicial oversight.” “Pennsylvania's Constitution begins from the principle that people possess inherent rights to liberty and that governmental power originates with the people.

Copy Letter Subject: Opposed — Proposed Rulemaking 10-242 (Communicable and Noncommunicable Diseases) To the Pennsylvania Department of Health and the Independent Regulatory Review Commission: I am writing to oppose proposed rulemaking 10-242.
While I support reasonable measures to control serious communicable disease, this proposal expands government authority without equal protections for the citizens subject to that authority.
I am specifically concerned about: 1.
Proposed §27.60a — Allowing health authorities to enter a home or building upon presentation of government identification.
The rule should expressly state that nothing in it eliminates the requirement for consent, a warrant, or other lawful judicial process where the Constitution requires it.
2.
Proposed §27.60c — Requiring schools to let health officials meet and speak privately with students and prohibiting school officials from interfering.
Government access to a minor at school must have clearly defined limits and preserve parental notice and involvement whenever existing law does not specifically require confidentiality.
3.
Proposed §27.60e — Granting health authorities access to patient medical records and permitting electronic transmission of those records.
Such authority should be limited to information actually necessary for a specific investigation, with clear standards for access, retention, security, disclosure, and accountability.
4.
Emergency authority — Broad phrases such as investigating "any case" or "unusual occurrences," combined with prohibitions on citizens obstructing investigations, create substantial discretion.
The regulation should contain objective thresholds, defined limits, due-process protections, and meaningful oversight.
Government can protect public health while remaining constrained by consent, warrants where required, parental rights, medical privacy, due process, and judicial oversight.
Public health and individual freedom do not have to be opposing principles.
I respectfully ask that the Department of Health amend the proposed rulemaking to include these protections before it is finalized.
