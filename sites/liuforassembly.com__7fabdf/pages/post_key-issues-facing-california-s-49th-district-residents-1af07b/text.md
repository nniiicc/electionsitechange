These dedicated professionals, often hailing from the very neighborhoods they protect, embody resilience and sacrifice, patrolling streets lined with family-owned businesses, historic temples, and bustling markets.
Yet, beneath this facade of community harmony lies a deepening crisis: chronic underfunding, debilitating staffing shortages, and a societal undervaluation that hampers their ability to deliver timely justice.
As a candidate rooted in this district, with family ties to local entrepreneurs and educators, I've witnessed firsthand how these issues erode trust and amplify vulnerabilities.
This isn't a recent anomaly but a culmination of decades of policy missteps, exacerbated by recent economic pressures, shifting priorities, and even federal proposals that threaten further erosion of support.
The roots of this crisis trace back to the early 1990s, when California's police staffing peaked amid a national crime wave and subsequent "tough on crime" initiatives like the Three Strikes Law.
However, the Great Recession of 2008 marked a turning point, slashing local budgets and forcing departments to downsize.
In Los Angeles County alone, which includes our district, agencies lost thousands of positions, a trend that persisted through austerity measures.
Fast-forward to the post-pandemic era: anti-police sentiment following high-profile incidents, combined with inflationary pressures and federal funding uncertainties, has intensified the strain.
Notably, the Trump administration's proposed 2026 budget sought to cut $1 billion from Department of Justice grant programs supporting police departments nationwide for violent crime reduction, hate crimes, and violence against women—ripples felt locally where state support already falls short.
In our district, this translates to officers juggling multiple roles—patrol, investigations, community outreach—amid rising calls for service, leaving residents feeling less secure in their daily lives.
Current statistics paint a grim picture.
According to the latest research from the Peace Officers Research Association of California (PORAC) in late 2025, California averages fewer than two sworn officers per 1,000 residents—nearly half the national average of 3.5—marking one of the lowest staffing levels in three decades.
Only two of the state's 58 counties exceed the national benchmark, with Los Angeles County falling firmly in the bottom tier at three or fewer.
Statewide, the profession has lost over 3,300 sworn officers and 400 civilian staff since 2020, despite slight recent upticks.
Locally, the Monterey Park Police Department, serving over 60,000 residents, operates with strained resources, as reflected in ongoing issues with vehicle burglaries and thefts amid persistent staffing gaps.
Alhambra reports burglary rates around 92 per 100,000, while San Gabriel maintains relatively low violent crime at approximately 4.7 per 1,000, yet both remain vulnerable to the broader trend where 78% of agencies struggle with recruitment and retention.
These shortages directly impact response times and investigations: PORAC data shows staffing as the strongest predictor of delays, with examples like Long Beach seeing highest-priority calls take over 20 minutes longer than in 2021, and statewide Priority 1 responses often exceeding targets in understaffed areas.
These shortages directly impact response times and investigations.
PORAC links staffing to efficiency, noting that low ratios cause delays: in Santa Clara County, Priority 1 calls average 7.3 minutes (over target), and Priority 2 calls hit 24 minutes (double the goal).
In Los Angeles, response times for highest-priority calls have increased by over 20 minutes since 2021 due to vacancies.
Sacramento projects staffing below 600 officers—half needed—while rural areas like Tehama have eliminated daytime patrols entirely.
In our urban-suburban mix, this means burglaries in Alhambra or thefts in Monterey Park often go under-investigated, fostering a sense of impunity.
Retail closures statewide, driven by unchecked crime, underscore the economic toll, as businesses in our district's commercial corridors face repeated hits without swift follow-up.
Funding woes perpetuate this cycle.
Local governments allocate over 14% of budgets to policing, yet totals lag behind needs.
The 2025-26 state budget proposes modest increases for the Department of Justice but delays forensic funding shifts, costing counties millions.
In Los Angeles, Mayor Karen Bass urged $4.4 million for 410 new officers in December 2025, warning of no academy classes in January 2026 without it.
Oakland's union counts 649 sworn officers, with over 100 on leave, prompting plans for five academies to reach 700.
Hiring processes, lasting up to a year for 47.5% of departments, deter applicants amid burnout and emotional strain.
Comparisons to other states highlight California's lag.
Florida and Texas, with lower taxes and pro-law enforcement policies, boast higher officer ratios and faster responses.
Yet, even in San Francisco, Mayor Daniel Lurie spared police from 15% cuts in 2025 amid an $876 million deficit, recognizing safety's priority.
The human cost is profound.
Officers endure overtime, missing family time, as PORAC President Brian R.
Marvel notes.
In our district, families face delayed justice for property crimes, eroding community bonds.
A personal anecdote: a local business owner in San Gabriel, a family friend, waited weeks for an investigation into a break-in, highlighting the frustration that builds when help feels distant.
Solutions demand action: reallocate funds for competitive salaries (e.g., El Monte's 5% raises in 2025), signing bonuses, and advertising.
Streamline hiring, invest in outreach to rebuild trust, and advocate federally against cuts.
As assemblymember, I'll prioritize bipartisan reforms, drawing from successful models like Berkeley's staffing assessments and PORAC's calls for efficient recruitment pipelines.
Our officers deserve resources; our communities demand security.
Let's restore justice together, ensuring that the families, businesses, and cultural heritage of Monterey Park, Alhambra, and San Gabriel are protected by a well-supported, adequately staffed police force that can respond swiftly and effectively to every call.
