top of page Home Blog Blog Post Issue Comparison Blog Post Issue Comparison Issues Media Contact Us Endorsements Media Press DONATE Blog Post Issue Comparison Debunking the 2026 Billionaire Tax Act: A Recipe for Economic Ruin Long Liu Office Mar 4 5 min read Updated: Mar 5 California's economy stands as a beacon of global envy, pulsating with innovation and prosperity that springs not from the heavy hand of government decrees but from the unbridled ingenuity of free markets.
This dynamic system has birthed tech giants, fostered entrepreneurial dreams, and elevated living standards for millions.
Yet, looming threats like the proposed 2026 Billionaire Tax Act risk undermining this very foundation, seducing voters with illusory promises of swift fiscal remedies while blithely disregarding the well-documented dangers of excessive taxation.
As a candidate vying for the 49th Assembly District seat, I've witnessed up close how overreaching government interventions choke off opportunities, stifle small businesses, and erode the competitive edge that makes California a powerhouse.
This act, which imposes a one-time 5% levy on the net worth of residents exceeding $1 billion as of January 1, 2026, represents a quintessential example of shortsighted policy-making that could hemorrhage jobs, drain capital, and hamstring long-term economic growth.
To truly appreciate the stakes, let's delve deeper into the triumphs of capitalism.
This economic model has proven time and again its unparalleled ability to generate wealth, enhance comfort, and unlock opportunities that no other system can match.
Consider the historical arc: From the Industrial Revolution's mechanized wonders to the digital age's silicon miracles, free markets have driven progress by rewarding risk-takers and innovators.
In California, this is vividly illustrated in our world-renowned tech hubs like Silicon Valley and the Bay Area, where companies such as Apple, Google, and Tesla have revolutionized industries, creating trillions in value and employing hundreds of thousands.
These successes aren't accidents; they're the fruits of a system where individuals pursue self-interest, leading to collective benefits through voluntary exchange.
Free markets allocate resources efficiently, spur competition that lowers prices and improves quality, and adapt swiftly to changing demands—far outperforming centrally planned alternatives, as evidenced by the collapse of socialist experiments in the 20th century.
Of course, government has a vital role in providing essentials like infrastructure, education, and public safety, which serve as the bedrock for market activities.
However, California's approach has veered into overreach, imposing a burdensome regulatory and tax regime that yields increasingly marginal returns.
Residents endure some of the nation's steepest taxes—sales taxes up to 10.25%, a top income tax rate of 13.3%, and hefty property taxes—yet the bang for our buck is woefully inadequate.
Our infrastructure woes are a glaring indictment: The California High-Speed Rail project, initially pitched in 2008 at a modest $33 billion for a bullet train linking Los Angeles to San Francisco, has ballooned to over $128 billion with scarcely a mile of operational track to show for it.
Plagued by endless delays, environmental lawsuits, land acquisition battles, and bureaucratic red tape, the project's completion is now optimistically slated for sometime after 2033, if ever.
This isn't an isolated fiasco; consider the Oroville Dam crisis in 2017, where deferred maintenance led to a near-catastrophic spillway failure, costing $1.1 billion in repairs and exposing systemic neglect.
Or the state's water infrastructure, riddled with aging pipes and inefficient allocation, exacerbating droughts despite billions in bonds.
These examples underscore how government inefficiency squanders taxpayer dollars, diverting funds from productive uses and eroding public trust.
Empirical data reinforces the case for restraint: Economies thrive under lower tax burdens.
States eschewing income taxes, such as Florida, Texas, Nevada, and Tennessee, consistently outperform high-tax peers like California and New York in key metrics.
Between 2010 and 2019, no-income-tax states saw GDP growth of 18.3%, compared to just 14.3% for those with the highest rates.
Drilling down, research from the Tax Foundation indicates that a 1% reduction in corporate taxes can elevate GDP by 0.2% to 0.6% over time, as businesses invest more in expansion and hiring.
The inverse holds true: Elevated taxes breed economic stagnation.
California's punishing 13.3% top marginal income tax rate has fueled a mass exodus, with the state hemorrhaging $24 billion in adjusted gross income (AGI) in 2022 alone, as high-earners flock to more hospitable climates.
This outmigration isn't limited to individuals; it's a broader trend, with net domestic migration losses exceeding 300,000 people annually in recent years, straining housing markets elsewhere while leaving California with fewer taxpayers to foot the bill.
Enter the Billionaire Tax Act, a proposal ostensibly designed to rake in $100 billion through a wealth tax on unrealized gains assessed as of December 31, 2026.
This retroactive measure not only targets accumulated assets but does so in a way that incentivizes preemptive flight, as residents scramble to relocate before the cutoff.
The fallout is already evident: Prominent billionaires like Google's Larry Page, PayPal co-founder Peter Thiel, and Tesla's Elon Musk have either departed or signaled intentions to do so, collectively representing over $1 trillion in relocated wealth.
By January of this year, at least six such ultra-wealthy individuals had exited, with reports indicating 20 more contemplating the move—their combined $1.3 trillion in assets underpinning an estimated 50,000 high-paying jobs in tech, finance, and related sectors.
This isn't mere speculation; historical patterns confirm it.
Following California's 2012 tax hikes, outmigration among top earners surged by 0.8%, per IRS data.
Similar dynamics played out in New Jersey after its millionaire tax, which saw a net loss of $5.5 billion in AGI over a decade.
The most poignant victims aren't the billionaires themselves but everyday Californians, particularly the vulnerable.
When wealth creators flee, they take investment capital, philanthropic dollars, and job opportunities with them.
California's corporate headquarters exodus—resulting in 77,600 lost jobs since 2020—stems directly from this hostile tax environment, according to analyses by the Hoover Institution and others.
For instance, Oracle's Larry Ellison, by relocating to Hawaii, potentially dodges $9.6 billion in taxes, but more critically, his company's shift deprives California of economic multipliers like supplier contracts and employee spending.
Small businesses suffer too: Reduced venture capital—down 15% in high-tax states per PitchBook data—means fewer startups get funded, stifling innovation in emerging fields like AI and biotech.
Low-income communities bear the brunt, facing higher unemployment and strained social services as tax revenues paradoxically decline due to the exodus.
At its core, the appeal of this tax is intuitive but economically flawed.
Proponents argue it levels the playing field, yet it ignores fundamental principles: Low taxes catalyze growth by encouraging investment and risk-taking.
The 2017 federal Tax Cuts and Jobs Act provides a clear case study, boosting business investment by 20% in its first year and lifting GDP by approximately 0.78% per percentage point of rate reduction, according to Congressional Budget Office estimates.
Extending this, studies from the National Bureau of Economic Research show that wealth taxes in Europe have historically raised far less revenue than projected while depressing savings and entrepreneurship.
California's persistent strategy of layering on more burdens only exacerbates inequality and hampers prosperity for all strata.
Instead, we must pivot to pro-growth policies that unleash potential: Streamline permitting processes to cut red tape that delays projects by years; reduce corporate and income taxes to competitive levels, perhaps mirroring successful models like Ireland's 12.5% rate that attracted global firms; invest in workforce development through vocational training and apprenticeships; and incentivize innovation with R&D tax credits.
By rejecting the Billionaire Tax Act and embracing these reforms, we can safeguard California's economic vitality, ensuring it remains a land of opportunity not just for the elite but for every resident aspiring to build a better future.
Let's preserve the free market magic that made us great, fostering a rising tide that lifts all boats rather than sinking the ship with misguided interventions.
