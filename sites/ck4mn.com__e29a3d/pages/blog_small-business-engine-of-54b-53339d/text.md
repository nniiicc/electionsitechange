Small Business Is the Engine of District 54B
There is a particular kind of quiet that happens in a small business on the 14th of the month.
Payroll clears on the 15th.
You have looked at the same account balance four times since breakfast.
Two customers who promised to pay last week have not.
And you are doing arithmetic in your head that no spreadsheet is going to make friendlier.
I have had that quiet.
It is one of the reasons I am running.
What a business teaches that a talking point cannot
When you own a small business, you do not get to be theoretical.
A policy is not an argument you win on television.
It is a line item, a form, a fee, a wait, a hire you make or a hire you postpone.
You learn very fast which rules are protecting somebody and which ones are just there.
That distinction matters, and it is the one I most want to bring to St.
Paul.
I am not running as someone who thinks every regulation is a burden.
Some rules are the reason your contractor is licensed, your water is clean and your kid's daycare is safe.
I am running as someone who has actually filled out the paperwork, and who can therefore tell the difference between a rule that does work and a rule that only makes work.
The three questions I ask about anything that touches a business
· Does it solve a problem that actually exists?
Not a problem someone imagines might exist, and not a problem that was solved three sessions ago and never revisited.
· Who pays for it, and can they?
A compliance cost that a large employer absorbs as a rounding error can be the difference between a small shop hiring a second employee or staying a one-person operation.
· Can a reasonable person comply without hiring a professional to explain it?
If the answer is no, the rule is not finished.
None of that is a partisan test.
I have watched proposals from both parties fail all three, and I have watched good ideas from both parties pass all three.
Which is rather the point.
What this looks like in District 54B specifically
Our district is not one economy.
It is several, sitting next to each other.
Downtown Prior Lake and downtown Jordan run on small storefronts where the owner is often also the bookkeeper, the marketing department and the person who shovels the walk.
Shakopee carries larger employers and the supplier network that grows up around them.
Our townships -- Jackson, Louisville, Sand Creek and Spring Lake -- hold farms and family operations where the business and the household are the same balance sheet.
Those are genuinely different needs.
A permitting delay is an annoyance to a large firm with a compliance department and an existential problem for a two-person shop.
A workforce housing shortage reads as a recruiting statistic in a corporate report and as "I cannot find anyone who can afford to live near this job" to a Jordan restaurant owner.
Representing 54B well means refusing to pretend those are the same conversation.
Three things I want to work on
1.
Make the state easier to deal with.
Not smaller for its own sake -- clearer.
Plain-language forms, predictable timelines, one place to get an actual answer.
A small business owner's scarcest resource is attention, and the state spends it carelessly.
2.
Treat workforce housing as economic development.
Our employers cannot hire people who cannot afford to live here.
That is not a housing story or a business story.
It is one story, and it is holding this district back.
3.
Connect our schools to our employers on purpose.
We already have real assets here -- the Academies of Shakopee and the CAPS program are doing work that other districts study.
The state's job is to fund and scale what is already working, not to invent a new initiative so someone can announce it.
The part I will not do
I will not tell you I can eliminate your costs, cut your taxes, raise your revenue and expand services at the same time.
You run a business.
You know that arithmetic does not close.
Anyone who promises you all four is either not serious or does not respect you enough to say so.
What I will tell you is that I will read the bill.
I will ask who pays.
I will vote against things my own party wants when the answer to "does this actually solve a real problem" is no.
That is not heroism, it is the job description.
Why I keep coming back to the 14th of the month
Because the people making rules for small businesses in this state are, with real exceptions, not people who have ever waited on a customer's check to make payroll.
That is not a character flaw.
It is just a gap.
And gaps in experience become gaps in policy.
I have run a business.
I have hired people and worried about keeping them.
I have read a regulation three times trying to figure out whether it applied to me.
When a bill lands on my desk that touches somebody's livelihood in Prior Lake or Jordan or Sand Creek Township, I will know what the quiet on the 14th feels like.
That is not the whole qualification for this job.
But it is not a small one either.
If you own or run something in 54B, I would genuinely like to hear what the state has made harder than it needs to be.
Not as a campaign exercise -- as a to-do list.
Reach out any time.
