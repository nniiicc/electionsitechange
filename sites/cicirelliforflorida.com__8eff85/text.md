The Punch List Where I Stand Seven issues in plain language.
The work that needs doing.
01 Equality Without Compromise The wealthiest nation in the history of the world has no excuse for failing to ensure every Floridian can live a healthy, dignified life.
Equal protection by the law and from the law is not optional.
It is the baseline.
Your right to love who you love, to make your own medical decisions, to protect your bodily autonomy, and to defend yourself and your family are nonnegotiable.
No exceptions.
Fear is not governance.
When the state uses fear as a tool for control, there is a word for that, and the word is coercion.
I will not govern that way, and I will fight anyone who does.
What I'll Do Vote no on every preemption bill that strips local anti-discrimination protections.
In the 2026 session alone, Tallahassee filed bills to ban local DEI initiatives and restrict how cities protect their residents.
That stops with me.
Co-sponsor or introduce a Florida Equal Rights Act modeled on state-level nondiscrimination protections that cover sexual orientation, gender identity, and gender expression in housing, employment, and public accommodations.
Florida is one of the few states with no comprehensive statewide nondiscrimination statute.
Oppose any bill restricting reproductive healthcare access , including restrictions on contraception, IVF, and medication access.
Fight to restore the viability standard that existed before the 6-week ban.
Support legislation expanding access to self-defense training and responsible firearm safety programs at the community level.
"Defend yourself and your family" should come with resources, not just rhetoric.
02 Home Rule For 28 years, one party has controlled Tallahassee, and they have used that power to silence your local leaders on everything from lagoon protection to housing to worker safety.
Your city and county officials cannot pass common-sense ordinances without the threat of state punishment or corporate lawsuits.
The decisions that shape your daily life are being made by people who have never set foot in your neighborhood.
The people closest to a problem should be the ones who solve it.
I am running to bring government back home, where it belongs, and to make sure your local leaders answer to you instead of a coercive Tallahassee.
What I'll Do Vote no on every preemption bill that overrides local authority without a compelling statewide interest.
The 2026 session had more than 60 preemption bills filed, more than any other state.
I'll be a consistent no vote on that agenda.
Co-sponsor repeal of SB 180 , the post-storm preemption law that blocks cities and counties from passing stronger building and development standards after hurricanes.
Our local leaders know what our communities need after a storm.
Tallahassee doesn't.
Introduce a Home Rule Protection Act requiring a two-thirds supermajority in both chambers to preempt local ordinances on housing, wages, environmental protection, or public health.
Make it harder to silence your local government, not easier.
Restore local authority on zoning and land use by opposing Live Local Act expansions that strip cities of the ability to manage development in their own neighborhoods.
Density decisions should be made by the people who live there.
03 The Disappearing Hospital Problem There is no single fix for healthcare access in North Brevard.
Our local hospitals are closing due to aging infrastructure and engineering concerns.
Those concerns are legitimate, but the consequences are a direct result of decades of failed planning, and the people living here are paying for it.
Access to care in our area is among the worst in the nation.
Ambulance transit times are up.
Healthcare outcomes are down.
The new hospital being built on Merritt Island will help, but it will not solve the problem on its own.
The issue is capacity: we need more beds, more doctors, and more ambulances.
I will work with public regulators, private healthcare partners, and local advisory boards to expand healthcare access in our district as fast as possible.
I want my children to be born here.
I will build a district where that is a safe choice to make.
What I'll Do Introduce legislation requiring a Healthcare Impact Assessment before any hospital closure in a medically underserved area.
When Orlando Health closed Rockledge Hospital in April 2025, the community had almost no warning and no transition plan.
Closing a hospital should require a public process with a minimum 12-month notice period and a binding plan for replacement services.
Fight for state funding to subsidize ambulance services in North Brevard during the gap period while new facilities are being built.
New facilities have been announced, but construction takes years.
In the meantime, ambulance response times are up and neighboring ERs are overwhelmed.
Support expanding the scope of practice for advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs) and physician assistants in underserved areas to increase the number of providers available in North Brevard without waiting for new physicians to relocate.
Push for a North Brevard Healthcare Task Force in Tallahassee: a formal legislative workgroup that brings Orlando Health, Health First, Parrish Medical Center, Brevard County EMS, and community stakeholders to the same table with a mandate to report back on capacity gaps and a timeline for closing them.
Advocate for Medicaid expansion to reduce the uncompensated care burden that makes rural and suburban hospitals financially unviable in the first place.
Hospitals close when they can't get paid.
Expanding coverage keeps facilities open.
04 Save Our Indian River Lagoon There is no version of representing this district that does not include protecting and restoring the Indian River Lagoon.
Full stop.
The Save Our Indian River Lagoon program is one of the best-run environmental restoration organizations in the state.
Their financial transparency is second to none, their data-driven approach keeps them on mission, and their results speak for themselves.
The way SOIRL operates should be a model for how every government program runs.
I support continuing and expanding these efforts, and I encourage every voter in the district to vote yes on the half-cent sales tax that funds this critical work.
The lagoon is not a political issue.
It is the thing that makes this place worth living in.
What I'll Do Campaign actively for the November 2026 renewal of the Save Our Indian River Lagoon half-cent sales tax.
This is on the ballot this year.
It raises $59 million annually, has completed 111 restoration projects since 2016, and has leveraged over $110 million in matching grants.
Renewing it is non-negotiable.
Introduce or co-sponsor state legislation increasing penalties for agricultural and industrial runoff violations that contribute to algal blooms and nutrient loading in the lagoon.
Current fines are a cost of doing business.
They need to be a deterrent.
Fight for dedicated state matching funds for the SOIRL program.
Right now, Brevard taxpayers are funding this almost entirely at the local level.
The lagoon spans five counties and is a statewide natural resource.
Tallahassee should be matching local dollars, not ignoring them.
Push for accelerated septic-to-sewer conversion funding.
The renewed sales tax would allocate $233 million over the next decade to sewage treatment and septic conversion.
I'll fight for state infrastructure dollars to speed up that timeline.
Septic systems are the single largest controllable source of nutrient pollution in the lagoon.
Support strengthening the Indian River Lagoon National Estuary Program at the state level and ensure Florida's congressional delegation is pushing for federal restoration dollars the way the Everglades gets them.
The lagoon deserves the same level of attention.
05 Education Our schools are struggling by nearly every measure, and the policy decisions that got us here were not driven by data.
They were driven by ideology.
At the state level, the voucher funding hole is bleeding public schools dry, and we need to plug it.
Every dollar diverted from public education without accountability is a dollar taken from the classrooms that serve the vast majority of Florida's children.
At the local level, I strongly support the work of the Brevard Schools Foundation and programs like Taking Stock in Children, which pair students with mentors and real support grounded in their own communities.
Every child deserves guidance and opportunity, especially the ones the system is most likely to overlook.
We also need a dramatically expanded use of juvenile civil citations in place of arrests.
The data is clear: keeping young people out of the correctional system lowers repeat offenses, improves long-term outcomes, and saves taxpayer money.
Arresting a teenager for a nonviolent offense does not make the community safer.
It makes the problem worse.
What I'll Do Fight to raise Florida's average teacher salary out of dead last.
Florida ranks 50th out of 50 states at $56,663 average teacher pay.
The state added $200 million for teacher pay in the 2026-27 budget, but that doesn't close a gap this large.
I'll push for dedicated recurring funding, not one-time bonuses, that gets us to at least the national average.
Demand real accountability for the $3.9 billion voucher program.
Taxpayers are funding about 30,000 students the state can't even accurately track.
That's $270 million with no accountability.
Voucher schools should meet the same testing, reporting, and financial transparency standards as public schools.
If you're spending public money, you answer to the public.
Plug the voucher funding hole that's draining public schools.
Vouchers now consume 23% of Florida's public education spending, up from 12% in 2021-22.
I'll co-sponsor legislation requiring that voucher expansion be budget-neutral to public school per-pupil funding.
No more robbing Peter to pay Paul.
Expand juvenile civil citation programs in Brevard County by working with the State Attorney's office, school districts, and local law enforcement.
The data is unambiguous: civil citations reduce recidivism by 70% compared to arrest, and every dollar spent on citations saves thousands in juvenile detention costs.
Fully fund the Brevard Schools Foundation and programs like Taking Stock in Children at the state level.
Mentorship works.
These programs pair at-risk students with community mentors and have a near-perfect high school graduation rate among participants.
06 Cost of Living and Insurance Reform Lowering the cost of living is the single most important thing I can do for the people in this district.
I will fight for, sponsor, and vote for any policy that puts money back in your pocket.
The biggest lever available in Tallahassee right now is insurance reform.
The policy vehicles already exist.
Homeowners insurance premiums in Florida could fall as much as 60 percent with the right reforms, and that reduction feeds directly into cheaper mortgages, lower rent, and more affordable condo fees.
We can restore price stability for millions of Floridians who own homes, drive cars, and are watching their costs climb every year.
When the private market fails to provide affordable coverage, the government has a responsibility to step in.
That is not ideology.
That is the job.
What I'll Do Co-sponsor SB 30-style rate cap legislation limiting annual homeowners insurance rate increases to 10-15%.
Rate hikes should be gradual and predictable, not a surprise bill that forces people out of their homes.
Support SB 234-style insurer affiliate transparency to expose the shell game where insurance company affiliates collect undisclosed profits while the parent company claims losses to justify premium hikes.
Floridians deserve to know where their premium dollars actually go.
Push for HB 767-style plain language disclosure requiring insurers to explain in plain English the specific factors driving any rate increase.
You shouldn't need a law degree to understand why your insurance went up.
Fight for roof inspection reimbursement so homeowners who get a qualified inspection showing their roof has five or more years of life don't get penalized with inflated premiums or forced into unnecessary replacements.
Insurance companies should not be in the roofing business.
Oppose the "do nothing" approach in the Senate.
Legislative leaders say they expect no major insurance changes because the reforms are "working." The average Brevard homeowner paying $4,000 or more a year for coverage would disagree.
The reforms passed in 2022-23 were a start, not a finish.
Support expanding Citizens Property Insurance as a public option backstop when private carriers refuse to write affordable policies.
When the market fails, the government steps in.
That's the job.
Target flood insurance costs by advocating for FEMA risk-rating reform at the state level and pushing for local flood mitigation projects that reduce premiums community-wide.
07 Environmental Data Transparency This one is personal.
I believe that data paid for by the taxpayer should be available to the taxpayer.
Period.
Florida has been funding environmental and pollution data collection for decades, but that data sits in agency silos where it makes little impact because it is not communicated well or understood easily.
We need a free, centralized, open-source dashboard that takes our existing environmental data and makes it visible, searchable, and understandable to every resident in the state.
Who is polluting, where, how much, and what is being done about it.
No single university or nonprofit can take on a project at this scale.
It has to come from the state.
It is time to stop collecting data nobody sees and start building tools that let the public hold polluters accountable.
We owe the next generation a state they can actually live in.
What I'll Do Introduce the Florida Environmental Transparency Act directing DEP to build and maintain a free, public, open-source environmental data dashboard.
Consolidate existing data from DEP, water management districts, county programs like SOIRL, and EPA records into one searchable platform.
Require real-time reporting for permitted polluters.
Facilities with DEP discharge permits should report emissions and discharge data electronically on a monthly basis, not buried in annual compliance filings that nobody reads.
Fund it through existing DEP technology budget lines.
This is not a new spending priority.
It is a reorganization of data the state already collects and already pays to store.
The marginal cost of making it public is minimal compared to the value.
Model the dashboard on the SOIRL approach.
The Save Our Indian River Lagoon program already publishes its project data, financials, and outcomes with exceptional transparency.
Extend that model statewide.
Partner with Florida's public universities for dashboard development and maintenance.
Graduate students in data science and environmental engineering get real-world experience; taxpayers get a world-class tool at a fraction of the cost of a state contractor.

Where He Started Daniel Cicirelli was born at Wuesthoff Hospital in Rockledge on June 1st, 1997, and has lived in Brevard County ever since.
He was homeschooled from first grade through graduation, finished with a 4.4 GPA, and earned his Associate's degree Summa Cum Laude from Eastern Florida State College with a focus in history and education.
He was on track to become a teacher, but made a decision that thousands of Floridians make every year: he chose a career that could actually support a family, because a teaching salary in this state cannot.
That tradeoff still sits with him, and it is one of the reasons he is running.
What He Built Daniel spent most of his professional career in critical infrastructure.
He started in construction as a framer before being hired by Johnson Controls as a design consultant for life safety systems, working on fire alarm and emergency communication infrastructure in hospitals, the Orange County Convention Center, and Kennedy Space Center.
When COVID hit, he was the youngest person in his office, which made him the one who went into the hospitals.
He spent the pandemic performing emergency repairs and system maintenance in medical facilities from Daytona to West Palm to Orlando, testing positive for COVID seven times along the way.
He later worked as an electrical estimator for Gulf Coast Power and Light before being recruited back to Johnson Controls at a significantly higher salary.
Through all of it, he developed a firsthand understanding of how the infrastructure people depend on actually gets built, maintained, and too often neglected.
Starting Over In 2023, Daniel's marriage ended.
In 2024, his divorce was finalized and he lost his house.
He started a handyman business that fall and began rebuilding in the same community where he grew up.
Around the same time, he started volunteering a day each week doing free handyman work for elderly residents in Cocoa who needed help and couldn't afford it.
Fixing screen doors, patching leaks, handling the small stuff that becomes serious when there's nobody to call.
Showing Up That volunteer work expanded quickly.
Daniel joined the Brevard Democrats in December 2024, the Young Democrats of Brevard in January, and by spring of 2025 he had founded the Space Coast Self Defense Club and Space Coast Protest Medics , become a precinct organizer for the Brevard DEC, joined the Environmental Caucus and the Progressive Democratic Caucus, and canvassed for the Vance campaign during the special election.
He wasn't running for anything.
He was just involved, consistently and without fanfare, in the daily work of trying to make his community function better.
Why He's Running On February 1st, 2026, Daniel gave a speech about ICE enforcement in Brevard County.
The next day, a stranger approached him and told him he should run for office.
A month later, he filed.
On March 18th, his paperwork was processed by the State of Florida, and here we are.
Daniel's family has been in Brevard for over four decades.
His father David moved from Miami in 1981 to work as an electrician at Kennedy Space Center.
His mother Joan was born and raised in Merritt Island.
They recently retired to a small sheep ranch in Gilchrist County.
His sister Gena lives in Cocoa.
Daniel has no plans to be the Cicirelli who leaves.
He is running for Florida House District 31 on affordable housing, healthcare access, clean water, functioning infrastructure, and a government that is more interested in solving problems than performing outrage.
He believes good governance should be quiet, competent, and relentlessly focused on the people who are already here.
This is his first campaign, and he's in it because someone told him he should be, and he couldn't find a reason they were wrong.
