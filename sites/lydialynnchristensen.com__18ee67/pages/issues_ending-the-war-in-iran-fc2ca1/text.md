Congress never voted to authorize it.
Since then, Congress has tried ten times to force a vote reining in this war, while the administration has argued the war powers deadline doesn't apply because of an April ceasefire, even as Trump said he was an hour away from ordering another strike weeks later.
On June 23, the House and Senate did something that had never happened before: both chambers passed a resolution directing a president to remove forces from an active conflict.
But it was a concurrent resolution, which by law never carries the force of it and never goes to the president's desk.
The next day, the Senate took up a different measure, Senator Kaine's joint resolution, the one that would have actually gone to Trump's desk and carried real legal force if signed.
Hours after Trump personally confronted two Republican senators at a closed-door lunch, both flipped their votes, and that measure was blocked.
That is exactly the problem with relying on a toothless mechanism.
Congress passed the version that couldn't bind the president, and killed the version that could, in the same week, after one lunch.
The Constitution gives the power to declare war to Congress, not the President, and a resolution designed from the start to carry no legal force isn't a check on that power.
