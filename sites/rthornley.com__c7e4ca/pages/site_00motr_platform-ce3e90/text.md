Campaign Reform Money & Elections A Washington Campaign Reform Proposal The Problem How much money should it take for a candidate to tell voters what he or she believes?
Washington elections have become increasingly dependent on fundraising, political advertising, political action committees, independent expenditures and other outside political spending.
The result is an election system in which candidates can spend hundreds of thousands—or even millions—of dollars competing for the attention of voters.
I believe elections should be primarily a competition of ideas, not a competition of fundraising ability.
Washington's Public Disclosure Commission gives us an opportunity to see just how much money is involved.

My Proposal 1.
Take Paid Advertising Out of Elections My long-term goal is to eliminate paid political advertising.
This would include: television advertising; radio advertising; political yard and roadside signs; paid internet and social-media advertising; robocalls; mass political text messages; and mass political email advertising.
Candidates would remain free to speak.
They could knock on doors, talk to voters, speak to organizations, participate in interviews, attend community events, participate in debates and candidate forums, receive news coverage and maintain their own campaign websites.
The objective would not be to silence candidates.
The objective would be to eliminate the financial advantage created by the ability to buy a larger political megaphone.
2.
Give Every Candidate an Equal Public Platform If paid political advertising is reduced or eliminated, Washington should provide voters with a much better neutral election-information system.
Every qualified candidate would receive equal access to: A standardized candidate webpage Biography and experience.
Positions on major issues.
Equal-length written statements.
Equal-duration candidate videos.
A link to the candidate's own website.
A schedule of debates and candidate forums.
Recordings of completed forums and debates.
Accessible and translated voter information.
No candidate would receive better placement simply because that candidate raised more money.
3.
A 50% Political Contribution Assessment I also propose examining a 50% assessment on political contributions .
The money generated would not simply disappear into the State General Fund.
It would be placed into a dedicated: Washington Voter Information Fund The fund could finance: candidate information; voter pamphlets; candidate video statements; public forums; debates; translation; accessibility; election-information websites; and other neutral ways of helping citizens make informed voting decisions.
Consider the potential.
Washington legislative candidates reported approximately: $32.69 million in contributions during the 2024 election cycle.

This example does not assume that such a tax is presently constitutional, nor does it include contributions to all PACs and other political committees.

I support that effort.
In the meantime, I would pursue every campaign-finance reform that Washington can constitutionally implement.

My principle is simple: The person with the most money should not automatically have the loudest political voice.
A person running for office should have to persuade voters with ideas, experience, judgment and character—not simply demonstrate an ability to raise enough money to blanket a district with advertising.

Under WAC 250-61-100, many bachelor’s degrees require 180 credits for graduation, but many degree programs require only 40 to 90 credits within the major itself.
Washington’s public colleges and universities should consider reducing the graduation requirement to 135 credits.
That would allow students to complete a degree in three years, with three quarters per year and 45 credits per quarter .
That would save students money and allow more students to move through each institution.
Today's students are different.
Washington has become a national leader in programs such as: Running Start, Advanced Placement (AP), International Baccalaureate (IB), College in the High School, Career and Technical Education dual-credit programs.
Many students now enter college with 20–60 college credits, and some graduate from high school with an associate degree.
The question is no longer whether students are academically prepared.
The question is whether the bachelor's degree itself has adapted to this new reality.
Rather than beginning with the assumption that every bachelor's degree must require 120 credits, Washington should ask: Why are 180 credits still the standard?
Which requirements are essential to producing competent graduates?
Which general education requirements duplicate learning already completed in high school?
Could many majors be completed with fewer total credits while maintaining quality and accreditation?

Water and Ecology A Washington Integrated Water System Planning Today for Tomorrow's Water Needs Washington has regions that experience severe flooding and others that face recurring drought.
Rather than treating these challenges as separate issues, we should explore whether they can be addressed through a comprehensive statewide water management strategy.
I support conducting a thorough feasibility study to determine whether Washington should develop an Integrated Water System to better manage one of our state's most valuable natural resources.
Rather than focusing on a single solution, the study should evaluate a variety of options, including: Water storage reservoirs and retention basins Intrastate water pipelines where practical Aquifer recharge and groundwater storage Water recycling and reuse Flood-control infrastructure Modern water monitoring and management technology Regional partnerships for water conservation and distribution The goal would be to capture excess water during periods of heavy rainfall and flooding and, when practical, move or store that water for use during droughts, agriculture, wildfire suppression, fish habitat restoration, municipal water supplies, and future population growth.
Let Science and Engineering Lead the Way This proposal is not a commitment to build a statewide water system.
It is a commitment to determine whether such a system is technically feasible, environmentally responsible, and financially practical.
Any study should include engineers, hydrologists, environmental scientists, tribal governments, agricultural representatives, utilities, cities, counties, business leaders, and other stakeholders.
The study should answer important questions such as: Can excess water be captured without harming rivers and ecosystems?
Where would additional storage provide the greatest benefit?
What are the environmental impacts?
What are the long-term operating costs?
How would water rights be protected?
Could the system reduce both flooding and drought impacts?
What benefits would it provide to agriculture, communities, fish habitat, and economic development?
A Legacy Infrastructure Investment If the study demonstrates that an Integrated Water System is practical and environmentally responsible, it could become one of the largest and most important infrastructure investments in Washington's history.
Like the interstate highway system transformed transportation, a statewide water system could strengthen Washington's long-term water security while creating thousands of high-quality, family-wage jobs over many years.
The project could improve: Flood resilience Agricultural productivity Drought preparedness Wildfire response Drinking water reliability Economic development Climate resilience Funding the Future If the project proves worthwhile, Washington should evaluate a variety of funding options, including federal infrastructure grants, public-private partnerships, state capital investments, and, where legally appropriate, climate-related funding sources such as revenues generated under the Climate Commitment Act.
Funding decisions should only be made after the project has demonstrated measurable public benefits and strong fiscal responsibility.
Accountability Comes First Large public investments require public trust.
Any feasibility study should follow the S.M.A.R.T. model by establishing goals that are: Specific Measurable Achievable Relevant Time-Bound The study should include: Clearly defined objectives A public project timeline Independent engineering and environmental review Regular public progress reports Transparent budgeting and expenditures Public hearings throughout the state A final report with recommendations before any construction decisions are made Washington taxpayers deserve complete transparency before committing billions of dollars to any major infrastructure project.
Looking Ahead Water is one of Washington's greatest natural resources.
As our population grows and weather patterns become more unpredictable, we should begin planning today for tomorrow's challenges.

Safe Harbor Law In 2021, the Washington Legislature passed the Safe Harbor Law (SB 1775) , sponsored by Senator Tina Orwall, to provide specialized services for juvenile victims of commercial sexual exploitation and trafficking.
The law envisioned creating two Safe Harbor Centers—one east of the Cascade Mountains and one west of the Cascades.
Unfortunately, neither center has opened.
A combination of stringent regulations, the inability to secure qualified operators, funding concerns, staffing shortages, and difficulties obtaining appropriate facilities prevented implementation.
The Legislature modified the law in 2024, but the program still lacks operators.
Several potential providers indicated that the regulatory requirements were too restrictive to make the program workable.
Washington should re-evaluate these requirements and work collaboratively with experienced service providers to develop a program that can realistically be implemented while maintaining appropriate protections for vulnerable youth.

Should Washington Consider a Different Model?
Washington should be willing to have an honest public discussion about whether a regulated legal framework could reduce neighborhood impacts and improve public safety.

No legal framework will eliminate human trafficking.

Such a significant policy change should not be imposed by the Legislature alone.
It should be considered only after extensive public discussion and, if appropriate, submitted to the voters through a statewide referendum.
