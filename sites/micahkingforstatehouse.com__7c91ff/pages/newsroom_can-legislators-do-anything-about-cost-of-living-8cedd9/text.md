Property Taxes: HB 581, Rollback Rates, and Assessment Mechanics
Property taxes are determined primarily by county commissions and local school boards, but the Georgia General Assembly writes the legal framework under which local taxes are levied.
Standard Homestead Exemptions and HB 581
Under O.C.G.A. § 48-5-44, Georgia provides a base homestead exemption of $2,000 off the assessed value (40 percent of fair market value) for primary residences.
However, rapid real estate inflation in recent years rendered that base exemption insufficient to protect homeowners from spiking tax bills.
To address this, the legislature passed House Bill 581, which was approved by Georgia voters in November 2024 and took effect on January 1, 2025.
HB 581 created a statewide floating homestead exemption that caps annual increases in a home’s taxable assessed value to the rate of CPI inflation, using 2024 as the base baseline year.
Under HB 581, even if local property values spike by 15 percent in a year, the homesteaded assessment only rises by the state inflation rate.
Local governments were given a window until March 1, 2025 to opt out via a resolution requiring three advertised public hearings.
Counties that stayed in the system can also utilize the Flexible Local Option Sales Tax (FLOST) to use local sales tax proceeds specifically to roll back property taxes for all parcels.
Rollback Millage Rates and Procedural Transparency
State law under O.C.G.A. § 48-5-32 and § 48-5-32.1 protects taxpayers through mandatory rollback millage rate calculations.
When market reassessments increase the total county tax digest, the county must lower (rollback) its millage rate so it collects the exact same total tax revenue as the prior year.
If a county commission or school board votes to keep the millage rate higher than the calculated rollback rate, state law classifies that action as a tax increase.
The levying authority must:
- Publish a formal “Notice of Property Tax Increase” prominently in the newspaper and online (explicitly barred from being hidden in the legal ads section).
- Hold three mandatory public hearings, with at least one hearing held on a weekday evening between 6:00 PM and 7:00 PM.
Despite these protections, rural counties face a structural dilemma.
Effective property tax rates in District 157 (ranging from 0.94 percent in Appling to 1.02 percent in Evans) sit higher than the statewide median of 0.77 percent because local property values are lower.
When state operations place demands on local infrastructure without proper reimbursement, county budgets get stretched thin.
For a deeper look at this dynamic, read my analysis on who is paying for Smith State Prison emergency responses.
What legislators can do
- Expand state reimbursements to rural counties hosting non-taxable state facilities (like state prisons) so local property owners are not forced to subsidize state operations.
- Protect and refine HB 581 floating homestead exemption rules so long-term homeowners are never priced out of their homes by assessment spikes.
- Ensure county boards of assessors maintain full statutory transparency during reassessment cycles.
Groceries: The Regressive Local Sales Tax Trap
One of the most frequent complaints from constituents is the cost of food.
Many voters are surprised to learn how tax policy directly impacts their register total.
The State Exemption vs.
The Local Stack
Under O.C.G.A. § 48-8-3, the State of Georgia exempts unprepared groceries from the state’s 4 percent base sales tax.
That is a vital consumer protection, but it only applies to the state portion.
Local option sales taxes (LOST, SPLOST, E-SPLOST, and TSPLOST) still apply to groceries.
Across Tattnall, Evans, Appling, and Jeff Davis counties, local sales taxes stack up to an additional 4 percent.
That means while you pay zero state sales tax on unprepared food, you still pay a 4 percent local sales tax at the grocery register, bringing the overall retail tax rate on non-grocery items to 8 percent.
For a family in District 157 spending $800 a month on groceries, that 4 percent local tax adds $384 a year in direct food taxes.
The GATE Exemption Balance
At the same time, Georgia supports our agricultural sector through the Georgia Agricultural Tax Exemption (GATE) program under O.C.G.A. § 48-8-3.3.
GATE allows qualified farmers to purchase equipment, seed, fertilizer, and fuel exempt from both state and local sales taxes.
This exemption saves Georgia agriculture an estimated $300 million annually and is vital for keeping our local farms competitive.
I strongly support supporting local farmers.
However, because massive agricultural inputs are exempt from local sales tax, rural county tax digests are narrowed, forcing local governments to rely more heavily on residential property taxes and retail grocery sales taxes to fund county services.
What legislators can do
- Work toward statutory options that allow rural counties to exempt basic staple groceries from local sales tax stacks without destroying local municipal budgets.
- Provide targeted state grant offsets for low-income rural counties that choose to eliminate local grocery taxes.
Utility Bills: PSC Oversight, Plant Vogtle, and Rural EMCs
Electricity is a non-discretionary monthly cost.
In rural Southeast Georgia, most residents are served by electric membership corporations like Canoochee EMC, Satilla REMC, and Altamaha EMC.
While EMCs are member-owned co-ops, their rates are heavily driven by decisions made at the state level by the Georgia Public Service Commission (PSC), which regulates wholesale power suppliers and Georgia Power.
Recent Regulatory Dockets Impacting Bills
- Fuel Cost Recovery (PSC Docket 44902): In 2023, the PSC approved Georgia Power’s recovery of $2.1 billion in unrecovered fuel costs caused by natural gas price spikes.
This added an average of $15.94 per month to typical residential power bills and raised the interim fuel adjustment cap from 15 percent to 40 percent.
- Plant Vogtle Units 3 & 4 (PSC Docket 29849): The prudency review settlement capped recoverable construction costs at $7.56 billion.
Oglethorpe Power Corporation (which supplies wholesale power to our local EMCs) reached a $413 million settlement over cost overruns while maintaining its 30 percent ownership stake.
- Wholesale Power Cost Adjustments (WPCA): Because of elevated wholesale generation and transmission costs, local co-ops have had to adjust member rates.
For example, Satilla REMC implemented a $0.02 per kWh WPCA.
For a rural home using 1,500 kWh in a summer month, that single line item adds $30 directly to the monthly bill.
- Data Center Grid Expansion (PSC Docket 45050): Georgia is experiencing a massive wave of industrial data center developments requiring thousands of megawatts of new power.
When the legislature passed HB 1192 to temporarily pause sales tax breaks on data center equipment while studying grid impacts, Governor Kemp vetoed the bill.
To protect families, the PSC has instituted large-load tariffs requiring data centers to pay for their own infrastructure.
You can explore our interactive Georgia data center map directory to see where these projects are located.
What legislators can do
- Enforce strict statutory safeguards through the PSC ensuring industrial tech developers pay 100 percent of their dedicated grid expansion costs so rural EMC members never subsidize Big Tech power usage.
- Expand state energy-efficiency weatherization grants specifically targeted for mobile homes and older housing stock in rural districts.
Price Controls vs.
Free Market Reality
When inflation rises, constituents often ask why the state representative cannot simply order grocery stores or gas stations to lower their prices.
Here is the legal reality under Georgia law:
The Price Gouging Statute
Georgia’s primary price control law is O.C.G.A. § 10-1-393.4.
However, this law only applies when the Governor declares a formal State of Emergency (such as after a hurricane).
During an emergency, retailers cannot raise prices above pre-emergency levels, except to cover verified increases in supply and transportation costs.
Outside of an officially declared emergency, Georgia operates as a free-market economy.
A state representative has no statutory authority to set grocery prices, cap gas margins, or dictate retail store pricing.
Anyone who promises you otherwise in an election year is selling you a fantasy.
Real legislative relief comes from tax policy, utility regulation, and lowering administrative burdens on small businesses, not from unconstitutional price fixing.
Healthcare and Housing Affordability
Beyond taxes and utilities, two other major drivers of living costs are healthcare and housing:
- Healthcare Costs: Certificate of Need (CON) laws restrict where new medical facilities can open.
While CON laws were intended to protect rural hospitals, they can also limit specialized services.
My rural healthcare policy plan focuses on keeping rural emergency rooms open, expanding telehealth infrastructure, and recruiting rural physicians.
- Housing Supply: While municipal zoning is local, state lawmakers can support modular housing standards, streamline state building permits, and fund trade skills training at local technical colleges to reduce construction labor shortages.
