All Posts Flock Cameras Data Centers Housing Fixed Income Search I Homeschool My Children | That Doesn't Mean I Don't Support Public Schools Rachael Dowling #ago 8 min read Recently, someone asked me a question about education.
They noticed that I'm involved in homeschool leadership and wanted to know how I would support our public schools if elected to the Wisconsin State Assembly.
And I think that's a fair question!
But I also think there's an assumption worth talking about.
Just because a family chooses homeschooling doesn't mean they're against public schools.
In fact, my own education involved both.
My Homeschool Story Started in 6th Grade I attended public school through 5th grade.
At the time, our school district had placed me in an experimental math program.
Then, in fifth grade, the district eliminated grammar instruction.
Those experiences were significant factors in my parents' decision to pull me out of public school and begin homeschooling in sixth grade.
And can I tell you something?
My world was shaken.
Imagine being a kid who had always gone to school, seen the same friends, followed the same routines, and suddenly everything changes.
I went from attending school every day to learning at home.
It was a huge adjustment, especially at an age when your friends and social connections are such an important part of your world.
But my parents understood something important.
Just because they had decided to take responsibility for my education at home didn't mean I needed to lose my connections to the school community.
So they enrolled me in public school music classes, where I played the violin.
Later, in high school, I also participated in music and art classes, including photography and drawing.
I was homeschooled, but I still had opportunities to learn from public school teachers, participate in classes, and spend time with friends.
And those experiences mattered.
Photography, in particular, eventually became part of my professional life.
I've been working in photography and video since 2007, and it's something I still love doing today as the owner of Drone Lady Productions.
Looking back, I appreciate that my parents didn't approach education as an all-or-nothing decision.
They recognized that different educational opportunities could complement each other, even if they weren't all happening in the same classroom.
They looked at what I needed and tried to make it work.
Then I Became the Parent Making Those Decisions Years later, when Joshua and I started raising our four boys, we made the decision to homeschool them, too.
But our reasons were different from my parents' reasons.
For us, one of the biggest factors was our family schedule.
We were raising four boys, running businesses, juggling work responsibilities, and trying to find a rhythm that worked for our family.
Homeschooling gave us the flexibility to build our school days around our lives rather than trying to fit everything into a traditional school schedule.
We also chose a curriculum that allowed our boys to study the same subjects at different levels.
With four children at different ages and stages of learning, that made a lot of sense for us.
One child might need more time to understand a concept, while another might be ready to move ahead.
And we could adjust.
Homeschooling has also given us the freedom to prioritize our children's interests, whether that's sports, hands-on projects, or something entirely different.
If one of our boys becomes particularly interested in something, we have the flexibility to spend more time exploring it.
Can public school parents do those things, too?
Absolutely!
It just might take a little extra juggling.
And believe me, I know plenty of public school parents who are really good at juggling.
The point is that we all want our children to have opportunities to learn, grow, discover their strengths, and prepare for adulthood.
We might organize our schedules differently or choose different educational environments, but that doesn't mean our goals are all that different.
We just might take different paths to get there.
Our Family Is Still Part of the Public School Community Here's something people might not realize: Our boys play football with Menasha, and I volunteer with the team.
We know public school students, families, coaches, and teachers.
We attend games, support the team, and appreciate the opportunities our local school community provides.
Choosing to homeschool doesn't mean we've separated ourselves from the people around us.
And it certainly doesn't mean I want our public schools to struggle.
Quite the opposite.
I want the children attending Menasha, Neenah, Appleton, and every other public school in Wisconsin to have the opportunity to receive an excellent education.
I want their teachers to have the support they need, and I want families to feel confident that their children are learning the skills they'll need for whatever comes next.
Five Years Helping Shape Educational Programs at NWTC Something else people might not know about me is that from 2019 to 2023, I served on an academic advisory committee at Northeast Wisconsin Technical College (NWTC).
The committee brought together local business owners who were invited to share their professional experience and help shape academic programs.
The goal was to help ensure students were learning skills that would prepare them for the workforce.
It was an opportunity to look at education from a different perspective.
What are students learning?
What skills do employers need?
And are the programs we're offering helping students successfully complete their education and prepare for their futures?
One of the changes I was involved in discussing was moving from traditional 16-week semesters toward shorter, 8-week sessions.
Why?
Because NWTC was looking at ways to help more students actually FINISH their education.
Think about it.
When you're struggling through a 16-week course, the finish line can feel pretty far away.
But when you can complete that course in eight weeks, suddenly the goal feels much more attainable.
And here's where the story gets interesting...
NWTC began transitioning toward its 8-Week Advantage model, with roughly half its classes using the shorter format by 2020 and #% by 2021.
In August 2025, Wisconsin Watch published an article examining what happened after the college made those changes.
And the results were encouraging.
According to the reporting, NWTC's full-time student retention rate increased by 19 percentage points since 2018, reaching 77%.
That means more students were staying enrolled or completing their programs from one year to the next.
The college's three-year graduation rate also increased, reaching 46%, compared with a national average of 35%.
Part-time student retention improved as well, though by a smaller amount.
Now, I want to be clear.
Those results belong to the students, instructors, administrators, and everyone involved in making those changes successful.
And we can't assume that changing the course schedule was the only reason student outcomes improved.
But it's an example of what can happen when educators are willing to examine a problem and try a different approach.
We didn't lower the finish line.
We made it easier for students to see it.
And here's another detail I appreciate.
NWTC continued evaluating the program and even returned some courses to longer formats when eight weeks wasn't the best fit.
Because the goal shouldn't be forcing every student or every subject into the same model.
The goal should be finding an approach that actually helps students learn.
That experience reinforced something I believe about education: When students are struggling, we should absolutely ask what support they need.
But we should also be willing to ask whether the system itself could work better.
Sometimes helping students succeed means being willing to do things differently.
You can read more about NWTC's results in the Wisconsin Watch article: https://wisconsinwatch.org/2025/08/northeast-wisconsin-technical-college-nwtc-class-course-retention-graduation-education/ So What Would I Actually Support in Madison?
First, I think we need to get back to the fundamentals.
Reading.
Writing.
Math.
Critical thinking.
These are the building blocks students need for just about everything that comes next.
I've been especially concerned about reading proficiency, because if a child is struggling to read, that challenge doesn't stay confined to English class.
It affects science, history, math word problems, and eventually their ability to navigate everyday life.
We need to make sure our literacy efforts are actually helping children learn to read, and that teachers have the tools, training, and support to make that happen.
I want us to measure success by how well students are learning, not simply by how much money we're spending.
But that doesn't mean funding doesn't matter.
It absolutely does.
I believe our public schools need reliable support for essential educational services, including classroom instruction, materials, and students who need specialized assistance.
I also have concerns about how often school districts find themselves turning to referendums to cover ongoing needs.
We should be asking whether Wisconsin's school funding system is working the way it should, and whether the money we're already spending is reaching the places where it makes the greatest difference.
And we need to listen to teachers.
They're the ones in the classrooms every day.
They see the students who are thriving, the students who are struggling, and the challenges that don't always show up neatly in a budget spreadsheet.
I also believe we need to be willing to evaluate programs and policies based on their results.
If something isn't working, we should be willing to ask why.
And if there's a better way to help students learn, we shouldn't be afraid to explore it.
Supporting our schools should mean supporting student success, not defending a system simply because it's the way we've always done things.
I Believe in Educational Choice AND Strong Public Schools These two ideas are not mutually exclusive.
I believe parents should have meaningful choices about how their children are educated.
Some families choose their neighborhood public school.
Others choose private school, homeschooling, or another educational option.
And sometimes those decisions change as a child's needs change.
I don't believe one educational model is automatically the right answer for every child.
My own experience growing up is a perfect example of that.
My parents chose homeschooling because they believed it was the best decision for me at the time.
But they also recognized the value of public school classes and teachers, so they made sure I could continue participating in those opportunities.
And years later, Joshua and I made a different decision for different reasons.
Different families.
Different circumstances.
Different needs.
But I also don't believe supporting school choice should mean ignoring the needs of our public schools.
Most Wisconsin children attend public schools.
Their education matters.
Their teachers matter.
And their futures matter.
When we discuss policies involving school choice and taxpayer funding, I believe we should also be asking questions about accountability, eligibility, and whether those policies are accomplishing what they were intended to do.
Those are reasonable questions to ask whenever public money is involved.
We Have More in Common Than We Think One thing I've learned from being involved in the homeschool community is that parents care deeply about their children's education.
But you know what?
So do public school parents.
So do private school parents.
And so do the teachers who spend their days helping children learn.
We might disagree about curriculum, school funding, educational policy, or the best environment for a particular child.
But I don't think those disagreements have to turn us into opposing teams.
We can support parental choice while recognizing the important role public schools play in our communities.
We can ask difficult questions about spending while also recognizing that teachers need resources.
And we can hold schools accountable for student outcomes while acknowledging that students don't all learn at the same pace or face the same challenges.
My own family has benefited from different educational opportunities over the years.
From my early years in public school, to homeschooling, to the violin and photography classes I attended, to my very first college chemistry class at age 16 at the College of Menominee Nation.
Each experience played a part in who I became.
And years later, I had the opportunity to serve on an academic advisory committee during a period when NWTC was rethinking how its courses were structured.
I don't look at those experiences as competing educational philosophies.
I look at them as different opportunities that helped people learn and succeed.
My decision about how to educate my own children doesn't change how much I care about your family's educational choices or how we support our public school teachers.
And if I'm elected to represent District 53, that's the perspective I'll bring to Madison.
Not every family needs to make the same choices mine did.
But every child deserves the opportunity to learn, grow, and build a future.
