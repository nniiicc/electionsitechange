Posted : Jun 13, 2026 in : Bill Review , House Bills , Opinion by : JJK Tags : Financial Policy , HB1300 , Tax Caps A Response That Raises a Larger Question The legislature recently passed a bill establishing a statewide ballot question for 2026 that would allow voters to cap property taxes tied to school funding and limit certain administrative expenses.
At first glance, that sounds like a reasonable response.
Property taxes have been rising, and people feel it every year.
But the real question is whether this kind of proposal actually addresses the cause of those costs—or just limits how they show up on the surface.
From Targeted Policy to Broader Systems We’ve already seen how this plays out.
When Education Freedom Accounts were first introduced, they were presented as targeted assistance for families with demonstrated financial need.
Over time, that focus shifted.
Eligibility expanded, and the program grew beyond its original scope.
That doesn’t happen all at once.
It happens gradually.
A policy starts with a specific purpose, expands over time, and eventually becomes something much broader than intended.
That matters because each step introduces new long-term obligations, even if they’re not immediately obvious.
This tax cap proposal should be viewed in that same context.
Managing Symptoms Instead of Structure Requiring towns to vote on property tax caps focuses on controlling outcomes.
What it doesn’t do is address why those costs are increasing in the first place.
Property taxes in New Hampshire are closely tied to how education is funded statewide.
When funding structures shift, or when new obligations are introduced without clear long-term planning, that pressure doesn’t disappear—it moves.
And more often than not, it moves to the local level.
Setting a cap doesn’t fix that.
It just limits how local systems can respond to it.
What Happens When Costs Keep Rising “That does not eliminate regulation of public schools, but it does prevent budgeting for changes to special education, health insurance premiums, teacher pay and reductions to state spending,” said Rep.
David Luneau of Hopkinton in comments to NHPR.
That’s an important point.
The issue isn’t that services are immediately cut.
It’s that flexibility is reduced.
Costs like special education requirements, healthcare, and staffing don’t stay flat year to year.
They grow.
And when budgets are restricted while those costs continue to rise, something has to give.
In practice, that often leads to tradeoffs: fewer support resources delayed investments increased pressure on staff Many teachers already spend their own money on classroom supplies.
Tightening budgets further doesn’t remove that pressure—it increases it.
The Reality for Most Communities Not every family has the ability to choose between different school systems.
For many communities, public schools are the only option.
When financial flexibility is limited at the district level, those communities don’t have an alternative.
They absorb the impact directly.
And that’s where broader affordability concerns start to show up—not just in education, but in the overall cost of living in the state.
The Larger Issue There’s nothing wrong with trying to control costs.
The problem is how it’s being done.
Policies that focus on limiting spending without addressing the reasons those costs are increasing tend to shift pressure rather than resolve it.
We’ve seen this pattern before.
Programs expand, obligations grow, and then new policies are introduced to manage the financial strain that follows.
Meanwhile, residents are still dealing with the same reality: higher costs, less predictability, and fewer clear long-term solutions.
