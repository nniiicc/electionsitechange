Recently, the state released an article in the NH Journal talking about the specific reasons about why taxes are so high and how the state is providing all it can.
The article can be found here: https://nhjournal.com/birdsell-osborne-whats-driving-high-property-taxes-in-the-granite-state/
The state’s response relies heavily on education funding increases — state adequacy aid, statewide education property tax relief, and meals and rooms distributions.
These are often cited as proof that New Hampshire has fully funded its obligations to local governments.
But municipal operating budgets — police, fire, roads, water, sewer, and retirement — are not funded through education aid.
According to the New Hampshire Municipal Association, state aid to municipalities excluding education is volatile, heavily reliant on one‑time surplus distributions, and declines after 2023 once those temporary funds expire.
This matters because one‑time money cannot support ongoing costs.
Towns do not hire permanent staff or maintain critical infrastructure based on temporary surpluses.
When those funds disappear, municipalities are left with higher baseline costs and fewer stable revenue sources.
Apples to Oranges
Compounding the problem, both the NHMA data and the state’s own claims are presented in nominal dollars.
In a period of elevated inflation, flat or even modestly rising aid represents a real reduction in purchasing power.
Local governments face rising labor, health insurance, energy, and retirement costs regardless of spending discipline.
New Hampshire municipalities cannot run deficits, levy income or sales taxes, or rely on temporary aid for operations.
When predictable state support fails to keep pace with unavoidable costs, the property tax becomes the default balancing tool — not because of mismanagement, but because there is no alternative.
The New Hampshire Municipal Association’s data illustrates this pattern clearly.
State aid to municipalities declines in both 2024 and the 2025 budget estimate — even as costs continue to rise.
So where does the rest get picked up?
Your local tax levy.
But the claim that funding has increased year over year does not hold up once inflation is taken into account.
So when they claim “record funding to towns,” they are often referring to:
- School aid
- Property tax relief mechanisms
- Temporary surplus distributions
However, none of these stabilize core municipal operating budgets.
“Outpacing inflation” depends on what years and what funding
Their next claim that state support grew by 74% and outpaced inflation by 29 points relies on:
- A short time window
- A period with unprecedented pandemic surplus spending
- Inclusion of one‑time distributions
However, the states own explanation for this is that “…one-time revenues or surplus accounts explain the $30 million increase in 2018 highway funding, $20 million in state municipal aid grants for state fiscal years 2020 and 2021, and $151 million in state fiscal years 2023 and 2024 for infrastructure and environmental projects, and contributions to the New Hampshire Retirement System.”
This matters because one‑time funds cannot support ongoing staff, maintenance, or benefits.
Municipalities cannot permanently lower property taxes with temporary money.
When the spike disappears (as in 2024–2025), towns are still left with higher baseline costs.
Nominal dollars ≠ real purchasing power
While the article does a great job at trying to point fingers and frame the situation very specifically, it does not adjust its spending growth comparisons for:
- Post‑2021 inflation in labor, fuel, construction, and health insurance
- NH Retirement System contribution increases
- Mandated cost growth
The NHMA chart is also nominal, which means:
- Flat or slightly rising aid can represent a real cut
- “Record funding” can still leave towns worse off year‑to‑year
In high‑inflation periods, cost growth without commensurate recurring aid guarantees property tax increases, even with level service budgets.
This framing obscures these fiscal realities, by attributing rising taxes solely to local financial mismanagement.
Local spending rises because it must, not because it is unchecked
Next, the article implies discretionary local spending decisions are the main driver.
While this would be a convenient scapegoat, reality is much different.
Major local cost increases include:
- Contractual wage steps
- Health insurance
- Energy and materials
- State and federal mandates
- Aging infrastructure
- Retirement system obligations
Municipalities cannot:
- Run deficits
- Use income or sales taxes
- Rely on one‑time aid for operations
So when state aid is unstable or non‑recurring, the property tax becomes the default balancing tool.
Tax caps don’t fix downshifting — they hide it
HB 1300 addresses how budgets are approved, not why costs are rising.
Tax caps:
- Do not reduce mandate costs
- Do not stabilize state aid
- Do not replace lost purchasing power
- Push risk downward when costs exceed caps
Historically, tax caps:
- Defer maintenance
- Increase fees
- Create fiscal cliffs
- Shift costs again — just less visibly
Rising property taxes in New Hampshire are not the result of runaway local spending, but of a structural mismatch between unavoidable local costs and unstable, non‑recurring state support.
