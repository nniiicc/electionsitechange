Given that the legislative session is ending this week, it’s worth looking back at some of the major bills passed over the last session and what their long-term impact may be on New Hampshire residents.
Concord’s agenda has increasingly focused on funnelling taxpayer money toward private educational institutions rather than addressing the growing financial pressure facing residents.
SB295 expands eligibility for Education Freedom Accounts regardless of income level.
The NHGOP argues this supports parental choice.
The larger concern is whether it also increases long-term financial pressure elsewhere in the system.
About whether expanding parallel funding systems without predictable long-term cost controls creates additional pressure on New Hampshire’s education and property tax structure.
The fiscal note attached to SB295 makes clear that the proposed expansion is expected to include households that were not originally the focus of the EFA system, while also creating potentially indeterminable long-term state obligations.
Originally, EFAs were presented as targeted assistance.
Now, they are becoming something much broader.
SB295 Expands an Already Expensive Program As of September 2024, 5,321 students were enrolled in the EFA program, with an estimated annualized cost of $27.7 million.
This is not a small pilot program anymore.
It is an expanding state obligation funded through taxpayer dollars.
The fiscal note itself acknowledges the likely makeup of the newly eligible population: “the newly eligible population would not be eligible for free or reduced-priced meal differentiated aid” In plain terms, the state expects that many of the newly eligible households would not qualify under traditional low-income assistance standards.
That raises an important question about the direction of the program.
SB295 expands the EFA system beyond demonstrated financial need while still requiring the state to continue funding both public education obligations and an increasingly broad voucher structure.
SB295 Creates New State Spending Obligations The fiscal note also states: “Students accessing the program attending non-public schools or home education settings would likely receive an estimated average grant of $4,419.
This would be an increased cost to the state as these students currently do not receive any state support.” This is important because it means the state is not simply shifting funding alongside students already in the public system.
Instead, the bill creates entirely new funding obligations for students who were previously outside the public funding structure altogether.
Even more concerning is the fact that the Department of Education cannot project the future scale of the program.
The fiscal note states: “The Department of Education states this bill will result in an indeterminable number of new students accessing the program.” In other words, the state does not know how large the program may become, yet taxpayers remain obligated to fund it indefinitely.
That is not a criticism of educational alternatives themselves.
It is a question of sustainability, predictability, and long-term fiscal planning.
At a time when residents are already dealing with rising property taxes, increasing housing costs, and higher energy bills, New Hampshire needs to think carefully about how many parallel obligations the current system can realistically support.
