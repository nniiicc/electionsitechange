It’s springtime in New Hampshire.
The birds have finished building their nests, and the voles are in full hole-digging mode.
It is also the time of year when Granite Staters witness another rare but familiar event: some form of cannabis reform passing at least one legislative body, only to be stalled, tabled, or vetoed before becoming law.
This year, that included SB 468, a bipartisan medical cannabis bill that would have allowed alternative treatment centers to use greenhouse cultivation in an effort to lower energy costs and patient prices.
Gov.
Kelly Ayotte vetoed it, writing, “I do not support expanding the cultivation of marijuana in our state.”
Governors have their reasons, and I won’t get into all of those here.
What I want to examine is the testimony and talking points used to justify keeping cannabis illegal — and why those arguments are often the wrong frame for New Hampshire.
Some previous reasons given include:
- For every $1 in revenue, NH would spend roughly $4.50 to mitigate effects of legalized cannabis.
- Legalization increases youth use
- Legalization creates more crime and criminal justice costs
- Legalization will cause a DUI crisis
- Will lead to poison control calls and visits to ER to go up
- Legalization worsens mental health
- Public support is weakening
Let’s go through these claims one by one and ask whether they actually justify prohibition.
In many cases, they rely on outdated evidence, selective readings of data, or century-old drug-war assumptions.
That does not mean cannabis has no risks.
Like alcohol, tobacco, or any other adult-use substance, cannabis should be monitored, regulated, and controlled — though I’ll leave coffee out of this fight for now.
For every $1 in revenue, NH would spend roughly $4.50 to mitigate effects of legalized cannabis.
The talking point appears to come from a 2018 Centennial Institute report on Colorado, which claimed that for every dollar gained in marijuana tax revenue, Coloradans spent approximately $4.50 mitigating the effects of legalization.
The report’s own table lists about $1.13 billion in alleged 2017 costs against $247.4 million in marijuana tax revenue, producing the 4.5-to-1 ratio.
But two categories drive most of that number: K–12 dropouts, at about $423 million, and hospitalisations, at about $382 million.
Together, those two categories account for roughly $805 million — about 71% of the report’s total alleged costs.
The problem is that the report itself admits causation around education is uncertain.
It acknowledges that lower academic success among marijuana users may be explained by other factors, including preexisting motivation, academic barriers, or other characteristics.
In other words, the causal chain is not clean, yet the report still counts $423 million in dropout costs as legalization costs.
That one category alone accounts for roughly 40% of the report’s total alleged costs
Let’s do some math:
- Original ratio: $1.13B costs / $247M tax revenue = about 4.57-to-1
- Remove only the dropout cost: ratio falls to about 2.86-to-1
- Remove only hospitalizations: ratio falls to about 3.03-to-1
- Remove both dropouts and hospitalizations: ratio falls to about 1.32-to-1
Therefore, the claim of ‘for every dollar gained is $4.50 dollars spent’ is not a stable fiscal fact – it depends on a handful of highly contestable assumptions.
Legalization Increases Youth Use
The next claim, and from SAM-NH’s own HB186 testimony, is that legalization would increase youth drug use and that legalizing at age 21 ignores brain development until around age 25.
Let’s stay with Colorado as the example, considering they were one of the first in the US to legalize it.
According to a 2023 Healthy Kids Colorado Survey, it found that 13% of high school students reported marijuana use past 30 days – the lowest rate since legalization in 2013.
The use-past-30-days mark fell from 19.7% in 2013 to 12.8% in 2023.
This directly undercuts the claim that regulated adult-use sales automatically increases youth usage.
If legalization inevitably drives youth use, Colorado should be the cautionary tale.
Instead, Colorado teen cannabis use is lower NOW than before legal adult-use stores opened.
Legalization Creates More Crime and Criminal Justice Costs
SAM-NH argued legalization is a criminal justice burden.
I’m not saying this is entirely incorrect, but you cannot solve every problem when legalized.
An official report from Colorado’s Division of Criminal Justice stated law enforcement still combats illicit-market activity and remains difficult to quantify due to its hidden nature.
But, the policy question is not ‘does legalization solve every problem?’.
It’s a question of whether prohibition is better or not.
Prohibition guarantees criminal records for conduct that is legal across state lines, while regulation can reduce arrests, shift police resources, and generate tax revenue for prevention, treatment, and public safety.
Legalization does not create a marijuana-enforcement burden from scratch; it replaces prohibition enforcement with regulated-market enforcement, while dramatically reducing arrests and court cases for possession.
Legalization Will Cause a DUI Crisis
First to start out, I will NOT minimize cannabis-impaired driving, as it is a real driver of concern.
The report previously referenced mentions fatal accidents where drivers tested positive for cannabinoids.
But typical anti-legalization oftentimes blurs THC presence with impairment and causation.
NHTSA’s report to Congress says THC blood concentrations correlate poorly with impairment and that setting simple per-se THC limits is not scientifically meaningful in the same way blood alcohol concentration is.
Post-crash toxicology can show cannabinoids even when cannabis was not the cause of the crash, and frequent users may test positive after impairment has passed.
NHTSA has explicitly discussed the poor correlation between THC concentration and impairment, which weakens arguments that positive cannabinoid test — legalization-caused crash.
Cannabis-impaired driving is a legitimate safety issue, but prohibition is not the only policy response.
Legalization can fund education, testing research, DRE training, and impaired-driving enforcement – while prohibition leaves consumption underground and unregulated.
Will Lead to Poison Control Calls and Visits to ER to Go Up
There is some factual basis that legal markets can increase reported edible exposures, especially where products are poorly labeled, packaged, or stored.
Colorado’s official reporting observed increases in poison-control calls, emergency-room visits, and hospitalizations related to marijuana.
But this is an argument for regulation, not prohibition.
The American Public Health Association supports an evidence-based public-health approach to regulating commercial cannabis, including strategies similar to tobacco and alcohol control, consumer protection, youth access prevention, and health-equity considerations.
Poison-control and ER concerns are real, but they support child-resistant packaging, potency limits, labeling, public education, and regulated retail – not an unregulated black market.
Legalization Worsens Mental Health
Cannabis does carry mental-health risks, especially for adolescents, frequent users, daily users, high-potency THC consumers, and people already vulnerable to psychosis.
The CDC says people who use cannabis are more likely to develop psychosis or long-lasting mental disorders such as schizophrenia, and that the association is stronger for people who start younger and use more frequently.
A 2019 Lancet Psychiatry study, summarized by the University of Bath, found that daily use of high-THC cannabis was associated with a five-fold increased risk of psychosis compared with never-use.
But this is not a blank check for prohibitionist claims.
Bath’s own summary states that the study “cannot provide definitive evidence for a causal relationship between cannabis use and psychosis,” and a Lancet Psychiatry correspondence warned that genetic and environmental confounding may explain part of the association.
That matters.
Evidence of higher risk among daily high-potency users is not the same thing as proof that adult legalization causes a statewide mental-health crisis.
It is an argument for regulating potency, labeling, age checks, advertising, and youth prevention — not for criminalizing adults and leaving consumers in an illicit market.
Public Support is Weakening
SAM-NH’s claim of weakening public support is simply not true.
HB186 sponsor Rep.
Jared Sullivan stated 70% of Granite Staters support legalization, including 55% of Republicans.
The main barriers to legalized marijuana is the NH Senate and the Governors office, not the NH public.
When you break down the arguments against legalization, many of them do not hold up as arguments for prohibition.
Some identify real risks — impaired driving, youth access, accidental ingestion, high-potency products — but those are arguments for regulation, not for leaving cannabis in an unregulated market.
New Hampshire is already surrounded by legal states.
Granite Staters are already using cannabis.
The question is not whether cannabis exists here.
It does.
The question is whether New Hampshire wants that use controlled by an illicit market, or by a regulated system with age checks, testing, labeling, tax revenue, public-health funding, and accountability.
The public is not the main barrier.
The New Hampshire Senate and the governor’s office are.
And if they are going to keep blocking reform, they should have to do so with evidence that survives scrutiny — not recycled scare numbers from a 2018 Colorado report whose own assumptions begin to fall apart under review.
So, when we look at the specific arguments that have been levied in opposition of ending cannabis prohibition, they are either simply not true, skewed, or have been misstated.
Sticking our heads in the sand over this won’t make cannabis go away, it’ll only force New Hampshire residents to continue to lose money across the border and paying for it with their property taxes.
