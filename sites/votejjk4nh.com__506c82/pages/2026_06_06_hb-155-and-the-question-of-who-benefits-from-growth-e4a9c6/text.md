We regularly hear about strong revenues, low taxes, economic competitiveness, and efforts to reduce the tax burden on businesses further through legislation like HB 155.
I understand the logic behind that approach.
A healthy private sector matters.
Businesses create jobs, invest in communities, and help drive the state’s economy forward.
A state that is hostile to business ultimately hurts workers and residents as well.
But I also think we need to ask a broader question: If our economy is doing so well, why are so many residents still feeling financially squeezed?
That is not a rhetorical question.
It is something people are experiencing every day.
Property taxes continue to create instability for homeowners.
Energy costs remain unpredictable.
Housing affordability has become one of the defining economic problems facing New Hampshire.
Young professionals are struggling to establish themselves here, and many long-time residents are finding it harder to remain in the communities they have lived in for decades.
At some point, we have to evaluate economic policy not simply by whether revenues are strong or taxes are low somewhere in the system, but by whether people actually feel greater stability in their daily lives.
That is where I believe the conversation around business taxation needs to evolve.
I do not believe New Hampshire should become anti-business.
Quite the opposite.
I believe businesses are an essential part of solving many of these problems.
But I also do not believe that every discussion about economic growth should begin and end with reducing business taxes further while residents continue absorbing rising costs in other areas of life.
A balanced economy requires balance in policy.
If the state asks businesses to contribute more, then the state should also create clear and measurable ways for businesses to offset those costs through direct investment in New Hampshire workers and communities.
To me, that is a far more practical and sustainable approach than broad tax reductions disconnected from outcomes.
For example, I believe businesses that create jobs in New Hampshire should be rewarded for doing so.
If a company expands its workforce here, that creates local economic activity, strengthens communities, and helps attract long-term residents to the state.
The same is true for workforce training and education.
Businesses that invest in apprenticeships, technical certifications, partnerships with community colleges, or tuition and student-loan assistance programs are helping solve one of the biggest long-term challenges facing the state: retaining and attracting skilled workers.
That kind of investment should matter in our tax policy.
If we want younger workers and professionals to build a future here, we need to recognize the financial barriers many of them are carrying.
Student-loan debt is part of that reality.
Businesses willing to help employees with repayment assistance or education incentives are contributing to workforce stability in a meaningful way, and I believe there should be thoughtful incentives that encourage more employers to take that approach.
The goal should not simply be lower taxes or higher taxes in isolation.
The goal should be alignment.
We should be aligning business incentives with the broader needs of the state.
That means rewarding actions that: create jobs locally, strengthen the workforce, improve employee retention, support long-term residency, and contribute to sustainable economic growth inside New Hampshire itself.
At the same time, if businesses contribute more overall through a balanced tax structure, those revenues can help reduce the pressure currently falling on residents through property taxes, infrastructure costs, and other public obligations that continue increasing year after year.
I do not think those ideas are contradictory.
I think they are connected.
A successful business environment depends on more than tax rates alone.
Businesses also benefit from stable infrastructure, reliable schools, affordable communities, and a workforce that can actually afford to live in the state where they work.
That is why I believe we need a more balanced conversation moving forward.
Not one built around slogans or absolutes, but one focused on measurable outcomes and long-term stability.
New Hampshire absolutely should remain competitive and welcoming to businesses.
But residents should also be able to see tangible benefits from the state’s economic growth—not just hear about them in budget headlines.
If people are still struggling with affordability and unpredictability despite strong revenues and continued economic expansion, then we have more work to do.
My approach is simple: Businesses that invest in New Hampshire workers, communities, and long-term growth should be rewarded for doing so.
Residents should also see the benefit of a fairer and more balanced economic system.
