Suozzi for New York’s 3rd Congressional District October 10, 2026 Publication: Newsday By: The Editorial Board Rep.
Thomas R.
Suozzi (D-Glen Cove) is running for reelection.
Credit: James Escher The 3rd Congressional District has a unique backstory.
Democrat Thomas R.
Suozzi represented it for six years before giving up the seat to oppose Gov.
Kathy Hochul in the 2022 party primary.
That year an unknown George Santos surprisingly captured the seat for the GOP during a midterm backlash against President Joe Biden.
After Santos was expelled from the House in 2023, Suozzi returned in a special election to fill the seat and has held it since.
Now Suozzi, 64, of Glen Cove, seeks a new term against Republican challenger Michael J.
LiPetri Jr., 36, of Farmingdale, whom he defeated 52%-48% in 2024 even as President Donald Trump carried the district.
Voters face a stark choice between the seasoned Suozzi and LiPetri, an attorney who served one term in the Assembly before his multiple efforts to win a seat in Congress, which started with a failed primary challenge to GOP incumbent Andrew Garbarino in CD2.
LiPetri signals that he’d serve mainly as a loyal vote for Speaker Mike Johnson if the GOP majority holds.
“I think what Mike Johnson has achieved with the majority razor thin, is an accomplishment,” LiPetri told the Newsday editorial board.
With those words, LiPetri defends Trump’s most divisive initiatives to now — the Iran war, DOGE, mass deportations and ballooning debt — even if those policies involve sidestepping the role of Congress.
Suozzi, who has been in public life for more three decades, is careful and selective when he critiques the elected GOP leadership and says the only chance to end the current Capitol chaos would be for the Democrats to recapture the majority.
Suozzi, who co-chairs the bipartisan Problem Solvers Caucus, says he needs Democratic, independent and Republican votes to prevail in his suburban district.
He consistently shuns the left-wing agenda of New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and rejects anti-police and anti-Israel rhetoric.
Local issues are key.
Both candidates say they support a restoration of unlimited state and local tax deductions on federal income taxes — which Republicans capped at $10,000 in Trump’s first term, but then agreed to hike last year to $40,000 with an income cap.
Both oppose the current plan for the proposed Propel NY Energy underground transmission lines on the North Shore.
Both also support expanding childcare tax credits.
Suozzi and LiPetri tout differing affordability proposals.
One that stands out for the incumbent is the so-called WISH (Well-Being Insurance for Seniors to be at Home) Act to create a public-private partnership so those qualified can receive stipends for long-term care at home.
The costs, standards and income requirements as presented sound flexible enough to have a chance to pass in fiscally difficult times.
For his part, LiPetri says he wants an insurance system that, while covering all catastrophic occurrences, rewards prevention and healthy habits and moves away from employer-based insurance by making it “portable.” He’s vague on how this would work without penalizing those who already have coverage.
Suozzi says the Iran war, driving debt and economic uncertainty, must end sooner rather than later.
LiPetri agrees with Trump’s assertions that only he can ensure Iran never gets nuclear weapons and that higher gas prices are the necessary cost of that effort.
They differ on immigration.
Last year Fernando Mejia, 41, was taken into custody by U.S.
Immigration and Customs Enforcement as he arrived at the bagel store he managed for several years in Port Washington.
Mejia, who overstayed a visa many years ago, ultimately was deported, leaving a 15-year-old daughter behind.
It’s to deal with such difficult immigration cases that Suozzi has been working on a comprehensive reform plan that would allow those who have established a life here and have no criminal background to have a path to legal status.
But LiPetri, when asked if Mejia is the kind of undocumented immigrant who should be deported, stuck to the line that the mass roundups are “about upholding law and order.” Suozzi shows sensitivity to the public mood.
Back in January, he crossed party lines to vote for a $64 billion Department of Homeland Security funding bill including $10 billion for ICE.
Suozzi soon expressed regret — and admitted his vote was a mistake in light of the agency’s operations in Minnesota which included the fatal shooting of civilian Renee Good.
LiPetri says Social Security benefits should not be taxable but that only would further drain Social Security’s primary trust fund, which is approaching insolvency.
Suozzi would make all salaried income subject to the Social Security tax to keep benefits secure.
This American moment demands a break from go-along partisanship of the kind that only pampers Trump’s institutionally destructive goals and projects.
Given his experience, Suozzi is a valuable voice for the concerns of his “purple” district whether he will land in the majority or the minority.
The next Congress must move quickly to address the nation’s problems and Suozzi is primed to do so.
Newsday Opinion endorses Suozzi.
