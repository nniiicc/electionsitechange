top of page News Principles Calendar About Menu Close Donate Your Grazing Rights Are Being Erased — Not by Law, But by Omission By Shane Klakken, Montana House District 37 July 22, 2026 If you’ve ever had a map that left out your property line, you know the trouble that causes.
Now imagine that happening to millions of acres of grazing land across the West — not by accident, but through decades of administrative neglect.
That’s exactly what the Montana Natural Resource Coalition of Counties has been fighting to correct, and it matters directly to every ranching family in Fergus County, Petroleum County, and across central Montana.
Here’s the issue in plain terms.
The Taylor Grazing Act of 1934 created what are called TGA Grazing Districts — federally reserved lands set apart specifically for domestic livestock grazing.
These aren’t just lines on a map.
They carry real legal weight.
Under the TGA, the Secretary of Interior is required by law to adequately safeguard grazing privileges on these lands.
That’s not a suggestion.
That’s a statutory obligation.
But here’s the problem: BLM Resource Management Plans across Montana — the very documents that guide how federal land is managed — don’t include the maps or adequate descriptions of these TGA reserved districts.
They’ve been quietly left out.
Why does that matter?
Because when those districts don’t appear in planning documents, it becomes easier for environmental groups and federal administrators to treat grazing lands as if they’re available for repurposing — rewilding projects, biodiversity corridors, habitat conservation plans.
The legal foundation for grazing gets eroded, not through Congress changing the law, but through bureaucrats and activists simply pretending the maps don’t exist.
The MtNRC — which represents Fergus County and several other Montana counties — submitted formal comments to BLM Director Steve Pierce earlier this month making exactly this argument.
The coalition is pushing BLM to incorporate the official National TGA district dataset directly into federal regulations at 43 CFR 4100.
They’re also calling on BLM to build an interactive public web map on blm.gov showing full TGA district layers, allotment overlays, and classifications across all ten western states where these districts exist.
Montana is already ahead of the curve here.
Through the Montana Grass Conservation Commission and the State Library, Montana has digitally mapped its TGA grazing districts in a user-friendly viewer that layers state lands, BLM lands, Forest Service, and Wildlife Refuges all together.
BLM should be doing this nationally.
Instead, they’ve left a void that their opponents have been more than happy to fill with their own narrative.
The legal case for these districts is rock solid.
The Taylor Grazing Act established them as formal federal reservations — not temporary withdrawals, but lands set aside for a specific designated public purpose: protecting the range, providing orderly use by farmers and stockmen, and stabilizing the livestock industry.
When Congress passed the Federal Land Policy and Management Act in 1976, it explicitly left the TGA provisions intact.
A 2003 Solicitor’s clarification confirmed it plainly — Congress did not repeal or modify the grazing provisions of the TGA when it enacted FLPMA.
The classifications, boundaries, and mission remain legally sound.
Montana has an additional layer of protection that no other state can claim.
In 1939, Montana enacted the Montana Grass Conservation Act, establishing State Grazing Districts that work in cooperation with the federal system.
Local grazing district boards — seated by actual stockmen — were put in charge of administering these lands to protect the range and stabilize the livestock industry.
That’s local self-government at its best, exactly what these districts were designed to produce.
The bottom line is this: your grazing allotment didn’t appear out of thin air.
It rests on nearly a century of federal law, Montana statute, and administrative precedent.
But legal rights that aren’t visible on official maps are rights that can be chipped away — one planning document, one environmental review, one federal administrator at a time.
The MtNRC is doing the right thing by demanding BLM put these maps back where they belong — in black and white, in the official record, for all to see.
Ranching built this county.
The law is on your side.
But you have to make sure the government is forced to acknowledge it.
