top of page News Principles Calendar About Menu Close Donate I-194: Read the Fine Print Before You Vote By Shane Klakken, Montana House District 37 October 1, 2026 This week, Russ Cleveland of Montana PLAN brought an I-194 town hall to the Yogo Inn in Lewistown.
The measure will be on our November ballot, and most of us will decide on a few lines of ballot language that even Cleveland admits is hard to follow.
So here’s a plain look at what was said, what each side has going for it, and what nobody can promise yet.
What it would do Cleveland’s pitch rests on an old idea: the state grants corporations their powers, so the state can change them.
I-194 would strip “artificial persons” (corporations, LLCs, nonprofits, unions, trusts and partnerships) of the power to make political contributions or expenditures.
Any money or thing of value going to a campaign would have to come from a named individual instead.
Businesses could still lobby the legislature, endorse candidates and host candidate forums.
What they couldn’t do is put a company name on a campaign check or give away goods and services they normally sell.
Cleveland’s example was a business owner who could still support a candidate publicly and could still write a personal check, just not one from the company account.
Why supporters back it Montana has fought this fight before.
A century ago, copper money out of Butte and Anaconda was buying Senate seats, and Montanans answered with the 1912 Corrupt Practices Act.
It held for 98 years, until Citizens United.
Cleveland says a single Montana Senate race hit $162 million in 2024.
Supporters point out that in a state with so few voters, every outside dollar counts for more.
They also say money can hop between trusts, nonprofits and PACs until nobody can say where it started.
Cleveland noted the campaign gathered about 50,000 signatures using only volunteers.
He also argued that once a donor’s name is attached to a check, voters can weigh who is behind a message, and that transparency has to come first before anyone can talk seriously about limits.
Where the questions are Start with what it doesn’t do.
Cleveland calls it a “base hit,” not a home run.
There are no spending caps and no contribution limits.
It only aims to show who’s paying.
He says backers plan to pursue a constitutional amendment in 2028, which tells you they see this as a first step, not the last word.
Next, the courts.
Cleveland expects a legal challenge and pointed to an injunction against a similar Hawaii law.
Because it’s a citizen initiative, a court can’t trim it.
It stands or falls as a whole.
And because it’s a statute and not a constitutional amendment, a future legislature can amend or weaken it.
That happens to citizen initiatives more than folks think.
Then there’s the reach.
“Artificial persons” covers a lot of ground out here, including nonprofits, trade groups and unions.
Cleveland says everyday operations are untouched and that most small businesses never write political checks.
But the line between educating and advocating will get tested.
Enforcement can come from any group of citizens, and violators could see shareholders held personally liable.
Supporters call that a strong deterrent.
Skeptics may call it a lawsuit waiting to happen.
Cleveland also admitted the ballot language is poorly drafted.
A longer version with fuller definitions would become law, but most voters will only see the short summary.
The Montana Chamber of Commerce opposes the measure.
Cleveland says it speaks mainly for its biggest donors.
I didn’t hear the Chamber’s side that night, and you should.
A fair warning Nearly everything above about what I-194 would do comes from the people promoting it.
That’s one side of the table, and it deserves the same scrutiny as any other.
What I’d suggest Read the full text, not just the ballot summary.
Look up who’s funding both the yes and no campaigns on the Commissioner of Political Practices website.
Ask hard questions of anyone who says this measure fixes everything, or ruins everything.
Central Montanans have never needed anybody to read the fine print for us.
Let’s not start now.
