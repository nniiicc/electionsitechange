top of page News Principles Calendar About Menu Close Donate Keep the Land Moving: Montana’s Quiet Guard Against Forever Ownership By Shane Klakken, Montana House District 37 September 10, 2026 Out here in central Montana, land isn’t just dirt and grass.
It’s the place where a family sinks roots, raises kids, and hopes the next generation can keep the place going if they choose to.
That’s why a short line in our state constitution deserves more attention than it usually gets.
Article XIII, Section 6 says simply: “No perpetuities shall be allowed except for charitable purposes.” The language is almost word-for-word the same as the 1889 constitution that came with statehood.
The 1972 convention left it alone.
No long debate.
No fancy rewriting.
They kept it because the idea still made sense.
A “perpetuity” is any arrangement that tries to lock property up forever—or for so long that future owners can’t really control it.
Think of a ranch that can never be sold, never divided, never mortgaged, no matter what happens fifty or a hundred years down the road.
Or a trust that keeps land in one bloodline until the end of time.
The constitution says those deals are not allowed.
The only permanent arrangements it blesses are charitable ones—land given forever to a church, a hospital, a school, or a public park.
Why does this matter to people who work the land?
First, it keeps property alive in the marketplace.
If a family hits hard times—drought, low cattle prices, medical bills—the land has to be able to move.
Banks won’t lend against property that can’t be sold.
Young people who want to start ranching need a chance to buy in.
The rule against perpetuities stops one generation from tying the hands of every generation that follows.
Second, it pushes back against the old idea of permanent family empires.
Our grandparents knew what concentrated land ownership could do.
The constitution’s framers, both in 1889 and 1972, wanted freeholders, not a permanent landlord class.
They believed ordinary people should be able to own land, use it, improve it, and pass it on—or sell it—without some long-dead relative still calling the shots from the grave.
Third, it leaves room for real charity.
A family that wants to give a section of ground forever to the county for a park or to a church for a cemetery can do it.
The law makes a clear distinction between private control that never ends and public-spirited gifts that serve the whole community.
Montana has put the constitutional rule into everyday law.
Under the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities, most future interests have to vest within roughly 90 years or within 21 years after the death of people alive when the interest was created.
That is long enough for careful estate planning.
It is not long enough to lock a place up until the Second Coming.
For ranch families, this is practical freedom.
You can still leave the home place to your kids.
You can still set up a trust that lasts for a good long while.
You just cannot invent a scheme that pretends the land will never again be free to change hands.
That limit protects the next generation from being trapped by yesterday’s decisions, and it protects the community from having productive ground frozen in place while the world moves on.
Some people hear “rule against perpetuities” and think of dusty law books and lawyers arguing over technicalities.
That misses the point.
At its heart, the rule is a vote of confidence in living people.
It says the folks who walk the ground today should have the power to decide what happens to it, not some ironclad plan written in 1925 or 2025 that no one can touch.
Central Montana has always valued that kind of practical independence.
We respect the past, but we do not let it own the future.
Article XIII, Section 6 is one of the quiet ways our constitution keeps that balance.
It is not flashy.
It does not get debated on the coffee-shop radio every morning.
Yet it helps make sure that land remains something a working family can still buy, sell, mortgage, and pass along—rather than a permanent monument to someone else’s will.
That is worth protecting.
In a place where the horizon is wide and the next generation still matters, keeping the land free to move is not a technicality.
It is common sense written into the highest law of the state.
