top of page News Principles Calendar About Menu Close Donate The Grazing System Was Built for Ranchers — Let’s Make Sure It Stays That Way By Shane Klakken, Montana House District 37 August 5, 2026 There’s a principle embedded in the Taylor Grazing Act that doesn’t get talked about enough.
When Congress created the federal grazing district system in 1934, one of its explicit goals was to provide “the greatest extent of self-government to local stockmen.” Not Washington bureaucrats.
Not environmental organizations based in New York or San Francisco.
Local stockmen.
That principle matters today as much as it did ninety years ago, and it’s at the heart of what the Montana Natural Resource Coalition of Counties has been pressing BLM to honor in its latest round of regulatory revisions.
The MtNRC’s July comment letter to BLM Director Steve Pierce covered several specific regulatory proposals, and it’s worth walking through what the coalition supported — and where it raised concerns — because the details affect every permittee grazing on public land in central Montana.
First, the good news.
The MtNRC supports the proposed revision removing special use permitting requirements for indigenous animals .
The coalition notes, correctly, that there’s no statutory basis for that permitting requirement to begin with.
The TGA preference right system was built for people engaged in the livestock industry — farmers and stockmen — not as a vehicle for wildlife management schemes layered on top of grazing allotments.
Getting rid of a permit requirement that has no legal foundation is a straightforward win.
The coalition also strongly supports the overall direction of giving permittees greater flexibility to respond in real time to range conditions — weather, drought, available forage.
Anyone who has run cattle in central Montana knows that the range doesn’t follow a schedule.
A permit system rigid enough that a rancher can’t move cattle when conditions change isn’t managing land.
It’s managing paperwork.
The MtNRC is right to push for flexibility, and BLM should be listening.
Now the concern.
The proposed revision to the Office of Hearings and Appeals process would render a BLM decision inoperative — essentially suspended — as soon as it’s appealed.
At first glance that might sound like a protection for permittees.
But think through the implications.
If a rancher with a long-standing permit in good standing — meeting all rangeland health standards, paying his fees, doing everything right — gets his renewal approved, and someone files an appeal just to delay the process, that permittee couldn’t turn out on the range while the appeal grinds through the system.
That’s not protection.
That’s a new weapon for obstruction.
The MtNRC is asking BLM to think this through more carefully.
The coalition’s position is reasonable: an appeal shouldn’t automatically stay a decision when a preference right holder is in good standing and meeting their obligations.
There should be safeguards to prevent the appeals process from being weaponized against ranchers who’ve done nothing wrong.
Stepping back, what connects all of these issues — the TGA district mapping fight, the NEPA categorical exclusion fight, the permit flexibility and appeals process concerns — is a single underlying question: Who is the federal grazing system actually for?
The law gives a clear answer.
The Taylor Grazing Act was enacted to stabilize the livestock industry and the farming communities that depend on it.
FLPMA preserved those priorities.
PRIA reinforced them.
Montana went further than any other state by enacting the Montana Grass Conservation Act, creating state-level grazing district boards seated by stockmen to administer these lands locally.
Fergus County and Petroleum County exists because of ranching and farming.
So does most of central Montana.
Our customs, our economy, our communities — they’re built around the land and the livestock industry that depends on it.
When federal regulations make it harder to build a water pipeline, harder to see your allotment’s legal protections on a map, harder to get a permit renewed without an opponent grinding you down through appeals — those aren’t abstract policy problems.
They hit families and operations that have been on this land for generations.
The Montana Natural Resource Coalition of Counties is doing the work of making sure the federal government honors what the law already says.
That’s worth paying attention to.
And it’s worth showing up to support.
The grazing system was built for ranchers.
Let’s make sure Washington remembers that.
