I've been teaching Constitution classes in Lewistown for a while now, and one thing I've noticed is that a lot of people use words like "democracy," "republic," and "federalism" interchangeably — as if they all mean the same thing.
They don't.
And the differences aren't just academic.
They matter every time someone in Washington or Helena tries to do something to you that they have no business doing.
Let's start with the one I get asked about most: What's the difference between a republic and a democracy?
The short answer is that in a pure democracy, the majority rules — period.
Whatever 51% wants, 51% gets.
The Founders had studied history carefully enough to know that never ends well.
James Madison laid it out plainly in Federalist No.
10: pure democracies have historically been "spectacles of turbulence and contention," incompatible with personal security and property rights, "as short in their lives as they have been violent in their deaths." John Adams was blunter still: "Democracy never lasts long.
It soon wastes, exhausts and murders itself." That's why they built us a republic instead — one where power derives from the people but is exercised by elected representatives operating under a fixed set of rules.
Representation, separation of powers, checks and balances, an independent judiciary, and explicit protections for minority rights.
The whole point was to slow things down, filter out mob passions, and protect individuals from being steamrolled by a temporary majority.
When Benjamin Franklin walked out of the Constitutional Convention and someone asked what they'd produced, he said: "A republic, if you can keep it." That "if" was doing a lot of work.
Now, where does federalism fit?
Federalism is the architecture — the blueprint for how power is divided between the federal government and the states.
The national government handles defense, foreign policy, currency, and interstate matters.
States handle education, local law enforcement, infrastructure, and the things closest to home.
Each level has constitutionally protected powers that the other can't just swipe away.
That's not an accident.
Federalism is a check on centralized power, and it's one of the main reasons Montana gets to be Montana instead of just a distant province of Washington, D.C.
Here in central Montana, we feel the tension between those two levels of government more than most.
When the federal government tells ranchers how to manage land their families have worked for generations, or mandates policies from a desk in D.C. that make no practical sense in Central Montana, that's federalism breaking down.
The Constitution didn't grant Washington unlimited authority — it granted specific, enumerated powers in Article I Section 8 of the US Constitution and left everything else to the states and to the people in the Tenth Amendment of the Bill of Rights.
There's one more concept worth understanding, and it comes up more than you might think: the “Fiction of the Law”.
A legal fiction is when the law deliberately treats something as true — even when everyone knows it isn't — in order to reach a practical result.
The most familiar example is corporate personhood: a corporation isn't a human being, but the law treats it as a "person" so it can own property, sign contracts, and be sued.
That's useful.
Legal fictions started in Roman law, were adopted into English common law, and exist today because sometimes applying rigid rules to complex reality produces unjust results.
But here's where I'd caution folks to pay attention: legal fictions can be used to expand power, not just achieve fairness.
When courts or agencies treat something as legally true that isn't factually true, it changes what they can do to you.
Jurisdictions get extended.
Rules get stretched.
Remedies get invented.
Jeremy Bentham, the English legal philosopher, called legal fictions "willful falsehoods" used by judges to quietly usurp legislative authority.
That critique still has teeth today.
“Fiction of the Law” has even stronger power in the false belief of the “separation of church and state”.
The reason I teach these things — and why I keep writing about them here — is simple.
A self-governing people can only hold their government accountable if they understand how it actually works.
Republic, federalism, legal fiction — these aren't words for lawyers and textbooks.
They're the vocabulary of self-defense for a free citizenry.
If you don't know the rules of the game, you can't call a foul when one gets committed against you.
Founding Father John Jay put it this way: “Every member of the State ought diligently to read and study the constitution of his country, and teach the rising generation to be free.
By knowing their rights, they will sooner perceive when they are violated, and be better prepared to defend and assert them.” Franklin said we have a republic, if we can keep it.
Out here in central Montana, I think we still want to keep it.
That starts with understanding what it is.
