THE LAW:
The constitutional boundaries governing the use of Flock/ALPR systems and data have not been conclusively defined or decided by either the Supreme Court of the United States or the Supreme Court of South Carolina.
But as a lawyer who has spent 30 years litigating constitutional rights in federal court, including the United States Supreme Court, and as a professor of constitutional law at two law schools, I would add two important considerations to this discussion:
YOUR RIGHT TO PRIVACY IS IN SOUTH CAROLINA'S CONSTITUTION
(1) South Carolina's Constitution goes further than the federal Fourth Amendment.
It expressly protects the people against “unreasonable invasions of privacy.” S.C.
Const. art.
I, §10.
Those words mean something; and
THE U.S.
SUPREME COURT JUST ADDRESSED A DATA PRIVACY RIGHTS CASE
(2) Just this June, the United States Supreme Court decided Chatrie v.
United States (2026), holding that government acquisition of a person's stored cell-phone location history is a Fourth Amendment search because we have a reasonable expectation of privacy in our location information.
The Court sent the case back to determine whether that search was constitutionally reasonable.
This SCOTUS decision could impact the privacy rights of ALPR / Flock camera data.
NOTE: As courts interpret this decision, and the law develops, it will be up to communities, like Oconee County just did, to determine what they want for their community.
As Attorney General, I will work to make sure that as surveillance technology changes, the rights guaranteed to you by our Constitutions continue to have meaning in your daily life while also providing for public safety.
The Rule of Law can and should keep you safe from criminals AND protect you from government overreach.
Those are not competing values.
I honor them both.
That's how The Rule of Law is supposed to work—and that's what your Attorney General is supposed to do.
