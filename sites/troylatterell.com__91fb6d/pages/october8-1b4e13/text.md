But eventually, you have to deliver something. ​ I think we should expect the same accountability from our elected officials. ​ Sponsoring bills, sitting on committees and proposing ideas are all parts of the legislative process.
But activity alone isn't a result. ​ The more important question is: What did you actually accomplish? ​ The Distinction between attempts vs results is important ​ The legislative process is difficult, and it should be.
Getting something accomplished requires more than introducing a bill. ​ You have to make the case for it, build support, work with people who may disagree with you, navigate committees and ultimately get enough people behind an idea to move it forward. ​ But there still has to be a distinction between work attempted and results delivered . ​ Throughout my career, if a project failed, I couldn't point to how many meetings we held or how much work went into it and call it a success. ​ I had to answer a much simpler question: ​ What did we actually deliver? ​ Public service deserves that same standard. ​ ​ Key 33 Details – Show Me 33 Our representative isn't elected simply to introduce legislation in Jefferson City.
The purpose of that work is to accomplish something for the people she represents.
What legislation successfully moved forward?
What policies changed?
What funding came back to our communities?
What problems facing our schools, cities, businesses, seniors or families were addressed?
What priorities from House District 33 were elevated into meaningful action?
Those are fair questions for any elected official, particularly after multiple years in office.
A legislative record shouldn't simply tell us what someone worked on.
It should help us understand what their work accomplished. ​ A Better Path Forward & Conclusion ​​​​ I believe representatives should be willing to measure themselves by results. ​ Identify the problem.
Learn enough to understand it.
Develop a realistic solution.
Build the relationships necessary to move it forward.
And then keep working until something meaningful happens. ​ At the end of a term, I don't want to simply hand constituents a list of bills I sponsored or committees I served on. ​ I want to be able to answer a much harder question: ​ What is better in House District 33 because you sent me there? ​ I've certainly had projects that didn't turn out the way I wanted.
But accountability means being willing to acknowledge that too, learn from it and determine what needs to happen next. ​ That's the difference between being busy and getting something done. ​ That's the value and the approach I would bring to Jefferson City.
