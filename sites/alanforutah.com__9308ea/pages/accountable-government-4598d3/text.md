Why it matters Politicians shouldn't rewrite the rules for themselves.
In 2018, Utah voters passed Proposition 4 to stop politicians from drawing district lines to favor themselves or their party.
Lawmakers later changed parts of that voter-led reform.
The Utah Supreme Court also reviewed whether Amendment D's ballot materials accurately told voters what the measure would do.
The principle is simple: voters deserve the full story before they cast a ballot.
This year: Amendment B.
In 2026, a petition to repeal Prop 4 failed after more than 10,000 Utahns withdrew their signatures.
Now the Legislature is asking voters to approve Amendment B.
It would require 60% of the vote for citizen initiatives that create or raise a tax.
When the Legislature raises taxes itself, it still needs only a simple majority.
That's one rule for politicians and a harder one for voters.
We shouldn't let the Legislature move the goalposts on voters.
I'm voting no on Amendment B.
Also on the ballot: Amendment A.
Right now, proposed changes to the Utah Constitution have to be printed in newspapers across the state for two months before an election.
Amendment A would drop that rule and let the Legislature decide how to publish them.
The plan is to post them on two state websites instead.
Not everyone goes looking on a state website, and I want it to stay easy to see what's on your ballot.
I'm voting no on Amendment A.
The Utah Supreme Court.
This year the Legislature added two justices to the Utah Supreme Court, taking it from five to seven.
That's a big change to our highest court, and voters never got a say in it.
I'd let voters decide whether the court grows or shrinks.
