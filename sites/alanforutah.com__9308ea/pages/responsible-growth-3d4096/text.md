What this means for your household Good growth should not quietly raise your costs.
Before a major project moves forward, residents should know how it could affect utility rates, reliability, water, roads, and noise.
They should also know who will pay for new capacity.
01 · POWER Protect grid reliability Make large power users show how they will affect capacity, household rates, and the upgrades needed to serve them.
02 · WATER Measure what Utah uses Invest in accurate water tracking so conservation plans are based on real use and clear results.
03 · GUARDRAILS Make project promises binding Require firm plans for efficient cooling, water reuse, less noise, and clean air before approval.
04 · INFRASTRUCTURE Coordinate the whole system Plan housing and business growth alongside roads, transit, utilities, schools, and emergency services.
05 · FAIR SHARE Make data centers pay their way Data centers should tell us how much water and power they use, and pay a premium for it.
Your utility bill shouldn’t be covering theirs.

Our lake should be here for generations to enjoy.
A local example What happened at East Bay In February 2026, a developer asked Provo to rezone part of the old Novell campus at 1507 S.
180 E. for a data center.
Its own estimates said it could use 5 to 50 megawatts of power, and 7,000 to 12,000 gallons of water a day in its first phase.
The planning commission recommended approval.
Then neighbors spoke up.
They asked why it would go next to a school and two hotels, whether its water plan would really work, and how little it would add to the local economy once it was built.
Council members also worried about power rates and emissions.
On March 10, the City Council voted 7–0 to turn it down.
They said they wanted to finish the city's economic plan before making a long-term decision.
In April, the council went further.
It voted 5–1 to stop data centers from building their own power plants, which keeps any new one at 50 megawatts or less.
It also required cooling systems that don't evaporate water.
Provo's leaders listened because residents showed up.
Not every community gets that chance.
Utah County is writing rules right now for data centers in rural areas, and people there deserve the same chance to weigh in.
What I'll push for statewide 01 Publish expected electrical and water demand in understandable terms.
02 Identify who pays for new power generation, transmission lines, and grid upgrades.
03 Make promises on efficiency, cooling, noise, and rate protection binding.
A statewide example The Stratos project Stratos is a huge data center project in Box Elder County, just north of the Great Salt Lake.
A state agency, the Military Installation Development Authority, and the county commission both signed off on it.
As first approved, it covered about 40,000 acres.
People filed about 3,900 protests against its request for water rights.
The company pulled the request and says it will try again.
When residents asked to put the county's approval to a public vote, they were turned down, and they're now fighting that in court.
I oppose Stratos.
It was too big, and the community was ignored.
Decisions like this should ultimately rest with the people.
01 Protect residents' right to a referendum on major decisions like this one.
02 Require data centers to disclose their water and energy use and pay a premium on their utilities.
03 Protect the Great Salt Lake by helping farmers switch to more efficient irrigation.
