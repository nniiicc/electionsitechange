Diehl says Oregon has enough tax momey For Diehl,Oregon needs to do a way better job of prioritizing its spending, not seeking new taxes The following is from state Representative Ed Diehl, writing about Oregon government's lack of competence and its inability to deliver on programs and not being able to prioritize its spending, and its continual lack of fiscal discipline and its continually looking for taxpayers to bail it out: Friend, Sometimes one person's story can expose everything that is wrong with a government system.
The Oregonian recently told the story of a formerly homeless Oregon woman who suffers from serious mental illness.
Her story is ultimately one of hope.
She found stability.
She found housing.
She is working.
But buried inside her story are three remarkable admissions about the systems that were supposed to help her.
She believes some people suffering from serious mental illness need a level of care they cannot obtain on their own.
“I think we should reinstate institutions to care for people who need more,” she said.
She also limits how much she works because earning too much could jeopardize the government health and disability benefits she depends upon.
And all of this is happening while Portland-area governments say they don't have enough money to keep hundreds of shelter beds open.
Put those three things together and you begin to see Oregon's real problem.
We don't necessarily lack money.
We lack priorities.
Hundreds of millions for climate programs.
Not enough for shelter beds.
Portland is reducing shelter spending as it deals with its budget shortfall.
At the same time, the Portland Clean Energy Community Benefits Fund is projected to begin FY 2026-27 with a fund balance of approximately $698 million.
Think about the scale of that number.
Nearly $700 million accumulated in a government climate fund while people suffering from serious mental illness are sleeping on Portland's streets and the city says it cannot afford existing shelter capacity.
That doesn't mean every dollar of that $698 million is uncommitted or can legally be transferred to shelters tomorrow.
Much of it is dedicated to grants, contracts and future expenditures.
But government created those priorities.
And Portland has already demonstrated that the wall around this money isn't absolute.
This year the city transferred approximately $27 million of PCEF interest earnings into its General Fund.
Apparently dedicated money can become flexible when elected officials decide it needs to be.
The question isn't whether we should empty the climate fund tomorrow.
The question is simpler: How did we create a government where accumulating hundreds of millions for future climate programs takes precedence over keeping vulnerable human beings off the streets today?
More than half a billion dollars saved for future preschool expansion Cross the governmental boundary from the City of Portland to Multnomah County and the numbers are just as extraordinary.
Multnomah County's Preschool for All program has accumulated approximately $514 million in "Dedicated Savings." Add approximately $47 million in contingency and reserves and the number exceeds $560 million.
Again, that money isn't an accounting accident.
It is intentional.
Multnomah County designed the program to collect more taxes than it currently needs and bank the excess to pay for preschool expansion in future years.
There may be arguments for doing that.
But there is also a moral question that shouldn't be ignored.
Should government collect taxes today and warehouse more than half a billion dollars for services it plans to provide years from now while cutting shelter capacity for desperate people who need help tonight?
You don't have to oppose preschool to believe the answer is no.
Government is about priorities.
And budgets reveal those priorities better than speeches ever will.
Then there's the money that was actually supposed to solve homelessness The third pot of money may be the most troubling.
Metro's Supportive Housing Services tax was specifically created to address homelessness.
During its first three fiscal years, the program collected approximately $592 million.
Yet Metro's auditor found that only about 40% of the tax revenue collected during those years had been spent.
The result?
An accumulated fund balance of approximately $354 million.
To be clear, that is a historical figure, not the current balance.
Governments have subsequently spent down much of that accumulated money.
But that raises an entirely different question.
How does a region collect so much money specifically for homelessness that it accumulates $354 million it cannot deploy — and then, just a few years later, find itself closing shelter beds?
That's not a revenue problem.
That's a management problem.
It's a priorities problem.
And ultimately it's an accountability problem.
Then listen to the woman we're supposedly trying to help The most powerful part of this story isn't actually the money.
It's what this woman says about mental illness.
After living through serious mental illness and homelessness herself, she recognizes something Oregon policymakers have been reluctant to acknowledge: Some people are simply too sick to seek the treatment they need on their own.
For decades, the dominant philosophy has been deinstitutionalization and community-based treatment.
Organizations such as Disability Rights Oregon continue to make preventing institutionalization an explicit priority.
Community treatment absolutely should be the first choice for people capable of succeeding with it.
But what about the person experiencing psychosis who doesn't believe he is sick?
What about the woman incapable of keeping an appointment, taking her medication or even recognizing that she needs help?
What about the person cycling endlessly between a sidewalk, an emergency room, a jail cell and back to the sidewalk?
Calling that "freedom" isn't compassionate.
Sometimes compassion means intervening.
Even NAMI has begun to recognize that This debate has changed substantially in just a few years.
I know because I've been part of it.
In 2023, I tried working with NAMI Oregon on civil-commitment reforms.
At that time, there was substantial resistance to expanding involuntary treatment, including from organizations within the mental-health advocacy community.
NAMI's position has since evolved.
By late 2024, NAMI Oregon publicly acknowledged how difficult this issue had been within its own membership.
Executive Director Chris Bouneff described civil commitment as a "tightrope" and said it wasn't an issue on which he originally wanted NAMI to lead.
But NAMI also acknowledged the reality staring Oregon in the face: Our current system often waits until someone suffering from serious mental illness commits a crime before government intervenes.
So in 2025 NAMI Oregon did something significant.
It sponsored legislation to lower Oregon's criteria for civil commitment.
That evolution shows that experience is forcing people across the ideological spectrum to reconsider the assumptions behind Oregon's mental-health system.
We don't need to recreate the horrific institutions of generations past.
We need a continuum of care.
Community treatment whenever possible.
Intensive outpatient treatment when necessary.
Secure residential treatment for those who need it.
Adequate psychiatric hospital beds.
And, for the relatively small number of people whose illness has rendered them incapable of recognizing their own condition or caring for themselves, a humane civil-commitment system that allows us to intervene before they end up in jail, dead or living under a tarp.
That isn't abandoning civil liberties.
It's recognizing that serious mental illness can sometimes rob a person of the ability to exercise those liberties meaningfully.
And then government tells her not to work too much Finally, consider what happens when someone actually makes it through this broken system.
This woman got stable.
She found housing.
She began working.
That's exactly the outcome taxpayers should want.
And then she encountered another government-created problem.
If she earns too much, she risks losing health coverage and disability benefits she depends upon.
So she limits her work.
She's responding rationally to the incentives government created.
Think about how backwards that is.
We spend enormous sums trying to move someone from homelessness toward stability.
Then, when she succeeds, we create a financial incentive for her not to become fully independent.
A rational safety net should work in the opposite direction.
Benefits should phase out gradually as someone's income rises.
Working another hour should always leave a person better off, not threaten the medical care or stability that allowed her to work in the first place.
Government should provide a ladder.
We've built a cliff.
Put people before programs That's what makes this woman's story so important.
She isn't a line item in a government budget.
She's the person all of these programs supposedly exist to help.
And look at what we've built around her.
A city cutting shelter capacity while its climate program carries an enormous accumulated balance.
A county cutting homelessness services while deliberately saving more than half a billion dollars for future preschool expansion.
A regional homelessness program that once accumulated $354 million because government couldn't deploy the money fast enough.
A mental-health system that too often waits for someone to deteriorate until the criminal-justice system intervenes.
And a disability system that can punish someone financially for working more after she finally gets back on her feet.
Oregon taxpayers are extraordinarily generous.
The answer cannot always be another tax.
Before government asks working families for another dollar, we should demand that it put first things first.
Keep emergency shelters available for people who need them.
Build enough psychiatric and secure residential capacity to treat people with serious mental illness.
Fix Oregon's civil-commitment laws so families don't have to wait for a loved one to commit a crime before getting meaningful help.
Reform benefit cliffs so work and independence are rewarded rather than punished.
And take a hard look at the hundreds of millions of dollars accumulating in dedicated government accounts while basic human needs go unmet.
We don't need to choose between compassion and fiscal responsibility.
Real compassion demands fiscal responsibility.
Because every dollar government warehouses, wastes or spends on a lower priority is a dollar that isn't available for the person sleeping outside tonight.
This woman's story proves there is hope.
Now Oregon government needs to get its priorities straight.
Thank you for taking the time to read and stay engaged.
I’ll continue working to bring common sense, accountability, and transparency to Salem, and I’ll keep you informed about the issues that matter to our communities and our state.
Thank you for the opportunity to serve you.
