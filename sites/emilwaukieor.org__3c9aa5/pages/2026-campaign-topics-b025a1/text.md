But this is nothing new for Oregon summers Oregon timber harvesting/sales down sharply - boosting timber sales could raise firefighting monies Randal O'Toole, who up until recently worked for the CATO Institute and previously represented environmental groups opposing U.S Forest policies as a researcher, writes that Oregon is naturally situated in a wildfire plain.
There is not a lot we can do to avoid wildfires and forest fires in Oregon and the Pacific Northwest.
Dry summers east of the Cascades, and dry lightning storms make Oregon and the Pacific Northwest susceptible to wildfires and forest fires.
O'Toole writes that historical data on forest fires is fraught with problems, such that wildfire trends are very much not certain.
Seems the Forest Service in the first half of the 20th century did a lot of prescribed burns, and the Forest Service included prescribed acre burns in the total for all burns.
Prescribed burns were not included in the data after the middle part of the 20th century.
In another twist, firefighters used to work right up to the wildfire line, but after the mid-1990s, firefighters worked farther back from the outer edge of wildfires.
We hear a lot about climate change being the cause of increased wildfires in the Pacific Northwest.
The counter is that the sharp drop in timber harvesting has led to a buildup of woody debris in the forests, feeding larger wildfire burns.
O'Toole dismisses the latter, saying wildfires are just plain unavoidable regardless of timber harvest levels.
But I would say that if we were to boost timber harvest by 50 to 100%, returning to 70% of the harvest levels of the 1960s through 1990s, that our forests could bring in several more tens of millions of dollars in tax proceeds (both timber tax and income tax) to help increase wildfire fighting resources.
This would help tend to help fight wildfires more quickly and thoroughly than presently.
Currently, Oregon is growing new trees faster than they are being harvested, such that a boost in harvesting by 50 to 100% could be sustainable.
Also, wildfires that turn into forest fires often are started in summer-dried grasslands.
Expanding grazing could help reduce grassland fires around forests.
More goats?
Oregon Senator Merkley and Oregon Congressman Bentz are sponsoring a bill in Congress called the Wildfire Reduction Market act.
The idea behind this bill is to turn the woody debris of U.S Forest lands into biomass fuel, which could boost monies for wildfire fighting and also reduce woody debris fueling forest fires.
Just blaming climate change for wildfires and demanding we ban all fossil fuels is really not much help at all in addressing wildfire risks.
Even if we could arrest the increase in climatic temperatures immediately, wildfires are going to continue, as now and in natural history.
Banning fossil fuels would likely lead to economic decline, which actually works against having adequate firefighting resources.
Rand O'Toole has quite the radical view about wildfires in the Pacific Northwest, some of which I do not agree, nor do many others agree.
But O'Toole is worth a read, very educational on the topic of forestry and wildfires.
