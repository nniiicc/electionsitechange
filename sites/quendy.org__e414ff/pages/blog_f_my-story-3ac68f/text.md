Introduction: This isn’t a campaign piece.
It’s my story — the one that shaped me into the person I am today and the reason I decided to run for state representative.
I’m sharing it because I believe honesty matters, healing is possible, and truth — even the painful kind — connects us all.
A Quick Disclaimer This piece isn’t directly about my campaign, so you may wonder why I’m posting it.
The reason is simple: I promised to be as honest and transparent as possible — and this is an important part of my “why.” Why I Decided to Run A few loved ones have questioned my decision to run for state representative.
They worry that I might get hurt in today’s volatile political climate.
Honestly, if you had asked me 10 or #ago, I would have agreed with them.
I’ve never had thick skin, and I am by nature, a conflict avoider.
I probably would have cried the first time someone asked me a difficult question or challenged me in any way.
But before I threw my hat in the ring, I realized something: I had already lived through much worse than anything a campaign could throw my way.
My Story — The Short Version I don’t like parading my past traumas, but I believe context matters.
I don’t want sympathy, but without my story, I wouldn’t be the person I am today — or the candidate I’ve become.
Over the past 20 years, my life has been shaped by both hardship and resilience.
In 2006, I divorced my college sweetheart — my husband of 15 years and the father of my two children.
It had never occurred to me that “forever” might not last.
Our kids were only six and eight.
That was my first real heartbreak.
I thought my life was over.
Around the same time, my mom was diagnosed with lung cancer.
I’ve never felt that I was there for her like I should have been.
I slipped into my first depression, but with the help of a dear friend and a good counselor, I began the process of healing.
A New Chapter — and New Challenges A few years later, I earned my Master’s in Middle School Education from UALR and moved from Little Rock to Fort Smith to marry my second husband, Bobby.
It was a major life change—for all of us.
When my daughter decided to return to Little Rock to live with her dad after living in Fort Smith for a year, it broke my heart.
I missed her so much and since my son stayed with me, hated that they were separated.
I sucked it up and did the best I could to still be a good mom.
It hurt terribly but thankfully, we have ended up with a great relationship now.
In 2012, another blow: Bobby was diagnosed with testicular cancer.
What started as something “98% curable” turned into years of major surgeries, chemo, and relapses.
We fought side by side through every test, every hospital stay, and every sleepless night.
In 2015, we heard, “Cancer is back, and you have 1-3 months to live.
Nothing we can do.” We went to Houston to MD Anderson as a last ditch effort.
“His courage to keep trying taught me more about strength than any campaign slogan ever could.” My Son’s Struggle Around that same time, my son, Evan, began experiencing seizures.
Despite multiple doctors and endless tests, no clear cause was found.
When he turned to drugs a few years later, it broke me in a new way.
Rehab after rehab, relapse after relapse — we tried everything any parent would and more.
Counseling, prayer, medical help, hope.
Sometimes hope was all I had left.
Even in his darkest moments, Evan’s heart was good.
He loved big, and he tried hard.
I saw in him qualities of myself.
Watching your child struggle is an awful feeling.
I heard in him the agony of his pain.
Addiction is so very cruel.
When It Rains, It Pours Despite all my efforts and prayers, neither of my patients were doing well.
We went to visit Evan in a rehab in Austin, Texas in late December 2016.
On the same trip, we had scheduled tests for Bobby at MD Anderson.
What we thought was lingering fatigue from prior treatments turned out to be leukemia — AML.
Another devastating diagnosis.
Another round of hospitals, relocations, and fighting for our lives.
I had 8 days to plan a move, find somewhere to live in Houston sight unseen, pack everything, take a leave of absence from my job, go visit my parents (my mom was not well) and whatever the heck else I managed to do.
Days in Houston were mainly spent at the hospital.
From treatments given in an isolation room for a month to daily visits for tests, transfusions, treatments……finally, a last chance stem cell transplant of his brother’s stem cells.
Having a “day off” was rare and precious, I took classes to learn to take care of ports and central lines.
I could even give certain nutrients and meds at home.
I planted a fairly large garden for fresh vegetables and outdoor mental therapy .
Of course, this was actually an easier time for me because I wasn’t working full time while trying to juggle all the things.
I treasure the time we had together.
As if that weren’t enough, Hurricane Harvey destroyed our Houston home in late August of 2017.
As the water got deeper and entered our house, our wonderful neighbors helped us into their garage apartment where we spent around 32 hours with a total of 5 adults, 3 kids, a pet rabbit, our over-tranquilized cat in a carrier, and our terrified dog in his crate.
We watched with fascination and horror as the water got deeper, the rain kept coming and our pretty tree lined street became a river for boat traffic instead of automobiles.
I had to get Bobby away from that filthy water.
Thankfully our car was parked in a higher area, and we were able to get out when the water receded.
After a few days in a sweet friend’s empty “for sale” home, I leased a furnished apartment, and we began again there.
Not long after, doctors told us the words we had long dreaded: “The leukemia is back, and there’s nothing more we can do.” Bobby passed away on October 16, 2017.
I had focused so much time and energy on keeping him alive, it had never really occurred to me that he might die.
After spending almost every minute together for our time in Houston, he was suddenly gone.
I never say he “lost” his battle — I believe he simply found peace for his battered body and mind.
With no real home or comforting space, I came back to my hometown of England.
Within 3 weeks, my mom passed away too.
I'm very grateful for those 3 weeks with her.
Her funeral was on a Friday.
Bobby’s memorial service was that Sunday.
There’s no handbook for that kind of grief.
Life After Loss After losing my husband and mother, I decided to stay in England to be with my dad.
In January of 2018, Evan begged to come live with me, and I agreed.
We lived in my parents’ RV while I remodeled my grandparents’ house next door.
There were more hard times as my dad fell and broke his hip.
He ended up intubated in the ICU while I couldn’t go to the hospital because I came down with the flu and strep throat.
Evan hit rock bottom.
He had found the drugs that are readily available in any small town.
I was running on a weird version of autopilot, and my dad was trying to rehab while grieving his wife of 56 years.
Remodeling and going to get my things from Houston was my escape.
Evan tried so hard to get well again.
He found a rehab in South Florida that helped him.
For quite a while — in rehab and recovery — it looked like he was going to make it.
He built a good life in South Florida: taking college courses, working several jobs, mentoring others, and finding a kind of peace.
He told me he was happy for the first time in the spring of 2019.
I cried.
Then, in November 2019, I got a knock at my door that no parent should ever have.
Local police had come to tell me that Evan had died.
Fentanyl.
Whether it was an overdose or a seizure triggered by bad drugs, I’ll never know.
It doesn’t really matter to me.
All I know is that my world stopped that night.
I only thought I had experienced pain.
Finding Purpose Through Pain Since then, I’ve kept going — sometimes barely.
Many days it was a triumph to get out of bed and take a shower.
In 2020, I took a job as the England Elementary School librarian, earned another master’s degree as a Library Media Specialist, and cared for my aging father as he battled serious health issues.
During his hospital stays, I often felt on the verge of losing my mind once again.
As many of you know, serving as a caregiver is not an easy job and doesn’t get easier with experience.
My daughter, Emma, earned her PhD and moved to Idaho for her career in agronomy.
She is my pride and joy — a testament to tenacity and strength.
I know Evan, Bobby, and my mom are so proud of her too.
My stepdaughter and her family have dealt with some very trying times that have preyed on my mind as well, but those are their stories to tell.
I will just say that I love them, and I am very proud of them for sticking together and showing great perseverance through their many trials.
My life feels divided into eras — my “many different lives.” My heart has seemingly endless holes and cracks.
There are days I feel too old for my age, days when I wonder what the point of it all was if it was just to be taken away.
Why plan a future when it will never work out?
But somehow, the light always returns.
Thank God.
Why I’m Running I know now that life isn’t about avoiding pain — it’s about what you do with it.
I’ve been lifted up by family, friends, coworkers, students, doctors, nurses, counselors, and countless strangers who became angels in my story.
I could never name them all, but I carry their kindness with me.
My goal is to do everything I can for the rest of my life to pay these many acts of kindness forward.
So, why write a blog post about depressing events when I’m trying to run a campaign focused on positivity?
Because I think we need to be vulnerable to help others.
Because this story is uniquely mine, and I've gained wisdom from it.
Because it's real life.
And real life is exactly why I’m running for state representative.
