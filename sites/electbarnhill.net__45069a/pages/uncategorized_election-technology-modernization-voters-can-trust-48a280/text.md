Election technology modernization is not about replacing every proven process with a new machine.
It is about using the right tools, with clear rules and paper records, so Nevada voters can trust the work from registration through certification.
Nevada’s counties serve very different communities.
What works at a busy Clark County polling place may need a different setup in Ely or a small rural community.
The standard should still be the same everywhere: lawful voters can participate, election workers can do their jobs securely, and the public can understand how results were verified.
Modern technology must leave a clear record Technology can reduce errors, speed up routine work, and give voters better information.
It can also create new risks if it is adopted carelessly or operated without independent checks.
A modern election system should never ask voters to take its accuracy on faith.
That is why paper matters.
Whether a voter marks a paper ballot by hand or uses an accessible ballot-marking device, the final ballot should create a voter-verifiable paper record.
That record is the durable evidence of voter intent.
It gives election officials and auditors something physical to review if questions arise, and it protects the public from having to rely solely on software.
Electronic systems still have an appropriate role.
They can help verify registration, manage check-in lines, format ballots, tabulate results, and publish unofficial returns.
But electronic convenience must be paired with accountable procedures.
Systems should be tested before an election, secured while in use, and audited against paper records after ballots are counted.
The goal is not to make elections more complicated.
The goal is to make each critical step understandable and verifiable.
A voter should be able to hear, in plain language, what system was used, what checks were completed, and when the final results became official.
Election technology modernization requires statewide standards Nevadans should not have to wonder whether basic election safeguards change from one county line to the next.
Counties need room to manage their local operations, facilities, and staffing realities.
At the same time, the Secretary of State’s office should set clear statewide standards for technology, security practices, training, documentation, and public reporting.
Those standards should begin before equipment reaches a polling place.
New voting and election-management systems should be thoroughly evaluated, certified under applicable requirements, and tested in conditions that reflect real election use.
Counties should not be left to decipher technical requirements on their own or reinvent training materials election after election.
A responsible modernization plan also recognizes that equipment has a life cycle.
Aging devices can become difficult to maintain and may no longer receive security updates.
Replacing them on a planned schedule is wiser than rushing a purchase after a failure.
The state should maintain an inventory of essential election technology, identify approaching replacement needs, and help counties prepare predictable budgets.
That approach respects taxpayers as well as voters.
The lowest initial price is not always the best value if a system is hard to support, difficult to audit, or expensive to replace in a few years.
Purchasing decisions should weigh security, paper verification, accessibility, training demands, service support, and long-term cost.
Security is a daily discipline, not an Election Day promise Cybersecurity is not a single product a county can buy and forget.
It is the daily work of controlling access, applying updates, protecting backups, monitoring systems, and training people to recognize risks.
Election offices need practical support in each of those areas.
A sound statewide program should require strong account controls, including multi-factor authentication where appropriate, so access to sensitive systems is limited and traceable.
It should establish tested backup and recovery procedures, because continuity matters when equipment fails, weather interrupts operations, or a network problem occurs.
It should also separate critical election systems from unnecessary outside connections whenever possible.
People remain one of the strongest defenses.
Election staff and temporary workers need clear training on device handling, password protection, chain-of-custody records, and suspicious emails or calls.
Training should be repeated and straightforward, not buried in a binder that is never opened during a busy election.
The public deserves confidence without being handed a technical manual.
Regular, plain-language reporting can explain what security steps were taken, whether required tests were completed, and how the state and counties are improving preparedness.
Transparency does not mean publishing information that could aid a bad actor.
It means reporting enough for voters to see that safeguards are real, documented, and reviewed.
Audits should answer reasonable questions with evidence An audit is not a ceremony after an election.
It is a disciplined comparison between reported results and the records that support them.
When paper ballots are available, audits can check whether tabulation results match the voter-verified evidence.
Nevada should use transparent audit procedures that are consistent, documented, and open to appropriate public observation.
The process should identify how ballots or contests are selected, what is being checked, who conducts the work, and how findings are reported.
Where the law permits, bipartisan participation and trained public observers can strengthen confidence in the process.
Risk-based audits deserve serious consideration because they focus effort where it matters most.
Rather than treating every contest exactly alike, they use statistical methods to determine how much checking is needed to provide strong evidence that a reported outcome is correct.
The method must be explained in language ordinary voters can follow, and it must never replace the paper record that makes a meaningful audit possible.
Public reporting should distinguish between unofficial election-night returns and certified results.
Election night is about timely information.
Certification is about completing canvasses, resolving lawful questions, and confirming that required checks have been performed.
Voters can handle that distinction when officials communicate it plainly and consistently.
Modernization must protect access for lawful voters Security and access are not opposites.
A system that protects ballots but creates unreasonable barriers has failed voters.
A system that is easy to use but cannot be verified has failed them as well.
Good administration does both.
Voter ID can be implemented with a practical plan that verifies identity while protecting seniors, students, rural residents, voters with disabilities, and other lawful voters who may need help obtaining acceptable identification.
Clear notice, early outreach, reasonable assistance, and consistent rules are essential.
No voter should learn the requirements for the first time while standing at a polling place.
Technology should also improve access to accurate information.
Voters should be able to confirm registration status, find their polling options, understand identification requirements, and know how to correct a records issue before Election Day.
Those services need alternatives for Nevadans who lack reliable internet access or prefer in-person assistance.
Modern service means meeting people where they are, not assuming everyone has the same tools.
Accessible voting equipment must be reliable, available, and supported by trained workers.
Privacy and independence for voters with disabilities are not extras.
They are part of running an election that respects every eligible citizen.
Results, not noise, should guide every upgrade The best election technology often does not draw attention to itself.
It helps a voter check in correctly, gives an election worker a dependable process, preserves a paper record, and gives auditors the evidence they need.
The public sees the result: an election administered with care and explained without excuses.
Brad Lee Barnhill’s experience as a computer systems analyst and Nevada litigation paralegal points to a straightforward principle: systems work best when the rules are clear, the records are accurate, and the work can be checked.
Modernization should be measured by those results, not by how impressive a new device looks in a demonstration.
Nevada voters deserve an election system that treats their ballot as something worth protecting from the moment it is cast until the final results are certified.
That is a practical standard, and it is one the Secretary of State’s office should pursue with discipline, transparency, and respect for every lawful voter.
