Two of us answered: Secretary Cisco Aguilar and me.
If you’re deciding how to vote, I’d encourage you to read both answers side by side .
They show two different ideas of what this office is for.
I wrote my answers before I filed my federal lawsuit over Nevada’s voter rolls, so you won’t find it there.
I’ll fill in that part here.
Credit where it’s due Secretary Aguilar replaced an outdated business filing system, moved the state onto a new voter registration system, and points to a national ranking that placed Nevada’s elections second in the country.
He also reports a big rise in tribal turnout in 2024.
Those are real efforts, and voters should weigh them.
Where we agree: Nevada’s voter data belongs to Nevadans When the U.S.
Department of Justice demanded Nevada’s complete, unredacted voter file, including birth dates, driver’s license numbers, and partial Social Security numbers, Secretary Aguilar gave federal officials the public voter list and declined to hand over the rest, citing Nevada privacy law.
A federal judge has since dismissed the DOJ’s lawsuit .
He was right, and I’d have done the same.
Nevadans gave that information to their state so they could vote, not so it could be handed to federal agencies.
Nevada’s voter registration information belongs to Nevadans.
That’s also why I keep pushing on the voter rolls.
Keeping Nevada’s data in Nevada’s hands only works if Nevada does its own list maintenance, and does it well.
If we want Washington to stay out, the state has to keep its own house in order.
Here’s where we differ.
New software isn’t the same as a working system Much of the Secretary’s record is new technology.
Technology matters, but I spent more than 20 years as a computer systems analyst , and the lesson I wrote in my answer is the one I’d bring on day one: if a process is poorly designed, automating it just produces an automated mess.
A new platform is a starting point.
The question voters should ask is whether the data inside it is accurate, and whether they can check it for themselves .
The voter rolls are missing from his answer Secretary Aguilar’s answer doesn’t mention voter-roll maintenance , Voter ID , or Question 7 , which is on your ballot right now.
Mine addresses all three, because that’s where public confidence is won or lost.
When I found 9,607 Nevadans with duplicate active voter registrations, I reported them to the Secretary of State’s office.
The office closed my complaint without disputing the data.
I’m not alleging fraud.
I’m asking for an audit and a fix .
Transparency you can check The Secretary lists transparency as a core value.
So do I .
The difference shows when a citizen tests it.
I didn’t get an explanation.
I got a closed file.
So I filed Barnhill v.
Aguilar in federal court.
Since then, the office has asked the court to put my case on hold behind a lawsuit brought by a national party committee.
I publish every filing and development on this site as it happens , so you can judge for yourself.
A bigger office needs bigger checks For a second term, Secretary Aguilar proposes creating a bank inside the office to process business card payments, and he’s seeking power to prosecute fraud directly.
Lower fees for small businesses and fewer fraud victims are worthy goals.
But both proposals expand the authority of the same office that runs our elections.
Before Nevada adds a bank and prosecutors to it, voters deserve answers: Who audits the bank, and who is liable if something goes wrong?
Who oversees decisions about whom to prosecute?
What will it cost, and how does it fit with the Attorney General and county district attorneys, who already prosecute fraud?
Those are fair questions for any candidate who wants more power, and I’d expect voters to ask them of me.
The short version Secretary Aguilar wrote that Nevadans shouldn’t need a law degree to start a business.
I agree.
They also shouldn’t need a federal lawsuit to get an answer from their Secretary of State.
I’ve written down the standard I’d hold my own office to , before a single vote is cast.
Read both of our answers, then decide who’s showing you the work.
