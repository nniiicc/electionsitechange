They should be able to see a process that checks the work, documents the results, and explains any difference plainly.
Nevada election audits are one of the best ways to provide that confidence, provided they are built around reliable records, consistent standards, and public accountability.
Nevadans do not all vote the same way or live in the same kind of community.
A voter in Las Vegas may cast a ballot under different local conditions than a voter in Ely or Elko.
But every lawful voter deserves the same basic answer: your ballot was handled securely, counted accurately, and checked after the count.
That is not a partisan demand.
It is a public duty.
Nevada election audits: check the work, show the proof, and explain it plainly.
An audit is a check, not a political argument An election audit is a structured review after ballots have been counted.
Its purpose is to compare the reported results with trustworthy evidence, especially voter-marked paper ballots or other verifiable paper records.
A good audit does not begin by assuming the outcome was wrong.
It begins with a simple question: does the evidence support the reported result?
That distinction matters.
An audit is not the same as a recount, although a recount can be appropriate under certain circumstances.
A recount generally counts ballots again.
An audit examines whether the election system, procedures, and reported totals can be independently verified.
It can identify a problem in a process even when the final winner does not change.
Nor is an audit a substitute for the canvass, where officials certify and review election returns under established procedures.
Each safeguard serves a different purpose.
Elections earn confidence when those safeguards work together instead of being treated as competing ideas.
For voters, the standard should be easy to understand: the count should be accurate, and the proof should be available.
Nevada election audits should start with paper records Electronic equipment can help elections run efficiently, but electronics alone should never be the final proof of an election result.
Systems can fail, software can contain errors, and cybersecurity threats are real.
The strongest defense is a voter-verifiable paper record that can be securely stored and reviewed.
That is why paper records for Nevada elections are a core part of my platform.
That paper record gives an audit something concrete to examine.
When an audit team compares a meaningful sample of paper ballots to the reported totals, it can determine whether the tabulation matched what voters marked.
If the sample reveals discrepancies beyond an acceptable threshold, the review should expand according to clear rules.
A risk-limiting audit is one approach worth serious consideration.
Rather than checking a fixed number of ballots regardless of the margin, it uses statistical methods to determine how much evidence is needed to confirm the outcome.
A race decided by a wide margin may require a smaller sample.
A close race may require a larger review, potentially reaching a full hand count if the evidence does not support the reported result.
The point is not to burden county election teams with unnecessary work.
The point is to direct the work where it provides the most assurance.
Nevada can use modern methods while keeping the process understandable to the public.
The rules must be clear before ballots are cast Confidence is weakened when people learn the audit rules only after results are known.
The standards should be published well before Election Day: which contests will be audited, how samples are selected, who may observe, what records are examined, and what happens when a discrepancy is found.
Random selection must be genuinely random and documented in a way ordinary citizens can follow.
Observers from different political backgrounds, nonpartisan voters, and members of the public should have meaningful opportunities to watch within reasonable security and space limits.
Transparency does not mean placing ballots or voters’ private information at risk.
It means showing the public how the verification process works.
The Secretary of State’s office can lead by setting statewide standards, training county staff, and publishing plain-language procedures.
County election officials bring valuable local knowledge, especially in rural areas where staffing, travel distances, and facilities can look very different from those in Clark or Washoe County.
Statewide standards should create consistency without pretending every county faces identical operational conditions.
I explain this balance in my plan for consistent election standards in every county .
That is the practical balance Nevada needs: one standard of accountability, with responsible flexibility in how counties meet it.
What public reporting should show An audit report should answer questions voters actually have.
It should identify the contest reviewed, the audit method used, the number of ballots examined, the comparison between the audit and reported totals, and the resolution of any discrepancies.
It should also explain the timeline and identify any procedural improvements needed before the next election.
Reports should be written for citizens, not only specialists.
Technical documentation has its place, but voters should not need a computer science degree or a law license to understand whether an audit confirmed the result.
Quarterly public updates can also help build confidence before Election Day.
The public deserves to know whether election technology is being tested, whether staff training is on schedule, and whether counties have clear procedures for preserving records.
Good administration is not mysterious.
It is disciplined work made visible.
Security and access belong in the same plan Election security works best when it respects lawful voters.
Nevada should protect ballot custody, maintain accurate voter rolls, test equipment, defend systems against cyber threats, and preserve auditable paper records.
It should also make sure that seniors, students, military families, voters with disabilities, and rural residents can understand the rules and use the voting options available to them.
My election cybersecurity plan covers the security side in detail.
These goals are not in conflict.
A voter ID policy, for example, should include practical ways for eligible voters to obtain the required identification and clear voter education long before a deadline.
Audit procedures should protect ballot secrecy while still allowing public observation.
Technology should make recordkeeping more accurate, not make voters feel shut out by a process they cannot see or understand.
Election officials should not be asked to improvise these safeguards at the last minute.
They need training, tested procedures, secure facilities, and enough time to complete required work correctly.
That requires planning and honest communication from state leadership.
It also requires accurate voter rolls .
Clean records reduce confusion, prevent ballots from being sent to outdated addresses, and help counties focus their resources where they are needed.
List maintenance must be careful and lawful, with appropriate notice and protections so eligible voters are not wrongly removed.
See my approach to voter roll maintenance for more.
Reliable audits depend on accurate, well-documented records.
Independent review strengthens public trust No single office should expect the public to accept its work without verification.
Audits should include clear chains of custody, bipartisan review where applicable, qualified nonpartisan observers, and documented procedures that can be examined after the fact.
Independence is not about excluding anyone.
It is about making sure no one is asked to simply take the government’s word for it.
I’m Brad Lee Barnhill, the Independent American Party of Nevada candidate for Secretary of State, and my approach is straightforward: run elections so well that voters have records, procedures, and public results to examine.
That means results, not noise.
It means respecting the professionals who administer elections while insisting that every system be accountable to the citizens it serves.
The strongest election system is not one that demands trust.
It is one that earns it, ballot by ballot, record by record, and audit by audit.
A standard Nevada voters can see The best time to prepare for a disputed result is before the first ballot is cast.
Nevada can set clear audit standards, maintain paper evidence, train county teams, publish results, and correct weaknesses between elections.
Those are measurable responsibilities, not campaign slogans.
You can see how this fits with the rest of my agenda in the 20/20 plan , and in my look at secure ballot counting in Nevada .
Voters do not need to agree on every issue to agree on this: every lawful ballot deserves careful handling, and every election result deserves proof the public can see.
When Nevada election audits are transparent, consistent, and grounded in real records, citizens have a better reason to trust the answer when the counting is done.
