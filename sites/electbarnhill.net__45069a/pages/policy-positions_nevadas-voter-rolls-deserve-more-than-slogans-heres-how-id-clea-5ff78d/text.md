That’s the wrong fight.
The real question is whether Nevada is doing everything it already can to keep its own rolls accurate — without waiting on anyone in D.C. to tell us how.
Where I agree with the current approach: Nevada shouldn’t be forced to hand its voter data over to federal agencies on demand.
That’s a state responsibility, and I’d defend it the same way.
Where it falls short: Nevada can use federal data — like Social Security and USPS change-of-address records — without handing our data to anyone.
Most states already cross-check some of this.
The question voters should be asking is how thoroughly, and how fast.
Here’s what I’d add as Secretary of State: Property record cross-checks.
If a mail ballot is going to an address that isn’t a residence — a vacant lot, a commercial building, a parcel that doesn’t exist — that’s worth a look.
Not an automatic removal.
A flag, a notice to the voter, and a chance to confirm or correct it, exactly as federal law requires — alongside a clean rollout of Voter ID once Question 7 passes .
Cluster detection.
A handful of registrations at one address might be a big family or a group home.
Dozens at one address is a pattern worth someone’s attention.
Public numbers.
How many flags.
How many resolved.
How many referred for further review.
Nevadans shouldn’t have to take anyone’s word for it — the data should be published.
None of this means removing anyone without due process.
It means Nevada doing its own homework instead of making this a partisan standoff with Washington — which is where the current officeholder, who also chairs the Democratic Association of Secretaries of State, keeps steering it.
Nevada can run clean elections without outsourcing the job to the feds and without pretending there’s nothing left to check.
That’s the independent, common-sense standard I’d hold this office to.
