Secure ballot counting in Nevada that voters can see and understand begins with a simple standard: every lawful ballot is handled carefully, counted accurately, protected from interference, and backed by records that can be checked.
That standard is not partisan.
Democrats, Republicans, independents, and nonpartisan voters all deserve the same answer when they ask whether their vote counted: yes, and here is how the public can verify it.
The goal is not more argument after Election Day.
The goal is an election system run so well that the facts are clear.
Secure ballot counting in Nevada: paper records, trained workers, and audits voters can verify.
Security and transparency must work together Election security is sometimes discussed as if it requires the public to simply trust officials behind closed doors.
That is the wrong approach.
Security matters because it protects the ballot.
Transparency matters because it allows voters, candidates, observers, and local communities to see that the rules were followed.
A secure count protects ballots from the time they are received through verification, tabulation, audits, storage, and final certification.
A transparent count provides lawful observation opportunities, clear procedures, accurate public updates, and understandable records.
Neither one replaces the other.
Nevada’s counties have different populations, distances, staffing needs, and facilities.
What works in Clark County may need practical adjustment in Elko, Nye, or White Pine County.
But the core standards should be the same statewide: documented chain of custody, trained election workers, secure equipment, paper records, and public reporting that does not leave voters guessing.
I explain how that works in my plan for consistent election standards in every county .
Paper records give voters something real to verify Technology can help election offices process ballots efficiently, but no voter should have to place blind faith in a machine.
A voter-marked paper ballot, or a paper record a voter can review before casting, creates a physical record of voter intent.
That record is essential when election officials conduct audits or resolve a close contest.
Paper does not mean abandoning modern equipment.
It means using technology wisely.
Tabulators can count large numbers of ballots quickly and consistently, while paper ballots provide the independent evidence needed to check the electronic total.
The strongest system uses both, which is why paper records for Nevada elections are a core part of my platform.
This is especially important for mail ballots.
Nevada voters who vote by mail deserve confidence that their ballots are received, secured, verified under state law, and included in the count.
Election offices should have clear procedures for tracking ballot movement, limiting access to authorized personnel, documenting transfers, and retaining records according to law.
A paper trail also serves voters who do not follow election policy closely.
They should not have to understand software code or election administration manuals to know there is a reliable record behind the reported result.
Secure ballot counting in Nevada requires trained people The best equipment in the world cannot compensate for unclear procedures or insufficient training.
Election workers carry serious responsibility.
They verify materials, process ballots, operate equipment, maintain logs, assist voters, and follow safeguards designed to treat every ballot fairly.
Nevada should invest in consistent, practical training for county election staff and poll workers.
Training should cover ballot handling, cybersecurity awareness, accessibility requirements, de-escalation, incident reporting, and chain-of-custody documentation.
Workers should know not only what step to take, but why the step matters.
Clear statewide standards can help counties avoid confusion while preserving the flexibility they need to serve their communities.
A rural county may have a smaller team and longer travel routes.
A larger county may process a far higher volume of ballots.
The process can account for those differences without lowering the standard of care.
Election observers also have a place in building confidence.
Lawful observation should be orderly, respectful, and meaningful.
Observers should be able to see that procedures are being followed without interfering with voter privacy or the work of election staff.
Good rules protect everyone involved.
Audits are a check, not a political talking point An audit is one of the most practical ways to confirm that reported results match the ballots cast.
It is not an accusation against election workers, and it should not be reserved for moments of controversy.
A well-designed audit is routine quality control.
Nevada should use transparent post-election audits that compare paper ballots with reported results under clear, publicly available procedures.
The public should be able to understand what was audited, how ballots or contests were selected, who participated, what was found, and what corrective action would follow if a discrepancy required it.
The details matter.
An audit must be independent enough to provide a genuine check, carefully documented enough to be repeatable, and timely enough to inform certification.
Audit methods can vary based on the contest, voting system, and legal requirements.
What should not vary is the commitment to publish results in plain language.
When people can see routine checks working, rumors have less room to grow.
Facts should arrive before speculation fills the space.
Cybersecurity belongs at the center of election planning Ballot security is not limited to the room where ballots are counted.
Election systems depend on voter registration data, county networks, tabulation equipment, vendor support, physical access controls, and communication between offices.
A weakness in any one area can disrupt an election, even if the paper ballots themselves remain safe.
The Secretary of State’s office should work with county clerks, technology specialists, and appropriate state and federal partners to strengthen cybersecurity before an election, not after a problem occurs.
That includes regular system assessments, tested backup procedures, access controls, software and equipment management, staff training, and incident-response plans.
My election cybersecurity plan lays out the details.
Public communication is part of security, too.
If an election office experiences a delay, equipment issue, or cyber incident, voters deserve prompt and factual updates about what happened, what is being done, and whether ballot counting or voter information was affected.
Silence creates uncertainty.
Clear communication protects confidence without disclosing information that could create a new security risk.
Clean voter rolls support an accurate count Accurate voter rolls are the front end of secure elections.
Counties need reliable tools to update records when voters move, die, become ineligible under the law, or need to correct outdated information.
At the same time, lawful voters must be protected from improper removal and given a fair opportunity to address errors.
Accurate voter rolls start with careful, documented record review.
That requires steady administrative work, not shortcuts.
Regular list maintenance, secure data matching, notice procedures required by law, and accessible ways for voters to confirm or update their information can reduce avoidable problems before ballots are issued.
See my approach to Nevada voter roll maintenance for more.
Voter ID is another part of a sound verification system when implemented with care.
Any rollout should protect every lawful voter, including seniors, students, rural residents, voters with disabilities, and people who need help obtaining the required documentation.
Security and access are not competing values when the rules are designed responsibly.
Nevada can verify identity while making sure eligible citizens are not left behind.
Public reporting should answer ordinary questions Election reporting often fails because it assumes every voter already knows the process.
A results page may show totals without explaining which ballots have been counted, which remain to be processed, or why updates occur at different times.
Nevada should make public reporting more useful.
Voters deserve plain explanations of what results mean, when updates will occur, which categories of ballots are included, and what steps remain before certification.
Counties should use consistent terms where possible so that a voter in Reno and a voter in Mesquite are not trying to decode two entirely different systems.
Quarterly public reporting on election readiness can also help.
Voters should be able to see progress on cybersecurity planning, equipment maintenance, training, voter outreach, and audit preparation well before Election Day.
Accountability works best when it is ongoing, not rushed into the final days of a campaign.
Results, Not Noise Secure ballot counting is not built by speeches after the fact.
It is built by patient preparation: accurate rolls, voter verification, paper records, trained workers, secure facilities, tested technology, transparent audits, and honest public communication.
I’m Brad Lee Barnhill, the Independent American Party of Nevada candidate for Secretary of State, and my approach is straightforward.
After 20 years as a computer systems analyst and 20 years as a Nevada litigation paralegal, I know that confidence comes from systems that can be tested, documented, and explained.
The Secretary of State’s office should set high standards, support county election officials, and answer to Nevada voters rather than political pressure.
Every lawful Nevada voter deserves to cast a ballot with confidence and see a count worthy of that trust.
The work is detailed, sometimes technical, and never glamorous.
That is exactly why it deserves steady leadership focused on getting it right.
