It earns trust one conversation, one honest answer, and one kept commitment at a time.
If you’ve ever wanted to do more than complain about the choices on your ballot, volunteering for a Nevada political campaign is one of the most direct ways to do it.
I’m Brad Lee Barnhill, and I’m running for Nevada Secretary of State as the Independent American Party of Nevada candidate and #YourIndependentVoice.
I spent 20 years as a Nevada litigation paralegal and 20 years as a computer systems analyst, and you can read more about my background here .
I solve problems for a living, and this campaign runs on neighbors talking to neighbors about how to fix them.
Why campaign volunteers still matter Nevada voters have heard plenty of promises.
What many want now is a plain explanation of how the Secretary of State’s office would work better: how records are maintained, how audits are reported, how counties get support, and how voters who face barriers are included.
A volunteer turns that explanation into a conversation.
A neighbor may never read a policy page or follow an election-administration debate.
They might, however, talk with a person at their door, pick up a handout at a community event, or ask a question after seeing a yard sign.
Your calm, informed presence gives them a reason to listen.
You don’t need to be a political expert.
You need honesty, a little preparation, and the willingness to say, “I don’t know, but I’ll find out.” Good volunteers don’t argue people into agreement.
They make sure voters have the information to decide for themselves.
How to volunteer for a Nevada political campaign Start with the work that fits your time, strengths, and comfort level.
Campaigns need people who enjoy meeting strangers, and they need organized people who prefer working behind the scenes.
A few focused hours are valuable, especially when you show up reliably.
Choose work you can do well Field outreach is the most visible role.
Volunteers talk with voters at their doors, help at community events, hand out campaign information, and invite supporters to upcoming activities.
The purpose isn’t pressure.
It’s to share my priorities and listen to what Nevadans expect from the Secretary of State.
Phone outreach suits people who can’t travel or have limited availability.
These calls help us answer basic questions and remind voters about events and election deadlines.
A respectful call is especially useful for voters who want information but not a long political exchange.
There’s also essential work away from the doorstep: organizing materials, preparing event spaces, entering data carefully, placing yard signs where the property owner and local rules allow, and helping with voter education.
Details matter in public service and in campaigns, and accurate work makes the whole team dependable.
Your own experience counts, too.
A retired teacher can explain a complicated issue plainly.
A small-business owner understands why accurate public records matter.
A technology professional knows what secure, auditable systems require.
A veteran or military family member brings discipline and a sense of service.
Every useful skill has a place.
Learn the message before sharing it Volunteers should be able to explain this campaign in plain language.
I’m a conservative independent who isn’t running to answer to party leadership.
I’m running to answer to Nevada’s citizens, and the 20/20 plan shows what I’d do with the office.
The pillars are straightforward: implementing voter ID in a way that works for the voter, careful voter-roll maintenance , paper records , a strong cybersecurity plan , and consistent standards in every county .
Clark County, Washoe County, and our rural counties operate at different scales, but lawful voters in each deserve the same secure procedures and the same confidence in the count.
Before an outreach shift, review these positions and ask for a short briefing.
Be specific about what the Secretary of State can do under existing authority, and don’t overstate it.
Credibility comes from knowing the limits of the office as well as its potential.
Keep conversations civil and useful Political volunteering can test your patience.
Some voters will agree right away, some will disagree strongly, and many will simply be busy.
The right response is the same each time: be courteous, respect their time, and leave the door open.
Ask a question before you give a speech.
What gives them confidence in elections?
What do they think needs more attention from state government?
Listen, then connect their concern to a policy we support if there’s a real connection.
If there isn’t, don’t force one.
Don’t repeat claims you can’t support.
Don’t speculate about election outcomes, criticize election workers, or promise what no candidate can guarantee.
A campaign that asks voters to expect accountability has to hold itself to that standard in every conversation.
The discipline behind a strong volunteer effort Campaign work isn’t only enthusiasm.
It’s follow-through.
Arrive when you say you will.
Use the materials and talking points provided.
Record voter feedback accurately when asked.
Protect personal information.
Follow the rules for signs, events, and private property.
That discipline matters most in a race about election administration.
Nevada needs public officials who communicate clearly and handle details responsibly, and volunteers show what that standard looks like long before Election Day.
It’s the same reason I filed Barnhill v.
Aguilar : accurate records and open reporting are worth the hard work.
Set realistic expectations, too.
Not every conversation changes a mind, and not every event produces an immediate commitment.
Sometimes the best result is that a voter learns there’s a candidate focused on election security and ballot access who won’t turn every issue into a partisan fight.
Trust grows through repetition and consistency.
Results, Not Noise The best volunteer schedule is the one you can keep.
A steady two-hour shift each week does more good than an ambitious plan that falls apart in a month.
Tell us when you’re available, what work you prefer, and whether you can help in person, remotely, or both.
If you bring friends or family along, share the same standard: be informed, be respectful, and focus on the voter in front of you.
Campaigning should strengthen civic participation, not add to the noise people are already tired of hearing.
Independence isn’t a wasted vote.
It’s a vote no party owns, and it’s how you can stop being unrepresented .
I’ve put my plans in writing and taken the fight for clean voter rolls to federal court.
Is your candidate for Nevada Secretary of State fighting this hard to protect your vote?
If not, it’s time to take a closer look.
