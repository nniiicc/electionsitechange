Nicole Uhre-Balk FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE Wednesday, September 24th, 2025 I could think of 650 million things I would rather spend money on than a $650 million prison.
My “yes” vote was one of the hardest decisions I’ve made, and I wavered back and forth.
But leadership sometimes means choosing between bad options—and leaving the current State Penitentiary as it is simply isn’t one of them.
Knowing that the conditions are inhumane for both workers and prisoners, and that our state government is now showing greater focus on rehabilitation and prevention, I was able to move forward with a yes vote.
A building alone will not solve our prison crisis.
For years, Democrats have brought forward bills focused on programming, prevention, and community-based diversion.
This year, the Joint Democratic Caucus, in collaboration with Governor Rhoden, secured good-faith commitments to support programs and legislation that keep South Dakotans out of prison in the first place or help with re-entry.
That, coupled with the Incarceration Rehabilitation Task Force and testimony from both sides of the aisle that true reform must be our priority, gives us a small but important opening to begin changing the system that feeds mass incarceration.
The Hill, our 1881 facility in Sioux Falls, is unsafe for staff and inhumane for those incarcerated.
Cells built for one person now routinely house two or three.
Plumbing breaks down, smoke and fumes drift through failing ventilation systems, the building is not ADA compliant, and basic safety standards are constantly compromised.
Some have compared the size of a cell to the space a dog gets at the humane society.
That is not a standard South Dakotans should accept.
Importantly, this project does not raise taxes.
The funds have already been set aside in the Incarceration Construction Fund, with interest earnings and a one-time transfer from reserves covering the total cost.
The new facility will not only replace failing infrastructure but will also create space for rehabilitation and programming that simply doesn’t exist now.
Education, addiction recovery, and vocational training areas will more than double compared to the current penitentiary.
The new number of beds is intended to replace the old prison and is not much of an increase; the design is about better use of space—ending three people in a room designed for one, improving safety, and providing more classrooms for treatment and training that can reduce recidivism.
My “yes” vote is not the end of this work, it is the beginning.
