Tuesday January 20th, 2026
Starting my second year in the South Dakota Legislature is a genuine honor.
Leaving family, friends, and the Black Hills to head east on I-90 is never easy, but arriving in Pierre brings a familiar rhythm.
There is a sense of reunion, colleagues reconnecting, offices being unpacked, and the work settling in.
This year, unusually warm and beautiful weather added to that feeling, making the start of the session feel almost like a return to summer camp, albeit one with serious responsibilities.
We are four legislative days into the 2026 session, and the pace has already shifted from ceremony to substance.
From a public perspective, Week One leaned heavily toward tradition and orientation.
The House spent significant time on the floor hearing addresses from Governor Larry Rhoden, Chief Justice Steven Jensen, and Kathleen Wooden Knife.
We also completed the foundational work that allows the Legislature to function effectively, including adopting rules, finalizing committee assignments, and swearing in staff and pages.
What moved forward
Returning to work alongside colleagues I have come to respect has reinforced an important truth about this institution.
Most legislators are here with a sincere commitment to public service and a deep love for South Dakota.
Disagreement is part of the process, but shared responsibility for the state’s future is a unifying force.
In the House Education Committee, after resolving an unexpectedly spirited debate over seating arrangements, which felt somewhat ironic in that setting, we heard HB 1017.
The bill attempts to address classroom behavior challenges.
While the proposal raised substantial concerns and lacked key support, the committee ultimately voted to defer it to a later date.
That decision preserves space for continued dialogue.
Addressing learning environments matters, but long-term improvement requires stronger student mental health supports alongside clear, practical guidance for educators.
I encourage constituents to share ideas on how this conversation can move in a more constructive direction.
What raised concerns
This week also revealed moments that fell short of the collaborative standard we should expect.
A long-developed resolution honoring the Declaration of Independence was amended on the floor to remove references to Indigenous South Dakotans and to strike the word “democracy.” These changes were justified through narrow interpretations of language but had the effect of excluding communities and ideas central to our shared civic history.
Governor Rhoden’s State of the State included a call to recommit to civility.
That message is timely.
Productive debate is essential, but it should never undermine mutual respect.
As the session progresses into more substantive policy debates, I hope attention shifts away from symbolic disputes and toward solutions that tangibly improve life for South Dakotans.
What’s coming next
Looking ahead, the Senate will soon hear Senator Rohl’s SB 7, which I am priming on the House side, which is a proposal to update South Dakota’s gold tax framework.
This bill has implications for fairness and long-term state revenue for water infrastructure projects that deserves close attention.
The House is also expected to consider HCR 6003, a resolution I am bringing to recognize conservation and restoration practices such as Post-Assisted Log Structures (PALS) and Beaver Dam Analogues (BDAs) as effective tools for addressing water shortages and mitigating wildfire risk in the Black Hills.
The resolution highlights locally driven, low-cost strategies that strengthen water resilience and reduce future infrastructure costs.
Additionally, I will be sharing the Living Donor Protection Act as soon as it is formally released.
This legislation protects living organ donors from insurance discrimination, encourages life-saving donations, and carries no cost to taxpayers.
South Dakota currently lacks these protections, despite rising rates of kidney failure and strong bipartisan support for similar laws in more than 35 states.
How constituents can engage
Legislative outcomes are shaped by public input.
Emails, calls, and respectful conversations with legislators make a measurable difference, particularly early in the process.
If a legislator has already signed on as a co-sponsor of a bill you support, taking a moment to say thank you reinforces that engagement matters.
I remain committed to keeping District 32, and anyone else who will read the column, informed as the session unfolds.
The work ahead will be demanding, but it is also an opportunity to focus on policies that strengthen education, protect our natural resources, and reflect the values of service that bring us together each year in Pierre.
To close, I want to emphasize that democracy is a team sport, strengthened when people stay informed, ask questions, and engage with the process alongside one another.
