Jul 30 Written By Forest B Rep.
Nicole Uhre-Balk for Re-election to SD House of Representatives District 32 As the banners come down and the 250th anniversary celebrations conclude, I keep thinking less about 1776 and more about 2076.
What will South Dakota look like when our country celebrates its 300th anniversary?
What progress will people celebrate?
What problems will they be surprised we were still struggling to solve in 2026?
The 250th anniversary invites us to reflect on both the ideals declared in 1776 and the people excluded from those promises.
We can recognize democracy, liberty, and self-government while also being honest about colonization, broken treaties, slavery, and generations of injustice.
Holding both truths is not about diminishing our country.
It is how we learn from the past and make better choices about the South Dakota we want people to inherit in 2076.
By the time our country turns 300, I want South Dakotans to have moved beyond debating whether children deserve safe, modern schools, whether communities can fund education without overwhelming property taxpayers, whether clean water should be protected before it is threatened, whether mining companies should be responsible for the impacts they create, or whether acknowledging the full history of our country and tribal nations makes us less patriotic.
I want those questions settled because we chose to do the difficult work now.
Across South Dakota, school districts are struggling with aging buildings and rising construction costs.
When a school needs major repairs or replacement, much of the burden falls on local property taxpayers.
Communities are left to delay needed work, raise taxes, or take on decades of interest payments.
Last session, I introduced legislation to create a zero percent revolving loan fund for school construction.
It would allow districts to repay construction costs without sending millions of additional dollars to banks in interest.
The proposal earned bipartisan support in committee because it offered a practical way to strengthen schools while reducing pressure on taxpayers.
The bill did not make it across the finish line, but the need has not disappeared.
I plan to bring the idea back.
South Dakota should be able to support strong schools and responsible property tax policy at the same time.
I also want the South Dakotans celebrating in 2076 to inherit a Black Hills region with clean and dependable water.
The decisions we make today about mining, data centers, development, industry, conservation, and groundwater use may affect our communities for generations.
Responsible development is possible, but it requires laws that match modern projects.
That could mean stronger reclamation bonds so taxpayers are not left with cleanup costs and an accounting of water use before projects are approved.
Protecting the Black Hills does not mean opposing every project.
It means setting fair expectations before problems occur and making sure taxpayers and future generations are not left responsible for the damage.
There was progress during the last legislative session.
We passed protections for living organ donors, improved clemency notification for survivors, helped small lodging businesses, and supported watershed restoration.
These practical changes came from listening to people, identifying problems, and finding workable solutions.
I want to pass more legislation like that.
Even in a Legislature where Democrats are outnumbered, good ideas can earn support when they are practical, carefully built, and rooted in people’s lives.
We should not have to choose between pride in our country and honesty about its history, economic development and clean water, or strong schools and property tax relief.
Democracy is a team sport.
It depends on people showing up, listening to one another, sharing ideas, and staying involved even when there is not an election right around the corner.
That is why gatherings like the upcoming Souper Tuesday matter.
Sharing food creates space for stories, concerns, and ideas about the policies shaping our communities.
Those conversations help us understand one another and remind us that lasting change is built through relationships, not just speeches or votes.
At a time when politics can feel increasingly divided and distant, coming together is more important than ever.
The South Dakota that people inherit in 2076 will be shaped not only by what happens in the Capitol, but by the people who keep participating, asking questions, and working together to build something better.
