Borders Under Siege: Spain’s Uprising and America’s Divide
Everyone has seen the images by now, the beach camps, the torn fencing, the crush of bodies pouring into Ceuta in late July.
More than 70,000 migrants surged across the border from Morocco in a matter of hours, overwhelming Spain’s North African enclave and triggering days of chaos.
Police were outnumbered and neighborhoods were transformed overnight.
Last night, Spain responded.
Across the country, tens of thousands of Spaniards poured into the streets.
In Madrid alone, 50,000 people marched, waving Spanish flags and chanting for accountability.
Some moved toward migrant camps, tearing down makeshift shelters and setting fires.
Others clashed with police.
Gunshots echoed through Ceuta.
Explosions lit the night sky.
Spain isn’t the only country that has lived through this.
Here in the United States, we experienced the chaos of open borders during the Biden administration and are still dealing with the fallout.
Millions of people poured into the country unvetted.
Drug cartels trafficked fentanyl and humans across the southern border.
Cities and states were overwhelmed.
In California, the consequences were impossible to ignore.
Housing costs skyrocketed as scarcity worsened.
Wages dropped as a flood of new workers entered the labor market, often taking jobs that once went to Americans.
Healthcare systems buckled, forced to absorb the cost of treating large numbers of people who arrived with no insurance and no ability to pay.
Communities fractured, politically and socially, as the strain deepened divisions across the state and the nation.
It's time to confront this head one and ask the tough questions:
Does a country have the right to its own sovereignty?
If tens of thousands can cross a border in a single night, what does sovereignty even mean?
Is a nation still a nation if it cannot enforce its own territorial limits?
Does a country have an obligation to take in foreigners?
Compassion matters but is it limitless?
Can a nation survive if it absorbs more people than it can house, employ, or integrate?
Should migrants be expected to assimilate?
If newcomers do not adopt the language, values, and cultural norms of the host country, how can social cohesion exist?
How can unity survive if parallel societies form?
And the hardest question of all, why is the burden always placed on the receiving country instead of asking why migrants do not stay and fight to make their own nations livable?
Is it humane to encourage mass departure from struggling countries, draining them of the very people who might rebuild them?
And finally, If a country cannot control who enters, can it truly call itself a country at all?
