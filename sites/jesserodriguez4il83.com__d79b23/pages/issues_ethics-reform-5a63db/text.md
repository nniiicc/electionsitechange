Meet Jesse Issues News Volunteer Contribute Home ❭ Issues ❭ Ethics Reform Ethics Reform The Problem: A Culture That Protects Politicians Instead of Taxpayers For generations, Illinois has been synonymous with public corruption.
Governors, legislators, lobbyists and political insiders have abused positions of public trust while taxpayers paid the price.
Recent events show that the culture has not changed: State Representative Harry Benton resigned on July 3 following a Legislative Inspector General investigation into sexual harassment-related allegations.
House Speaker Chris Welch said the investigation uncovered conduct that was “outrageous, unethical, and unbecoming,” but the investigative report still has not been released publicly.
State Representative Carol Ammons was indicted on 10 federal charges, including wire fraud, making a false statement to the FBI and conspiracy to obstruct justice.
Prosecutors allege a scheme involving campaign money, kickbacks and state grant funding.
Ammons has denied wrongdoing and is presumed innocent unless proven guilty.
The Legislative Ethics Commission, made up entirely of lawmakers appointed by legislative leaders, can block the release of many ethics reports.
The commission has not released such a report since 2020.
Public Office Is a Public Trust As a Navy veteran, Aurora small-business owner and candidate for the 83rd House District, Jesse Rodriguez believes elected officials should be held to the same standards of honesty, accountability and personal responsibility expected of every Illinois family and business.
1.
Empower the Legislative Inspector General (House Bill 1382) Allow the Legislative Inspector General to issue subpoenas without first obtaining permission from the Legislative Ethics Commission, whose members are appointed by legislative leaders.
Require completed investigative reports and official responses to be released publicly within 60 days, with appropriate protections for victims, witnesses and confidential information.
2.
Stop Politicians from Using Campaign Funds as Legal Defense Funds (House Bill 1554) Prohibit campaign committees from paying legal expenses arising from criminal charges connected to public misconduct or civil claims involving sexual harassment, discrimination or abuse of public office.
Politicians accused of personal wrongdoing should pay their own legal bills, not use money contributed by supporters.
3.
Make Corrupt Politicians Pay a Price (House Bill 1727) Suspend pension payments when a public official is charged with a felony connected to public service.
Payments would be restored with interest if the official is not convicted, but permanently withheld following a conviction.
4.
Close the Politician-to-Lobbyist Revolving Door (House Bills 1385 and 3115) Establish a three-year cooling-off period before legislators and other senior state officials may become lobbyists.
Public officials should not be able to leave office and immediately cash in on their government contacts and influence.
5.
Require Disclosure and Recusal for Conflicts of Interest (House Bill 3121) Require legislators to formally recuse themselves from matters in which they, their spouses or immediate family members have a financial interest.
The recusal must include a written public explanation.
The bill would also strengthen restrictions on lawmakers representing private interests against state or local taxpayers.
6.
Strengthen Lobbying Transparency at Every Level (House Bill 1384) Expand lobbying registration and disclosure requirements to cover efforts to influence officials and decisions within units of local government.
The public deserves to know who is being paid to influence government decisions, whether those decisions are made in Springfield or closer to home.
7.
End Middle-of-the-Night Budget Deals (House Bill 2813) Require appropriation and revenue legislation to be publicly available for at least 72 hours before a hearing or vote.
Amendments would also be subject to public-review periods, preventing legislative leaders from dropping massive budgets in the middle of the night and demanding an immediate vote.
The Bottom Line Illinois does not need another task force, another carefully worded statement or another promise that politicians will police themselves.
