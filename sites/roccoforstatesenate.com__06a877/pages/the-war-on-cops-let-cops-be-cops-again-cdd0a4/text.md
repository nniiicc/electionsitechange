Defund was not a slogan.
It was a plan.
On June 7, 2020, nine of thirteen Minneapolis City Council members stood in Powderhorn Park and pledged to dismantle the Minneapolis Police Department.
That was a veto-proof majority.
The follow-through was a charter amendment to replace MPD with a “Department of Community Safety and Violence Prevention.” The Charter Commission blocked it from the 2020 ballot.
A later version went to voters in 2021 as Question 2.
Voters rejected it.
The intent was not in doubt.
When dismantling failed, the council still moved money.
In December 2020 it shifted about $8 million from the police budget to violence-prevention and mental-health programs and parked more hiring money where the council could control the tap.
Attrition did the rest.
The department emptied.
Before May 2020, Minneapolis had roughly 880 to 920 sworn officers.
The force fell into the mid-500s — the lowest in decades — and stayed under the city charter’s minimum for years.
Overtime exploded because there were not enough officers left.
Statewide, union leaders have described a shortage measured in the thousands.
That is what “defund” looks like after the cameras leave.
Democrats later claimed “defund the police” was a Republican talking point.

The crime numbers they do not want next to “defund.”
Minnesota’s murder count jumped from 117 in 2019 to 185 in 2020 and 201 in 2021.

The 2025 BCA report shows a drop to 132 murders — better than the peak, still above 2019.
Minneapolis had 48 murders in 2019 and 62 in 2025.
That is not “back to normal.” It is a city that never fully climbed out.

Hennepin and Ramsey counties hold about a third of the state’s population and have accounted for the large majority of Minnesota murders in recent years.

The charging desk is part of the war.
Minnesota does not have a statewide “district attorney.” The people who decide whether an arrest becomes a case are county attorneys.
In Hennepin County, that is Mary Moriarty.
Her office’s own description of carjacking work is the problem in one paragraph.
For a 12-month window she cited, agencies reported 349 carjacking incidents.
Police submitted about 105 cases.
Her office charged 63 — and only 13 of those 63 as carjacking.
The rest were pleaded down or charged as something else.
That is how “we charged it” becomes a press line while the street still calls it a carjacking.
In 2025 she announced Hennepin County would stop charging most felony cases that start with a low-level traffic stop and would generally refuse cases built on consent searches.
That is a standing order to cops: do not find the gun, the dope, or the fugitive on a tail light, because we will not take the case.
On juveniles, independent review of her charging data found her office charging youth cases at a lower rate than her predecessor and using more pre-charge diversion — while Hennepin juvenile person crimes ran far above the rest of the state.
