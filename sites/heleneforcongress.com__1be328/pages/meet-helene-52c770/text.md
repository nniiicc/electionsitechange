Mine answers a much more important question: "Why am I this way?" I was five years old when I hid beneath our dining room table with seven mismatched chairs.
A man wearing a black fedora and suit, carrying a briefcase, sat across from my mother, trying to persuade her to place her six children in an orphanage.
My father had left.
My youngest sibling was only two months old.
Hidden beneath the long tablecloth, I listened in silence, terrified.
My mother smiled politely.
Then she quietly said, "No." She never considered giving us away.
That quiet moment became the foundation of my life.
She lost her Irish immigrant parents and siblings early in life, and at just 31 years old, she was left to raise six children alone.
Our father left when I was five and never returned, leaving my mother responsible for children ages eight, seven, six, five, four, and a newborn just two months old.
My late, beautiful Irish mother was my first and greatest hero.
My Earliest Memory Has Never Left Me For many years, I carried a quiet sense of shame about growing up without a father.
In 1965, it was taboo to be from a divorced family; we were often viewed differently.
Over time, that shame gave way to gratitude.
I no longer define my childhood by what was missing, but by the extraordinary strength of the woman who refused to let our circumstances define our future.
With no one else to depend on, my mother worked three jobs while attending Saint Joseph's University in Philadelphia, eventually becoming a nursing home administrator and accountant.
She did whatever it took to keep our family together, providing food, shelter, and a Catholic education.
She was a master at bartering.
In exchange for tuition, she kept the church's books.
When I broke my arm, she prepared the emergency room physician's tax returns in exchange for my cast.
She stretched every dollar, protected her family, and somehow always found a way.
My love of distance running began in sixth grade.
During the summers, I ran 1.7 miles each way to the nursing home where my mother worked.
My youngest siblings attended nursery school, and my older siblings worked at the nursing home, leaving me home alone at age 11.
I simply wanted to see my mom, so I ran.
I remember watching my mother stand in the doorway at my seventh and eighth basketball games because she couldn't afford the one-dollar admission fee.
After long days managing the nursing home or busing tables at the country club on weekends, where she had once been a member, she would come home exhausted.
My siblings and I would soak her tired feet in large pots of hot water, hoping to give her a few moments of comfort.
Even while shouldering our family's weight, she never stopped helping others.
At the nursing home, she hired people who had fallen on hard times, giving them dignity, purpose, and a second chance.
My mom even ran for county office when I was 11.
I remember her lighting matches to read the results posted inside Oakmont School's window.
She did not win, but she was thrilled to receive almost as many votes as her opponents.
Without realizing it at the time, she was teaching me lessons that would guide the rest of my life: compassion, perseverance, humility, service, and the belief that no one should ever be left behind.
A Family Dedicated to Service Service runs deep in my family.
Four of my siblings served our country—three devoted their careers to the military, while my younger sister served overseas during the Gulf War.
Their commitment to duty, sacrifice, and protecting others shaped my understanding of leadership long before I entered public service.
Everything I have done—as a coparent to two sons, as a nurse, public health professional, entrepreneur, endurance athlete, author, cancer survivor, and now candidate for Congress—can be traced back to one little girl hiding beneath a dining room table— Watching.
Listening.
Learning.
That little girl learned that courage doesn't always shout.
Sometimes courage quietly says, "No." My mother said no to giving away her children.
She said yes to sacrifice - Yes to hard work - Yes to hope.
She taught me that leadership isn't about titles.
Leadership is showing up.
It protects those who are vulnerable.
It is treating every person with dignity.
It is believing that no one is beyond compassion.
That lesson guided me through every patient I've cared for...every mile I've run...every obstacle I've overcome...and every decision I've made.
It's not cancer.
It's not running.
It's not politics.
It's this: Compassion cannot be conditional.
It is also why I am asking for the privilege of serving the people of North Dakota.
Why Not Me?
