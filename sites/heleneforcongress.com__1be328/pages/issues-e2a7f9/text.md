Read More Data Centers “Major energy and technology developments — including data centers and artificial intelligence infrastructure — must be planned transparently and responsibly so they do not overwhelm local resources or undermine agricultural land and rural communities.” ~ Helene Neville Data Centers, Artificial Intelligence, and Responsible Development Artificial intelligence is transforming the world, and data centers are becoming a critical part of America's technological infrastructure.
A data center is a specialized facility that houses computing infrastructure used to store, process, and manage enormous amounts of digital information.
They support everything from cloud computing and online services to artificial intelligence applications.
The growth of artificial intelligence has dramatically increased demand for new data centers across the United States.
Technology and innovation create opportunities, but major developments must be evaluated carefully before they are approved.
Communities deserve answers to important questions: • How much electricity will be required? • How much water will be consumed? • What impacts will occur to surrounding farmland and property owners? • What are the long-term environmental and public health considerations? • How will local communities' benefit? • Who is responsible if problems arise?
I support transparency, independent review, and meaningful public participation before major projects move forward.
As a Community Health Consultant and Surveillance Specialist working with the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR), I participated in environmental health investigations and community assessments.
Before major environmental evaluations were conducted, baseline information was gathered through community outreach, environmental sampling, and public health assessment.
Responsible planning begins with understanding existing conditions before development occurs.
When projects have the potential to affect communities, air quality, water resources, agricultural land, or public health, decisions should be guided by science, transparency, and community input.
I believe North Dakota can welcome innovation while also protecting the land, water, and rural communities that define our state.
Technology should serve people.
People should not be expected to sacrifice their communities for technology.
North Dakota and Data Centers North Dakota is increasingly being considered for energy-intensive technology development because of its available land, energy resources, and cooler climate.
There are currently 23 centers in North Dakota.
As discussions continue regarding future data center and artificial intelligence projects, North Dakotans deserve complete transparency regarding potential impacts on energy costs, water resources, agriculture, infrastructure, and local communities.
The question is not whether technology should advance.
The question is whether development will occur responsibly, transparently, and with the informed consent of the communities affected.
As of March 2026, there are 4,011 data centers in the United States — more than any other country by a wide margin, ahead of the UK (511), Germany (507), China (368), and France (344).
These facilities operated with a combined capacity exceeding 50 GW at the end of 2025 — a figure confirmed by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission in its March 2026 State of the Markets report — representing 24% compound annual growth since 2020 .
The US accounts for approximately 54% of all hyperscale data center capacity worldwide , a concentration that reflects both the country’s technological leadership and the strategic imperative every major cloud provider feels to build at maximum speed in the American market.
In 2025, US primary data center markets absorbed a record 2,497.6 MW of capacity — up 38% from the already-record 1,809.5 MW absorbed in 2024 — reflecting a demand engine that has not slowed in years.
This should concern us.

House Energy Costs, Household Utility Bills Energy Costs and Household Utilities "Reliable and affordable energy is not a luxury.
It is a necessity for families, seniors, farmers, and small businesses." ~ Helene Neville Across North Dakota, families are feeling the pressure of rising household costs.
For a typical North Dakota household, monthly utility expenses can easily reach $365 per month or more when electricity, natural gas, water, sewer, trash service, and internet access are combined.
That amounts to approximately $4,380 per year before a family buys groceries, fills a gas tank, pays rent, or makes a mortgage payment.
For seniors living on fixed incomes and working families already stretched by housing, healthcare, and childcare costs, these expenses add up quickly.
Heating and cooling often account for nearly half of a household's energy use, making North Dakota families particularly vulnerable to energy price increases during periods of extreme cold or heat.
North Dakota produces energy for the nation, and the people who live here should benefit from reliable and affordable power.
As new energy demands emerge — including large-scale data centers and artificial intelligence infrastructure — policymakers must ensure that residential customers, family farms, and small businesses are not forced to bear the burden of higher costs.
Energy policy should focus on: • Reliability and affordability • Responsible infrastructure investment • Consumer protection and transparency • Energy efficiency and conservation opportunities • Protecting rural communities from unintended consequences of large-scale development Families should not have to choose between keeping the lights on, filling a prescription, or buying groceries.
Affordable energy strengthens households, supports economic growth, and helps North Dakota communities thrive.
Typical monthly utility costs in North Dakota are about $187.01/mo., based on 1,000 kWh of electricity and 5,000 gallons of water (plus sewer and trash where available).

Fairness in Women's Sports Fairness in Women's Sports "Every athlete deserves dignity, respect, and a fair opportunity to compete." ~ Helene Neville As both a nurse and a lifelong athlete, I believe this issue deserves thoughtful discussion grounded in science, fairness, and respect for every individual.
I believe competitive women's sports should provide a level playing field for female athletes.
Human biology matters in athletic performance.
On average, males experience puberty with higher testosterone levels, greater muscle mass, higher bone density, larger hearts and lungs, and skeletal differences that can contribute to advantages in strength, speed, power, and endurance.
While hormone therapy can change some physiological characteristics, it does not reverse every effect of male puberty.
For that reason, I support policies that seek to preserve fairness and safety in women's competitive sports while treating every athlete with dignity and respect.
This is not about questioning anyone's identity or worth.
Every person deserves to be treated with respect and free from discrimination.
It is about ensuring that governing bodies develop evidence-based policies that balance inclusion, competitive fairness, and athlete safety.
I believe these decisions should be guided by current scientific evidence, medical expertise, and the specific demands of each sport—not by political rhetoric.
North Dakota's athletes deserve rules that are fair, transparent, and consistent for everyone who competes.

Medical Cannabis and Patient Access Medical Cannabis and Patient Access When treatment is recommended by a qualified healthcare professional, patients should not face unnecessary financial barriers to receiving it.
As a stage IV cancer survivor, I understand chronic pain from personal experience.
After discussing my symptoms with my oncologist, I was encouraged to consider medical cannabis to help manage pain and reduce some of the side effects of treatment.
Like many patients, I was hesitant at first.
My goal has never been to seek another medication.
My goal has always been to improve my quality of life.
What surprised me most was not the treatment itself—it was the cost of accessing it.
In North Dakota, patients using the medical cannabis program may face recurring expenses that include required medical evaluations to maintain certification, application and renewal costs, and the cost of the medication itself, which is generally not covered by Medicare or most private insurance plans.
For patients living with cancer, chronic illness, or severe pain, these expenses can become another financial burden during an already difficult time.
Patients should not have to choose between managing their pain and paying their bills.
I support reviewing North Dakota's medical cannabis program to reduce unnecessary barriers for patients with legitimate medical needs.
That includes evaluating certification requirements, reducing unnecessary costs, and improving access while maintaining appropriate medical oversight and safeguards.
I also believe it is time for an honest, evidence-based national conversation about cannabis policy.
Public policy should reflect current medical research, protect public safety, and prioritize the well-being of patients rather than outdated assumptions.
As a nurse, a public health professional, and a cancer survivor, I believe healthcare decisions should be guided by science, compassion, and the best interests of patients.
No one battling a serious illness should face unnecessary obstacles to obtaining legally recommended treatment that may improve their quality of life.
Rural Broadband Rural broadband is a legitimate topic, especially in North Dakota .
But there is an important nuance: North Dakota is already comparatively strong in broadband infrastructure.
This is less about internet connectivity and more about closing remaining gaps, improving affordability and reliability, and what broadband enables in rural communities.
North Dakota Information Technology reports that the state is on track for full broadband coverage and that more than 90% of residents have access to at least 100 Mbps.
The current federal BEAD final proposal identifies only 279 eligible broadband-serviceable locations, with 277 slated for fiber-to-the-premises.
Healthcare: broadband supports telehealth, remote patient monitoring, specialist access, and rural workforce extension.
North Dakota's current Rural Health Transformation effort specifically includes telehealth infrastructure and remote monitoring.
Broadband can be treated as part of rural healthcare infrastructure because telehealth does little good when the patient cannot maintain a reliable connection.
North Dakota itself identifies broadband availability as a barrier to telehealth.
Agriculture and business: reliable connectivity affects precision agriculture, business operations, education, remote work, and rural economic development.
Affordability/adoption: infrastructure isn't the entire issue.
North Dakota's Digital Equity Plan identifies subscription cost and limited digital skills as barriers even where service exists.
Last-mile coverage: the remaining locations can be particularly expensive or difficult to reach; North Dakota's broadband planning has specifically identified terrain in the western Badlands as a deployment challenge.
