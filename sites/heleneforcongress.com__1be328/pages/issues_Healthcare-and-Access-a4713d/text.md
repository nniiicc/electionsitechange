Healthcare and Access
Too many Americans face a harsh reality: when serious illness strikes, families often turn to crowdfunding just to stay financially afloat.
Cancer patients in the United States frequently rely on GoFundMe campaigns to cover treatment costs, while other developed countries ensure their citizens receive care without financial ruin.
That should concern all of us.
This issue is personal to me.
While I was undergoing chemotherapy in Bismarck and Fargo, my savings ran out, and my friends started a GoFundMe campaign to help me through treatment.
We need healthcare policies that prioritize patients and strengthen access to care.
I support:
- Improving access, affordability, and prevention
- Protecting people with pre-existing conditions
- Ensuring mental health parity
- Strengthening rural healthcare systems
- Supporting nurses and frontline healthcare workers
- Enforcing antitrust laws to reduce healthcare consolidation
- Increasing transparency in healthcare pricing
Reform must be responsible.
We can lower costs without breaking access.
We can demand accountability without destabilizing care.
Healthcare should not be a privilege for the fortunate.
It should be accessible, affordable, and reliable for every family in America.
America is also facing a growing shortage of healthcare workers.
We anticipate shortages of nearly 200,000 nurses and tens of thousands of physicians in the coming years.
Supporting healthcare workers and strengthening training pipelines must be part of the solution.
North Dakota Healthcare
Healthcare access is especially challenging in North Dakota, where rural communities face provider shortages and long travel distances for care.
North Dakota currently faces a shortage of approximately 2,185 nurses.
I personally worked as a traveling nurse in North Dakota during 2019 and 2020 to help address these shortages.
Physician shortage is also significant.
North Dakota is projected to face a shortage of 500 doctors by 2040, with rural areas hit hardest.
Pregnant women often travel long distances for prenatal care, and mental health services remain limited in many communities.
Half of North Dakota’s critical access hospitals are losing money, threatening access to lifesaving care in rural areas.
The state also ranks 37th in the nation in doctor-to-patient ratio, highlighting the need to strengthen healthcare infrastructure.
Medicaid is also essential to healthcare access in North Dakota.
Approximately 39,000 North Dakotans could risk losing coverage under proposed changes, which could create additional barriers for seniors, people with disabilities, and low-income families.
Expanding telehealth, supporting community health clinics, and strengthening rural healthcare systems can help improve access across North Dakota.
Healthcare access should not depend on where you live.
Strong healthcare systems support families, strengthen communities, and ensure a healthier future for North Dakota.
