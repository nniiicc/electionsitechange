Stewardship is the judgment to know why, when, and how.
Our guiding principle is simple: protect what matters, prevent what we can, and never treat the human cost of war as an afterthought.
America must rebuild its reputation as a nation whose strength is matched by its judgment, whose commitments can be trusted, and whose power serves a clear purpose.
Our allies must know that our word means something.
Our adversaries must understand that our commitments are serious.
And the world must see a United States that can defend its interests without abandoning its principles.
Our international standing cannot be sustained by military capability alone.
It also depends on consistency, credibility, restraint, and the confidence other nations place in our leadership.
When our actions appear unpredictable, our commitments shift without explanation, or our government appears aligned with brutal dictators whose conduct contradicts the values we claim to uphold, we weaken the trust that makes American leadership effective.
We must reclaim moral authority.
That is not authority to dictate morality to the world, but the discipline to hold ourselves accountable to our own principles.
This requires a new posture toward war.
The United States must remain prepared to defend its people, honor its legitimate commitments, deter aggression, and respond decisively when necessary.
But military readiness must not become a permanent, voracious appetite for military action.
We must distinguish self-defense from self-serving intervention, deterrence from provocation, and strength from the impulse to dominate.
I.
Stewarding American Power 1.
Use Force With Clear Purpose I support reigning in any president’s war powers and redistributing that power back in practice to Congress.
We are living through a dangerous loosening of restraint and an even more dangerous lack of consequences when presidents rush into war and drag the nation and its allies with them OR when we allow ourselves to be rushed into war in support of allies with questionable motives that do not align with our interests into the future.
Military force is one of the most consequential powers entrusted to a government.
It should never be exercised casually, for spectacle, or without a clear understanding of what success requires.
Before entering a conflict, the United States should be able to answer fundamental questions: What specific threat or national interest justifies the use of force?
What lawful authority permits action, and what role must Congress play?
What are the objectives, and how will we know whether they have been achieved?
What are the likely costs in American lives, financial resources, regional stability, and civilian suffering?
What happens after the immediate military objective is accomplished?
What conditions would justify ending the operation, and what conditions would require us to reconsider it?
These questions are not signs of weakness.
They are the obligations of responsible leadership.
Self-defense is not the same as self-serving action.
Defending Americans from an imminent or continuing threat, deterring aggression, and meeting legitimate security commitments are different from using military power to pursue vague ambitions, secure prestige, punish a rival without a durable objective, or become entangled in conflicts whose purpose cannot be clearly explained.
The United States should act with resolve when force is necessary.
But the threshold for war must be serious, the legal basis must be clear, and the objectives must be attainable.
The same goes for how we talk about war and how we respond to human suffering as a government.
We should never confuse the ability to begin a war with the ability to bring it to a responsible conclusion.
2.
Diplomacy First, Readiness Always Diplomacy should be the first instrument of serious statecraft, not the last resort after threats have escalated and lives have been lost.
That means investing in professional diplomacy, intelligence, conflict prevention, crisis communication, arms-control efforts where verifiable and appropriate, and direct channels that can reduce the risk of miscalculation, even with adversaries.
Diplomacy is not an act of trust in every government.
It is a tool for managing a world in which interests conflict and misunderstandings can become catastrophic.
We can negotiate without endorsing a regime.
We can communicate without surrendering leverage.
We can pursue de-escalation without pretending threats do not exist.
At the same time, diplomacy requires credible readiness.
The United States must maintain capable armed forces, resilient supply chains, effective intelligence, secure communications, strong cyber defenses, and the capacity to protect its people and meet its commitments.
Our service members should never be asked to compensate for poor planning, neglected readiness, or political decisions made without a serious assessment of risk.
Diplomacy first does not mean readiness later.
It means preparing for conflict while working relentlessly to prevent it.
3.
Restore Credibility Through Consistency American credibility is a strategic asset.
It influences whether allies coordinate with us, whether adversaries believe our warnings, whether ceasefires hold, and whether diplomatic agreements are taken seriously.
We must rebuild that credibility through consistency rather than theatrical displays of strength.
When the United States draws a red line, it must have a clear basis for doing so, communicate the consequences of crossing it, and be prepared to follow through lawfully and proportionately or explain openly when circumstances require a change.
When we commit to a ceasefire, we should work to uphold it, monitor compliance, and address violations through a defined process.
When we enter an agreement, our partners should not have to wonder whether it will survive the next political announcement.
Credibility does not mean refusing to reconsider a bad decision.
It means making changes transparently, honoring legitimate commitments, and ensuring that our words and actions remain connected.
We should not make threats we are unwilling or unable to carry out.
Nor should we make promises we have no serious intention of keeping.
A red line without a credible policy behind it is a liability.
A commitment honored consistently is a source of strength.
4.
Strengthen Alliances Without Surrendering Judgment America is stronger when it works with reliable partners.
Alliances can deter aggression, share burdens, expand diplomatic options, improve intelligence, and reduce the likelihood that a crisis becomes a wider war.
But alliances are built on trust, consultation, and mutual responsibility.
They cannot be sustained by treating partners as disposable when inconvenient or expecting their support without respecting their interests.
We should consult allies before consequential actions whenever circumstances permit, communicate changes in policy clearly, and meet legitimate commitments.
We should also expect partners to contribute meaningfully to collective security and to respect the obligations they have accepted.
Our relationships should be guided by durable interests and shared responsibilities, not personal loyalties between leaders, side deals, ideological convenience, or a willingness to overlook brutality when committed by a government that happens to be useful to us.
We must maintain a clear distinction between engaging a government and endorsing it.
No leader's willingness to cooperate with the United States should automatically excuse repression, aggression, or the deliberate abuse of civilians.
America does not need every nation to agree with us.
It needs other nations to understand what we stand for, what we will defend, and what they can reasonably expect from us.
5.
Build the Capacity to Defend and the Discipline to Account Military readiness is a public responsibility.
It requires sustained investment, skilled personnel, modern equipment, a strong industrial base, and careful stewardship of taxpayer resources.
The United States should strengthen the domestic capacity needed to maintain its defense, including advanced manufacturing, aerospace, shipbuilding, semiconductors, cybersecurity, resilient energy systems, and the skilled workforce behind them.
Strategic dependence on fragile or adversarial supply chains can create vulnerabilities that become visible only in a crisis.
But readiness is not a blank check.
Defense spending should be tied to clearly identified needs, credible assessments of threats, measurable capability, and effective oversight.
Procurement should reward performance and reliability, not political connections.
Programs that repeatedly fail to deliver should face meaningful review.
Emergency spending should be transparent to the greatest extent compatible with genuine operational security.
Congress must exercise its constitutional responsibilities, including oversight of military action and the public resources committed to it.
The executive branch must provide timely, meaningful explanations of the purposes, risks, and costs of significant operations, subject to legitimate protections for classified information.
We must also plan for the end of a conflict before the beginning of one.
If a military operation creates a foreseeable need for stabilization, humanitarian assistance, infrastructure repair, or long-term security support, those obligations belong in the initial assessment, not in a future administration's pile of unresolved consequences.
The question is not simply whether we can afford to launch an operation.
It is whether we have accounted for the full cost of carrying it through responsibly.
Readiness without accountability can become waste.
Power without a plan for what follows can become a lasting liability.
Military action without humanitarian commitments is inhumane.
II.
Recognizing Threats Without Becoming Defined by Them 6.
Face Adversaries With Clear Eyes The United States must be realistic about governments and organizations that threaten its people, undermine international security, coerce their neighbors, or seek to weaken our institutions.
Strategic competition with China requires sustained attention to military deterrence, technological leadership, economic resilience, secure supply chains, and the protection of critical infrastructure.
We should compete firmly where interests conflict, cooperate where meaningful common interests exist, and preserve channels that reduce the risk of accidental escalation.
Russia's aggression, regional conflicts, nuclear risks, terrorism, cyberattacks, and attempts to interfere with democratic institutions demand serious and adaptable responses.
Our policies must be based on the capabilities and conduct of specific actors, not on slogans or assumptions that every disagreement is an existential struggle.
We must distinguish governments from their populations.
We must distinguish legitimate competition from an unavoidable march toward war.
And we must not mistake hostility toward an adversary for a strategy.
An adversary's wrongdoing does not relieve us of the responsibility to think clearly about our own actions.
Our objective should be to deter aggression, protect American interests, reduce vulnerabilities, and create conditions in which conflict is less likely.
We should not aim to manufacture permanent enemies in order to justify permanent confrontation.
7.
Defend Against Emerging and Asymmetric Threats National security extends beyond conventional battlefields.
Cyberattacks, terrorism, sabotage, foreign interference, attacks on critical infrastructure, threats to space-based systems, and the misuse of emerging technologies can harm Americans without a traditional declaration of war.
We must strengthen intelligence coordination, cyber resilience, infrastructure protection, emergency preparedness, and cooperation among federal, state, local, and private-sector partners.
We should improve our ability to detect threats early and respond proportionately, with clear legal authority and appropriate oversight.
Preventing an attack is often less costly in money, lives, and public confidence than responding after damage has been done.
Our security policies must also protect the freedoms they are intended to defend.
Counterterrorism and counterintelligence efforts should be effective, lawful, and subject to meaningful safeguards.
Fear must not become a permanent justification for unchecked government power.
The purpose of security is to protect the society we value, not to transform that society into a permanent state of emergency.
8.
Treat Economic Security as Part of National Security A nation cannot remain strategically secure if it is unable to sustain the industries, infrastructure, energy systems, technologies, and workforce on which its defense depends.
Trade policy, targeted sanctions, export controls, investment screening, and industrial policy can serve legitimate security objectives.
They should be designed carefully, with defined purposes, measurable criteria, and periodic review.
Sanctions should have an identifiable objective and a credible theory of how they might achieve it.
Their humanitarian effects, enforcement challenges, and potential to strengthen adversarial alliances should be assessed honestly.
Where restrictions no longer advance their intended purpose, they should be reconsidered.
We should protect sensitive technologies and prevent hostile actors from exploiting critical vulnerabilities without treating every foreign investment or international commercial relationship as a threat.
Economic pressure is a tool, not an end in itself.
Its success should be measured by whether it advances a defined security or diplomatic objective, not by how severe it appears.
A durable national security strategy must also recognize that America's industrial capacity, research institutions, energy systems, ports, agricultural strength, and skilled workforce are strategic assets.
Protecting and developing those assets strengthens our ability to deter conflict and respond when necessary.
III.
Humanity, Morality, and the Responsibility of Power 9.
Put Human Life at the Center of All Decisions About War.
Period.
War must never become so familiar that we grow indifferent to what it does to human beings.
Every conflict produces more than military statistics.
It produces parents who bury children, children who lose parents, families driven from their homes, people injured for life, communities stripped of their livelihoods, and generations that may inherit fear and instability long after the fighting ends.
These losses are not less real because they occur outside our borders.
They are not less tragic because the people suffering them belong to an opposing nation.
The United States must retain the ability to recognize the humanity of people on every side of a conflict while remaining clear-eyed about responsibility, aggression, and legitimate self-defense.
This is not moral equivalence.
A nation defending itself is not the same as a nation launching an unjustified invasion.
A deliberate attack on civilians is not the same as a lawful attack on a military objective.
Responsibility matters.
So does the distinction between combatants and civilians and we must operate in truth and integrity when the lines begin to blur, like when self-defense morphs into genocide or democratic objectives turn into economic ventures.
The humanity of those who suffer must never depend on whether their government is our ally, their military is our enemy, or their lives are politically convenient to acknowledge.
We should never revel in bloodshed, treat destruction as entertainment, or turn human suffering into a performance of national pride.
Writing political slogans or personal names on bombs is an abhorrent expression of heartlessness, not strength.
The use of military force is a grave necessity, or some would call it a noble responsibility, but it should never be a stage for celebrating the capacity to kill.
Our military should be respected for its discipline, professionalism, courage, and commitment to lawful service, not for a flippant appetite for destruction.
10.
Make the Protection of Civilians a Strategic and Moral Priority Avoiding civilian harm must once again be a central priority in American foreign policy and military planning and we must hold our allies to the same high standard.
The United States should uphold its obligations under international humanitarian law, including the principles of distinction, proportionality, and feasible precautions in attack.
These obligations must shape operational decisions, not merely appear in statements after casualties occur.
That requires rigorous target verification, credible assessments of civilian presence and risk, appropriate review of weapons and tactics, and a willingness to delay or cancel an attack when the expected civilian harm is excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.
It also requires serious investigation when credible allegations of unlawful harm arise, public explanations where security permits, and accountability when rules have been violated.
Civilian casualties should never be dismissed as an inevitable cost of doing business like what we are seeing today around the world.
Even when an attack is lawful, the human consequences warrant scrutiny, acknowledgment, and learning.
We should improve the way civilian harm is tracked, investigated, reported, and prevented.
We should learn from mistakes, change practices that create unnecessary risk, and ensure that operational urgency does not erase the responsibility to protect civilian life.
No policy can promise that every civilian will be spared in every conflict.
But the impossibility of eliminating all harm is not a reason to stop working to prevent as much of it as possible.
What we cannot guarantee, we must still strive to protect.
What we cause, we must be willing to examine.
11.
Plan for Repair Before the First Strike War does not end when the last target is struck.
Military operations can destroy roads, hospitals, water systems, power infrastructure, homes, schools, and the institutions on which daily life depends.
The consequences may outlast the fighting by years or decades, creating humanitarian emergencies, economic collapse, displacement, political instability, and new conditions for extremism.
If we know that an operation could create these consequences, we have a responsibility to consider them before acting.
Any significant military intervention should include a realistic assessment of post-conflict requirements, including stabilization, humanitarian access, infrastructure repair, security, governance risks, and the responsibilities of the United States and its partners.
The scale of the plan must match the foreseeable scale of the damage and the stated objectives of the operation.
That does not mean America must assume responsibility for rebuilding every country affected by conflict.
Nor does it mean that every intervention is justified because reconstruction might follow.
It means we must honestly weigh the obligations we may create, the resources they will require, the risks of abandoning them, and the limits of what we can responsibly undertake.
The costs of war include more than weapons, deployments, and immediate casualties.
They include the repair of what is broken, the support of those harmed, the security needed to prevent renewed violence, and the consequences of failing to plan for the day after.
When these costs cannot be responsibly met, that reality must inform the decision to use force in the first place.
We should never be eager to break what we have not seriously considered how to repair.
12.
Restore the “Department of Defense” as a Statement of Purpose Names shape institutional culture.
They communicate what an organization values, what it considers its mission, and what kind of conduct it expects from its people.
For that reason, the United States should reaffirm the Department of Defense as the guiding name and posture of America's military establishment, rather than elevate the Department of War as its public identity.
This is not a call to weaken our armed forces or obscure the reality that they must be capable of fighting and winning when the nation must defend itself.
It is a statement about the purpose to which that capability is directed.
The word defense begins with a responsibility: to protect the nation, its people, its sovereignty, and its legitimate commitments.
It places military strength in service of security rather than treating war itself as the defining objective.
Our institutions should communicate readiness without glorifying conflict, confidence without contempt, and resolve without recklessness.
The United States must be capable of decisive action.
But we should be equally committed to preventing unnecessary war, limiting harm when war occurs, and restoring peace when fighting ends.
The purpose of military strength is not to make war our identity.
It is to make the protection of our people and the preservation of peace more achievable.
13.
Reclaim Moral Authority Through Our Own Conduct America cannot credibly advocate for human rights, sovereignty, international law, or the protection of civilians if it treats those principles as optional whenever they become inconvenient.
Our moral authority must begin with our own conduct.
We should apply our standards consistently, recognize the wrongdoing of partners as well as adversaries, and refuse to excuse brutality simply because it serves a short-term strategic interest.
This does not require moral perfection, nor does it require us to withdraw from a complicated world.
It requires honesty about the gap between our stated values and our actions, and the willingness to narrow that gap.
We should engage governments when engagement serves a legitimate purpose.
But engagement must not be confused with endorsement, and strategic cooperation must not become a blank check for repression or aggression.
We should speak clearly about atrocities, protect the independence of our decision-making, and avoid relationships or public displays that reasonably suggest that American support is unconditional regardless of a partner's conduct.
Our credibility will not be restored by declaring ourselves morally superior to other nations.
It will be restored by demonstrating that our own principles impose real limits on our choices.
Moral authority is not something we can demand from the world.
It is something we must earn through consistency, restraint, accountability, and courage.
14.
Make Peace a Measurable Outcome A responsible foreign policy should be evaluated not only by military victories, but also by the security and stability that follow.
We should assess major operations and diplomatic initiatives against clearly stated objectives, including whether threats have been reduced, commitments have been honored, civilian harm has been minimized, escalation has been contained, and conditions for a durable peace have improved.
Where a ceasefire is negotiated, we should measure whether it holds and whether violations are addressed.
Where sanctions are imposed, we should assess whether they advance their stated purpose.
Where military action is taken, we should evaluate whether it achieved its objectives and whether the aftermath was responsibly managed.
The public deserves meaningful reporting on the objectives, costs, and results of significant military engagements, consistent with legitimate national security requirements.
Congress should receive the information necessary to exercise its oversight responsibilities.
Policies that fail should be revised.
Commitments that cannot be sustained should be addressed honestly.
Successful approaches should be strengthened.
The objective is not to preserve a doctrine, an operation, or a political narrative.
The objective is to protect people, defend legitimate interests, and produce a more secure and stable outcome.
A Stronger America Through Responsible Strength The world does not need an America that is reckless, withdrawn, or indifferent.
It needs an America that is capable, dependable, and disciplined.
We need to be a nation prepared to defend itself and its allies, serious about deterrence, committed to diplomacy, and unwilling to treat human suffering as an acceptable political spectacle.
We must be clear about our interests without making every disagreement a war.
We must be prepared to act without becoming eager to intervene.
We must defend our commitments without making promises carelessly.
And when force becomes necessary, we must carry the responsibility for its use with the seriousness it deserves.
Our allies should be able to trust our word.
Our adversaries should understand our resolve.
Civilians should not disappear from our calculations simply because they live on the other side of a conflict.
And the American people should be able to see that the resources entrusted to their government are being used toward clear, defensible purposes.
This is the standard of Strength and Stewardship.
Resolve without recklessness.
Diplomacy first, readiness always.
Strength in defense, discipline in action, humanity in judgment, and accountability in every single outcome.
America must be strong enough to protect, wise enough to restrain itself, and responsible enough to help secure the peace that should be our ultimate goal.
