Healthcare
Rural Tennessee hospitals are closing mainly because they lose money on patient care.
Low patient volume, high uninsured rates (Tennessee never expanded Medicaid), private-insurer underpayments, Medicare Advantage denials, staffing shortages, and the end of pandemic aid have left many with no financial cushion.
The state ranks near the top nationally in closures, with 12 hospitals recently listed at immediate risk.
Direct primary care (DPC) could ease several of those pressures without replacing hospitals.
Patients pay a flat monthly fee ($50–$150) for primary and urgent care with no insurance billing; same-day access and longer visits have been shown to cut ER use by about 40% and admissions by 20%, reducing the money-losing cases rural hospitals absorb.
Lower paperwork lets DPC clinics stay viable with smaller panels, which can help recruit and retain rural doctors.
A hospital could run or partner with a DPC clinic for more predictable cash flow and to keep patients local.
For uninsured Tennesseans, a membership is often cheaper than insurance plus a deductible for routine care, lowering uncompensated-care losses.
DPC does not cover inpatient stays, surgery, or true emergencies, so patients still need catastrophic coverage; it works best as a stronger outpatient front door that reduces hospital losses rather than as a standalone solution.
This along with expanded medicaid could save rural hospitals and expand access to more people until we can get to the root of the issues that cause chronic illness in TN.
