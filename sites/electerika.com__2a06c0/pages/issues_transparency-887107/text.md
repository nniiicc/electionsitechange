Transparency and a Government That Answers to You
When the process is transparent, the people who benefit are constituents.
Until recently, lawmakers on Beacon Hill took most of their votes behind closed doors, and Massachusetts is the only state in the country where the governor, legislature, and judiciary all exempt themselves from public records law.
They prioritize corporate interests over our communities, and with no transparency, we don't have the ability to hold legislators accountable.
It is no coincidence that ours is also one of the least productive legislatures in the country: when power is concentrated behind closed doors, the bills our communities need fail quietly in committee.
Erika co-founded Act on Mass to change this.
Act on Mass organized ballot campaigns across the state, and won: as of last year, every committee vote is now posted publicly online.The transparency we fought for is already delivering results: when the public could see what was in the energy bill, the poison pills that would have raised your bills were stripped out, and with that same accountability the Legislature finally moved legislation to protect our immigrant neighbors.
In the Senate, Erika will fight to:
- Ensure everyone has enough time to review and meaningfully advocate for a bill before filing amendments or taking a vote.
Almost always, legislators have only a few hours to review a bill before filing amendments, even if it is hundreds of pages long.
- Pass public records law that applies to all three branches of government.
- Apply open meeting law to the Legislature, which is particularly important for conference committees where six lawmakers determine the near-final version of a bill before sending to the Governor to sign.
- Defend the voter-approved audit of the Legislature, which passed with 72 percent support.
Transparency Is How We Win Everything Else
Transparency is not separate from the fights for housing, healthcare, climate action, and workers' rights.
It is a precondition for all of them.
A 2021 report from the Climate and Development Lab at Brown University, "Who's Delaying Climate Action in Massachusetts?", documented the problem directly: 90 percent of public testimony on climate bills were in support of taking bold action, yet the vast majority of those bills died in committee, where legislators influenced by fossil fuel lobbyists voted against climate advocates in secret, and with impunity.
The report's top recommendation was in support of transparency, by making committee votes public, recording floor votes, and archiving testimony.
That pattern repeats across every issue our communities care about.
For years, a bill could have a majority of the Legislature signed on as co-sponsors, from preventing wage theft to committing to 100 percent renewable energy to protecting reproductive healthcare access, and still be shelved in secret, with no way for you to know how your own representative voted.
When the process is opaque, it benefits greedy corporations.
When the process is transparent, it's constituents who win.
Transparency is how we shift the balance of power, and that is why Erika treats it as the first fight, not a side issue.
What We Have Already Won
Erika co-founded Act on Mass before she was ever elected, because she saw from the outside what she now sees from the inside: secrecy is a policy choice that protects the powerful.
Alongside grassroots volunteers, Act on Mass organized ballot campaigns in districts across the state, and the pressure worked.
As of last year we won:
- Every committee vote is now public.
For the first time in decades, you can see how your legislators vote on every bill in committee, where most legislation lives or dies.
- Written testimony is now released, so the public can see who is pushing for and against a bill, not just who wins.
- Hearings come with real notice and hybrid participation, so working people can weigh in without taking a day off to travel to the State House.
- Conference committees must open their first meeting to the public, a first crack in the most secretive room on Beacon Hill.
And the wins compound: every public committee vote gives organizers a target, every released testimony shows who is standing in the way, and every open meeting makes the next fight easier to win.
Accountability Starts With How Erika Serves
Transparency is not only a demand Erika makes of the institution.
It is how she does the job.
She has personally knocked over 15,000 doors in the district, sends a weekly newsletter on what is happening on Beacon Hill, holds regular town halls, and writes to constituents explaining her reasoning on hard votes, including when some constituents disagree with her.
She believes engaged, critical feedback from the people she represents is exactly how democracy is supposed to work, and she answers to it directly.
That is the standard every legislator should be held to, and it is the standard she will bring to the Senate.
Standing Up to the Pressure
State House leadership holds real tools to keep legislators quiet: control over committee assignments, control over stipends that make up a meaningful share of legislators' pay, and the threat of being shut out of shaping legislation.
Erika has lived this for three terms, and she has learned that the threat is real but the answer is leverage that leadership cannot take away: do the policy work, build coalitions with colleagues and with unions, tenants, and community organizations on the outside, and mobilize constituents.
The broker fee ban did not pass because leadership handed it to Erika.
It passed because she built a coalition that made it impossible to ignore.
And when a Home Rule Petition threatened to weaken the Lawrence Teachers Union's bargaining position, Erika was the only House member willing to use her procedural right to halt it, and she worked with AFT Massachusetts to win the fight.
Years of organizing around the State House taught Erika a lesson she asks voters to apply to every candidate, including her: it is not enough for a legislator to agree with you on the issues.
What matters is whether they will stand up for those issues in public, even when it is uncomfortable.
The Unfinished Agenda
In the Senate, Erika will keep working with the transparency coalition she helped build, including Act on Mass, good-government advocates, and colleagues in both chambers who are ready for a Legislature that answers to the public.
And she will fight to:
- Give everyone time to read the bill.
Almost always, legislators have only a few hours to review a bill before filing amendments, even when it is hundreds of pages long.
Erika will fight for a guaranteed review period measured in days, not hours, so legislators, advocates, and the public can weigh in before a vote, not after.
- Apply the public records law to all three branches of government.
Massachusetts is the only state where the governor, legislature, and judiciary all exempt themselves, which means the public cannot request the documents behind the decisions that shape their lives, a basic right people have in every other state.
That must end.
- Bring the open meeting law to the Legislature.
Every city council and school committee in Massachusetts deliberates in public.
The Legislature should too, especially conference committees, where six lawmakers determine the near-final version of a bill behind closed doors.
- Defend the audit voters demanded.
Nearly 72 percent of voters approved the audit of the Legislature, and it still faces resistance and litigation on Beacon Hill.
Erika will fight to see the voters' will carried out in full.
- Reform the stipend system.
Leadership-controlled stipends flow to a large majority of legislators and function as a mechanism of control, rewarding silence and punishing independence.
Erika supports reforming the system so legislators answer to their constituents, not to the leaders who set their pay.
