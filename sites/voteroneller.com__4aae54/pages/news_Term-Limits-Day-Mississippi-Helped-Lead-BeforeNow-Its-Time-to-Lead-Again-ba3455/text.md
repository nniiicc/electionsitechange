By Ron Eller, Mississippi State Chair, U.S.
Term Limits
On February 27th, Americans celebrate Term Limits Day, marking the 75th anniversary of the passage of the 22nd Amendment, which limits a president to two terms in office.
It's a milestone worth reflecting on—especially here in Mississippi, a state that has long understood the value of citizen government and constitutional guardrails.
When Congress debated the 22nd Amendment in 1950 and 1951, the idea of term limits for presidents was far from universally accepted.
In fact, some of the loudest opposition came from Washington insiders who had spent decades in power themselves.
Opponents argued that elections alone were enough—that if voters wanted to keep reelecting the same president, the Constitution shouldn't stand in the way.
They claimed term limits would deprive the nation of experienced leadership and weaken the presidency.
And many of the leading critics were long-serving politicians, including Sam Rayburn, Alben W.
Barkley, and Emanuel Celler—men who had spent much of their lives inside the corridors of power.
Does this sound familiar?
Seventy-five years later, we are hear the exact same arguments used to oppose congressional term limits.
We're told elections are enough.
We're told seniority equals wisdom.
We're told experience justifies permanent incumbency.
And once again, many of the loudest voices making these claims are career politicians who have benefited the most from the status quo.
History tells us those arguments were wrong then—and they're wrong now.
Before the 22nd Amendment, there were no formal limits on presidential terms.
The system relied on tradition and self-restraint.
But once that norm was broken, Americans realized something important: a republic cannot rely solely on good behavior from those in power.
It needs enforceable rules.
That lesson has served us well.
For 75 years, the two-term limit on presidents has provided stability, prevented the consolidation of power, encouraged leadership renewal, and reinforced the idea that no office belongs to any one person or political party.
Mississippi understood this early.
Our state was the 18th in the nation to ratify the 22nd Amendment, helping ensure it became part of the Constitution.
Mississippi leaders recognized that term limits weren't an attack on democracy—they were a protection of it.
Yet today, Congress operates without any such limits.
Members routinely serve 30, 40, even 50 years.
Entire committees, leadership posts, and power structures are dominated by a handful of long-tenured politicians.
Competitive elections have become the exception rather than the rule, as incumbency advantages grow stronger with each passing decade; 90 plus percent of all incumbents are reelected.
And once again, we're told to trust the system as it is.
But Mississippi families know better.
We know that real accountability requires more than theory.
It requires structure.
It requires limits.
And it requires the courage to say that public service was never meant to be a lifetime occupation.
That's why it's imperative that Mississippi once again step forward—this time by joining other states in calling for an Article V Convention limited to proposing congressional term limits.
This approach allows the states, not Washington insiders, to lead the reform—just as the Constitution intended.
An Article V convention limited to term limits is not radical.
It's responsible.
It doesn't rewrite the Constitution; it strengthens it.
And it directly addresses a problem with Congresses proven unwilling to fix this issue on its own.
On this Term Limits Day, as we celebrate the wisdom of the 22nd Amendment 75 years later, Mississippi has an opportunity to honor that legacy—not just with words, but with action.
We helped lead once before.
Now it's time to lead again.
