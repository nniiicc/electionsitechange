The legislature passed a bipartisan plan to finally fund our schools fairly, then broke its own promise.
It funded schools $2.75 billion below what that plan called for, shifted the cost onto your property taxes, and found more than a billion dollars a year for private school vouchers with almost none of the accountability it demands of public schools.
Meanwhile, a Columbus teacher describes classrooms of 42 kids, Lima schools cut 24 and a half positions this spring, and 70% of new school levies fail because homeowners can't afford another hike.
Every superintendent in Allen County signed the same letter asking the state to step up.
This is what happens when Columbus stops working for your community.
What that shift costs you at home is on the economy page .
The short version Finish and fund the bipartisan school funding plan Columbus walked away from.
Vouchers paid for somewhere other than your public schools, under the same public rules.
Property tax relief that lasts, by putting the state back to paying its share of schools.
What I'll fight for in Columbus Fair funding, fully implemented.
Finish and fund the bipartisan Fair School Funding Plan, written together by Bob Cupp, a Lima Republican, and John Patterson, an Ashtabula Democrat.
Columbus funded the plan's first two phases in two straight budgets, while it was cheap.
When the final bill came due, the House Speaker announced, "We won't be doing the Cupp-Patterson Plan…" (Lima News, April 2, 2025), and the budget came in $2.75 billion below what the formula called for .
A promise made is a promise owed.
I'll vote for the budget that finishes the plan and against any budget that keeps walking away from it.
Vouchers can't be paid for out of your public schools.
Ohio spent more than a billion dollars on private school vouchers last year, and the current two-year budget sets aside $2.44 billion , the most ever.
Vouchers and public schools are paid out of the same state budget, so when one grows that fast, the other is what gives — and what gave was the school funding formula the legislature promised to finish.
Here in Lima, the district's then-superintendent said last year that 591 students were using vouchers, that only 28 of them were low-income , and that nearly five million dollars in public funds was leaving the district.
This isn't about telling a family where to send their kids.
It's arithmetic: if Columbus wants a voucher program, it has to find the money somewhere other than the schools that take every child who walks in the door.
I'll fight for accountability, transparency, and enrollment caps on the voucher and charter programs — published budgets, state tests, and the same anti-discrimination rules public schools already follow — and I'll vote against any budget that grows the program faster than its accountability.
Targeted property tax relief.
Your property taxes climbed because the state cut its share of school funding: from 44.8% to 33.5%, by Allen County's own superintendents' count , while Ohio fell to 45th in the nation .
Columbus did pass a package of relief bills in December 2025 and a one-time credit this June for some seniors and disabled homeowners.
None of it restores the state's share, so your school district still has to come back to you with another levy.
I'll fight for circuit-breaker relief that caps property tax bills for homeowners and renters, and for the state to carry its share of school costs again.
That's the relief that lasts.
I'll also fight for Universal public preschool.
Allen County went from 60 in-home childcare providers to 15 in eight years ; a public preschool option helps parents get back to work and kids arrive at kindergarten ready.
Fully funding STRS.
Teachers' pensions run on contributions from educators and their employers, not the state, so the honest lever is the employer rate: I'll support raising it, and vote for the state school funding that lets districts carry it without cutting classrooms to do it.
I'll also oppose the budget maneuver that cut the board's elected educator seats from 7 to 3 and raised political appointees from 4 to 8, inserted at 1 a.m. with no public testimony; a court has paused that change while educators' lawsuit is heard.
Support for teachers, faculty, and staff.
A $50,000 minimum teacher salary, grow-your-own recruitment, free school meals: I'll vote for every bill that gets us there, whoever sponsors it.
Also protected collective bargaining and tenure, paid family leave, real support for student teachers so the cost of training isn't what keeps someone out of the profession, and an end to teachers buying classroom supplies out of their own pockets.
Classrooms over politics.
Politicians should spend less time telling teachers what they can't say and more time paying what they owe.

A legislative package introduced in the Ohio House in May 2024 would raise Ohio's minimum teacher salary to $50,000, create a Grow Your Own teacher training program, and fund universal public preschool and free meals in public schools.
Read the announcement of the package They passed a plan to fund our schools fairly, then broke their own promise Between 1997 and 2002, the Ohio Supreme Court ruled four separate times that Ohio's school funding system was unconstitutional.
In the first DeRolph decision, the court wrote that the system must undergo a "complete systematic overhaul," because leaning so heavily on local property taxes created unfair gaps between wealthy and low-income districts.
Representatives Bob Cupp, a Republican from Lima, and John Patterson, a Democrat from Ashtabula, answered with the Fair School Funding Plan.
An earlier version passed the Ohio House 87 to 9, and the legislature enacted the plan in 2021 with a six-year phase-in.
Columbus funded the first two phases.
When the final phase came due, the House Speaker announced, "We won't be doing the Cupp-Patterson Plan, but all (school) districts will be getting an increase." The enacted budget funded schools $2.75 billion below what the formula called for.
That wasn't belt-tightening.
That was breaking a promise to every kid in Ohio.

That didn't make schools cheaper.
It made your property taxes higher.
