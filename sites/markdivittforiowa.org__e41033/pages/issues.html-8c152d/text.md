Understand Iowa's Cancer Crisis Iowa has the second-highest cancer rate in the country, and it's one of the only states where that rate is still rising while the rest of the nation's is falling.
Nobody can point to a single cause — cancer is complex — but elevated pesticides, nitrate, PFAS, and radon are among the factors researchers have linked to higher risk.
We shouldn't keep waiting for perfect answers while Iowans get sick.
Mark will push to launch a serious effort to understand what's actually driving Iowa's cancer rates — starting with fully funding the water-quality monitoring that shows us what's in our water — so the state can take targeted, effective action based on evidence, not guesswork.
Public Health Health insurers are increasingly using AI to automatically deny legitimate claims, and that hits rural hospitals the hardest — places like Compass Memorial in Marengo, the only hospital in Iowa County.
Patients and small-town hospitals end up paying the price.
Mark will push for a law requiring a licensed physician, not an algorithm, to sign off before a claim can be denied, with firm response deadlines and public reporting of denial rates.
He'll also fight for Iowans at risk of losing coverage under the new Medicaid law, and make sure local providers have the support they need to keep their doors open.
Public Schools Mark believes in public schools.
Full stop.
If a family wants to send their kids to a private school or a religious school, that's their right, and he respects that completely — it's not his place to tell anyone how to raise their kids.
But he doesn't think the rest of us should be asked to foot the bill for it.
Iowa's voucher program now sends over eight thousand tax dollars per student out of the public system to private and religious schools — no income limit, so families making six figures qualify for the same subsidy as anyone else.
That's money that used to go to your local public school, your kids' teachers, your gym and band program.
Here's where that has left us.
This year Iowa ranked 27th in the nation for education in the Annie E.
Casey Foundation's Kids Count report — down from 20th #ago, and the first time Iowa has landed in the bottom half of the states since that report began in 1990.
Seventy percent of Iowa's fourth graders are not proficient in reading.
Mark is careful about what those numbers mean, because Iowa's teachers have earned that care.
Iowa students test slightly better today than they did in the nineties.
The schools did not get worse.
Iowa was top five in this country and is 27th now because other states decided to invest and Iowa decided not to.
Standing still is a choice, and it was made in Des Moines — not in a classroom here.
Iowa still graduates a higher share of its students than any other state, and still ranks in the top three on the ACT among states that test more than half their kids.
That is the teachers' doing, and it deserves saying before anyone uses a ranking to run down the people who earned it.
Where Iowa has genuinely slipped is math and early reading — and the state's own Department of Education has said Iowa needs evidence-based support in math.
Mark would fund it.
He will fight to fund public schools first, because that's the promise we all made to each other.
Water & Air Quality Clean water and clean air are a basic duty the State of Iowa owes its citizens — not something to be traded away in decisions made behind closed doors.
Mark believes Iowa has an obligation to protect its people and build a healthy environment for the next generation to grow up in, with clean water to drink and clean air to breathe.
He'll push for transparency and enforceable standards, informed by his direct experience fighting the Morgan Valley Energy Center power plant.
Data Center Moratorium As the AI boom drives a wave of new data centers across Iowa, ratepayers and rural communities shouldn't foot the bill for someone else's power and water demand.
Counties have already acted where Republican leadership in Des Moines hasn't — a bill combining data-center transparency and ratepayer protection, House File 2690, passed the House Commerce Committee 23 to 0 this year and still never got a floor vote.
Johnson County, part of this district, passed its own data center moratorium in November 2025, and Linn County followed in July 2026.
Mark is proposing an 18-month statewide moratorium on new large-scale AI data center approvals, with a catch: it doesn't simply expire when the calendar runs out.
Before a single new approval, ratepayer protections, water-use limits, and air quality standards have to be written into law — not just proposed.
Eighteen months is enough time to get it right, working with industry rather than against it.
Iowa needs the investment and the jobs, but not at the cost of our ratepayers, our water, or our air.
Support for Farmers Family farms are the backbone of Iowa County and rural Johnson County.
Mark will back policies that support small and mid-size operations — not the interests with the loudest voices in Des Moines.
That includes right to repair: farm equipment today is often locked down by manufacturer software, forcing farmers to wait days for an authorized dealer while a broken combine sits idle at harvest.
Iowa's House already passed a right-to-repair bill 70-18 this year with bipartisan support, but it stalled in the Republican-controlled Senate.
Mark will push to get it across the finish line, so no farmer has to ask a corporation's permission to fix equipment they already paid for.
The Price at the Pump Every trip to the pump costs more than it should, and in a district this rural, that's not an occasional expense — it's the drive to work, to town, to the doctor, to school pickup.
Diesel hits farmers and anyone hauling a trailer even harder.
Mark doesn't think Iowans should just get used to paying more for less while Des Moines moves on to the next headline.
District 91 needs a voice in the Statehouse that's actually working for the people driving these roads every day — not showing up every two years and asking for a vote.
Property Taxes You work your whole life, pay off your house, and the tax bill keeps coming — for a retiree on a fixed income, that can feel like renting your own home from the government, and that fear deserves an answer.
At the same time, property tax is the most local dollar in Iowa: it funds your kids' school, your volunteer fire department, your county sheriff, and the snowplow on your gravel road, collected here and decided here.
Brad Sherman, the conservative who previously held this seat, warned that eliminating it would force a much bigger state sales tax controlled by Des Moines, and that rural counties “would go broke.” Mark's first proposal doesn't need a new law.
It needs Iowa to fund one it already passed.
In 1994 the legislature extended a property tax credit to all low-income Iowans, and in 1995 added rent reimbursement for low-income renters — because renters pay property tax too, buried in their rent.
Iowa Code went as far as creating the fund and naming the departments that would run it.
Thirty-two years later, according to the Legislature's own fiscal staff, not one dollar has ever been appropriated to it.
The Iowa House and Senate have failed to properly engage with a law already sitting on their own books, and Mark will push to change that.
The Code itself sets the figure: $27 million pays those claims in full.
Iowa's Taxpayer Relief Fund holds about $3.6 billion and earns roughly $80 million a year in interest, so the entire promise costs about a third of what that fund earns sitting still — no new tax, and no new program to build.
Mark would also move Iowa's existing assessment freeze down from age 70 to 65, and fix who pays for it: today the state reimburses counties for only a fraction of that freeze and the county absorbs the rest.
And he'd keep pressing on the rising valuations that push bills up even after this year's property tax law (SF2472) — passed by the Republican majority in Des Moines — which caps local governments' revenue growth but doesn't guarantee your bill actually goes down.
Helping Iowans Age at Home Most older Iowans want the same thing: to stay in their own homes, in their own communities, for as long as they can.
Mark wants to respect that wish and help make it possible — supporting home-based care, family caregivers, and the local services that let people age where they're rooted.
Not everyone has family nearby, and that's where the rest of us come in.
Being Iowa Nice isn't just waving at your neighbor; it's making sure the folks who built these communities can grow old in them with dignity.
Here's what that promise looks like on paper right now.
Iowa runs Medicaid waiver programs whose whole purpose is to keep people in their homes instead of an institution.
As of August 2026, 30,269 Iowans are on waiting lists for those programs, and 26,259 are enrolled in them.
More Iowans are waiting for this help than are getting it, and the lists grew by more than 3,500 people in the first eight months of this year.
The oldest application still waiting on the Health and Disability list was filed in March 2020 — someone asked Iowa for help before the pandemic and still hasn't gotten an answer.
The Elderly Waiver itself has no waiting list, and Mark won't claim otherwise.
But it is 98 percent full — 7,874 of 8,009 slots in use — while the number of Iowans over 75 keeps growing.
There's no line yet, and that's exactly when you fix it.
Here's the part that makes this a choice rather than a shortage: the federal government has already approved more slots than Iowa pays for — authorization for more than fifteen hundred people the state budget doesn't fund.
Medicaid is a match program, so the federal government covers the majority of the cost of each of those slots.
Every slot Iowa declines to fund is also federal money Iowa turns down.
Iowa's own Department of Health and Human Services gave the Legislature the arithmetic on clearing these lists back in 2023.
It wasn't done, and the lists have more than doubled since.
It gets more expensive every year we wait.
Approach How Mark Solves Problems Mark doesn't believe in zero-sum politics.
He's spent his working life building and fixing things — balancing real, competing constraints like cost, efficiency, privacy, and energy use — and he knows the best solutions take hard work and input from everyone at the table.
That's the discipline he'll bring to the Statehouse: solutions that hold up, not compromises that give in to the loudest voice in the room.
Who I Fight For A Voice for Those Who Have None Mark isn't interested in platitudes.
District 91 has plenty of people who don't have a lobbyist, can't take a day off to testify in Des Moines, or are talked down to instead of listened to — and they're who he'll work for.
The Race Why This Seat, Why Now Mark never planned to run for office.
But when a power plant project showed up next to his community — with real questions about water and air that nobody would answer — he and his neighbors stood up and fought it.
That's when he learned Des Moines won't look out for District 91 unless we send someone who will.
Take property rights: out-of-state pipeline companies shouldn't be able to seize your land through eminent domain, something Iowans across the aisle agree on.
So why should it stop the second it's a power plant or a data center instead of a pipeline?
Iowans' land and water don't deserve less protection just because the industry changed.
That's why Mark is running.
Iowa Values This Isn't Who We Are The culture wars dominating national politics didn't start in Iowa, and they don't reflect who we are.
Iowans have a long history of leading on fairness, often years ahead of the rest of the country.
In 1868, Iowa's Supreme Court ordered the desegregation of its public schools — the first court in the nation to reject "separate but equal" in education, eighty-six years before Brown v.
Board of Education made it the law of the land.
A year later, Arabella Mansfield of Mount Pleasant became the first woman in the United States licensed to practice law, and three years after that Judith Ellen Foster became the first woman to actually practice it here.
That's who we've always been: hard-working, practical, and willing to treat people fairly before it was popular.
Iowa values are simple — a fair day's work, a neighbor you can count on, a family you show up for, plain dealing instead of empty talk, and treating people with respect whether you agree with them or not.
Those values don't belong to one party, and they never have.
Mark won't import fights that belong to national politics.
Iowa runs its own elections, and Iowans decide who represents them — not politicians in Washington.
He wants to work with Republicans and Democrats alike to protect the values that built this state.
