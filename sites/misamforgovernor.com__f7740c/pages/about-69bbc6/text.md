Origins I was born and raised in Lahore, Pakistan, where I spent my early years before moving to the United States in 1997 as a teenager.
Growing up there taught me something important about people.
Pakistan did not have the material abundance that America has, but it was rich in community, compassion, and human connection.
Families shared what they had.
Life moved slower, and people valued one another deeply.
America When my family moved to the United States, I encountered a very different world.
America offered opportunity, innovation, and economic strength on a scale I had never seen.
But the contrast between those two experiences gave me a unique perspective.
I saw one society rich in human connection but struggling economically, and another rich in opportunity but often weighed down by financial pressure and constant stress.
"How do we build a society where people can experience both prosperity and peace — where opportunity exists without losing our humanity?" Jobs along the way Dry cleaner counter Gas station McDonald's Hotel night shifts Taxi driver PC technician Rug sales Telecom sales (MCI, Sprint) Best Buy retail Systems administrator Temporary work & odd jobs Entrepreneurship Financial Education & Innovation Later I worked as a personal banker at Bank of America, where I gained deep knowledge about credit, lending, and financial systems.
That experience led me to start my own credit consulting company helping people repair and rebuild their financial lives.
Many people I worked with were not irresponsible.
They were simply navigating financial systems they had never been taught to understand.
Why do schools not teach people how credit works?
Why do so many Americans graduate without the tools needed to build financial stability?
Eventually my work deeply expanded into cryptocurrency and financial innovation.
I realized early that blockchain is the evolution of technology into a decentralized construct.
Due Process Standing Up for Justice In recent years, businesses I was involved with were shut down by state enforcement actions and their assets were frozen.
To protect my right to due process and continue my legal defense, I had to sell my home and use the equity simply to fund the ability to fight the case.
That experience revealed something deeply troubling about how systems can function.
On paper, every citizen has rights — due process, legal defense, and the presumption of innocence.
But in reality, the ability to exercise those rights can become nearly impossible when a person no longer has access to the financial resources required to defend themselves.
Justice should never function that way.
Due process must remain real and accessible — not simply something written on paper.
