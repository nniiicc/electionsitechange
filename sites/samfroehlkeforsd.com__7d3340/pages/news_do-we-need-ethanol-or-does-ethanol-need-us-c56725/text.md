I read a letter to the editor in our local paper this week.
A candidate for state office had made assertions during the Deuel County Lincoln Day Dinner regarding what he viewed as inappropriate amounts of money having been donated to his current opponent by the ethanol industry in the 2024 campaign.
The letter was written by one of these ethanol companies, and they accused him of using inaccurate figures in his address.
Part of what they said was, in fact, technically correct.
The limit on what can be contributed to a legislative campaign by an entity or an individual is $1,000.
Three things come to mind when the subject of the ethanol industry comes up.
First, what was not mentioned in this letter was a practice by which various corporations and special interest groups can circumvent these campaign finance restrictions.
The contribution to a legislative candidate committee may be only $1,000, but the limit for contributions to a political action committee (PAC) is $10,000, and there is no limit on what a PAC can contribute to a candidate.
Of course, there’s no blatant violation of the law, no one can prove that the 10 grand which went in one end of a PAC is the same 10 which came out the other end, contributed to a candidate.
Secondly, to address the broader question of ethanol companies making PAC or campaign contributions at all; since they appear to live off taxpayer funded subsidies, it seems immoral, if not explicitly illegal, for them to be able to help candidates who can vote them more tax money.
Not to mention the millions of dollars the ethanol industry poured into the ballot question committee trying to push through the carbon pipeline.
Because there is no limit whatsoever on how much can be contributed by an entity to support or oppose a ballot question.
And third, the elephant in the room, is ethanol working for us?
With the price of corn so low, and margins thin enough as it is, why are we producing so much corn?
The government created a monster in the form of ethanol.
It worked as a greenwashed farm subsidy for a while, and as a price support, but ultimately, like all price supports, it only led to unsustainable overproduction.
Ethanol subsidies, and the overproduction of corn it brings with it, are keeping the price of corn low and land high.
If a farmer knows that no matter what, the government will subsidize corn through ethanol, the smartest business choice is to borrow as much as possible, buy as much as possible, and produce as much as possible.
Million dollar implements, and $10,000 an acre land aren’t a reasonable response to actual market demand and prices, they’re a bet that as long as the ball keeps rolling, there’ll be federal subsidies paying for it all.
When the fair market and good sense are overshadowed by free government handouts, it destroys the world where small, sensible farmers grow crops and support themselves.
It has led to a South Dakota where only the largest farmers can compete, and have to be dependent on federal welfare to do it.
In short, if ethanol is a viable, working business, then clearly they should be able to survive without tax subsidies and carbon capture credits.
And if there is no untoward assistance of political campaigns going on, then I’m sure they wouldn’t mind the elimination of PACs.
If the largest, wealthiest farmers can make money on land they bought at $10,000 an acre, good for them.
Let the ethanol industry rise and fall on its own merit, and let politicians and farmers do the same.
Eliminate ethanol subsidies.
Eliminate political action committees.
Eliminate price supports.
None of these policies were for us.
It’s a system where your tax money is taken by the politicians, who give it to the ethanol companies and largest landowners.
And it’s used to back more politicians who vote them more tax money, and the smaller farmers are crushed by the unrealistic price margins.
This year, use the South Dakota Secretary of State’s website to look up the financial disclosures for all your candidates before voting.
Follow the money.
Ask yourself why these people are getting so much from special interests, and who they’ll really represent in Pierre.
Because we do have one weapon to fight this system.
Our vote.
And we can use it on June 2nd and November 3rd, to try to throw out as much of the whole rotten mess as we can.
