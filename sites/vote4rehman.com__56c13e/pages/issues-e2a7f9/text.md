Staying In The Home You Own THE PROBLEM Maryland tells retirees it has their back.
It points to a pension exclusion worth up to roughly $40,600 in 2026 and to the fact that it does not tax Social Security.
In practice, for most middle-class retirees, that exclusion is worth close to nothing, because state law reduces it dollar for dollar by your Social Security benefits.
A retired couple who both collect Social Security often gets no benefit from it at all.
WHAT VOTERS ARE FEELING If you have lived in District 9A for twenty or thirty years you can probably name the neighbors who left.
Most longtime homeowners here do not have a mortgage any more.
They paid the house off.
What is squeezing them out is not the price of housing, it is the cost of carrying the house: property tax bills that climb every year with assessments, insurance premiums that jumped, and utility costs rising faster than anyone in Annapolis seems willing to address.
For someone on a fixed income in a paid-off house, those are the bills that decide whether they can afford to stay.
Social Security is not taxed, which is good, and the senior tax credit of up to $1,750 helps.
But the pension, the 401(k), the IRA withdrawals, the money someone spent forty years saving, are taxed at the full rate.
Here is the part that should frustrate every voter in this district regardless of how they vote.
A bill that would at minimum remove the Social Security offset has been introduced in the General Assembly four years running: 2023, 2024, 2025, and again in 2026.
Every year it is assigned to the same committee.
Every year that committee holds a hearing.
And every year the committee never takes a vote, so the bill never reaches the floor where the full House could decide.
That is not a shortage of revenue.
It is a shortage of priorities.
The counties have done what they can.
Howard County's Aging in Place Tax Credit gives residents who are at least 65 and have lived in their home at least 30 years a credit worth 20 percent of their county property tax on a home assessed up to $650,000, and the county extended it to ten years at the end of 2025.
Montgomery County runs a parallel credit for residents 65 and older who have been in their home at least 40 years, worth 20 percent on homes assessed up to $550,000 for up to seven years.
Both counties acted without waiting for the state.
Annapolis has not matched them.
SOLUTIONS Remove the Social Security offset from the pension exclusion, so the break Maryland advertises to retirees is worth what the state says it is worth Cap year-over-year homestead taxable assessment growth at the rate of inflation for primary residences, which protects retirees and young families on both sides of the county line Require a recorded committee vote on any bill that has received a hearing, so a fix cannot die four years running without a single member going on record Phase the 2024 and 2025 vehicle registration increases back over three years, paid for by tightening MDOT discretionary fee authority and capping dealer processing fees A neighbor who has paid into this county for forty years should not have to leave the house they raised their family in because the carrying cost outran a fixed income.
The counties saw that and acted.
The state has had four chances and has not taken a vote.
Utility Bills and Who Pays For The Grid THE PROBLEM BGE has asked regulators for $156 million more, about $8 per month on a typical bill, and at the same time asked to raise its guaranteed return from 9.5% to 10.4%.
About 290,000 BGE customers were already behind on their bills in March 2026.
WHAT VOTERS ARE FEELING Gas delivery rates have more than tripled since 2010 and electric delivery has nearly doubled, while BGE profits went from $147 million in 2010 to $527 million in 2024.
Pepco, which serves Damascus and Clarksburg, follows a similar pattern.
Both are subsidiaries of the same parent company.
The reason is the formula, not the villain.
Maryland regulators set utility rates on a cost-plus basis: the utility is allowed to earn a percentage return on what it spends.
Spending more is therefore how it earns more.
Regulators can push back case by case, and in August 2026 they did, cutting Pepco's request from $119.8 million to $50.9 million and rejecting one project as imprudent.
Montgomery County bills still went up about 2.25%, roughly $3.94 a month, effective August 29.
Winning one argument at a time is not the same as fixing the incentive, and the incentive is a design choice Annapolis can change.
The same formula decides who pays when the grid has to be expanded.
If the cost of new capacity is spread across every household instead of charged to the customer who caused it, your bill absorbs someone else's expansion.
That is a choice about cost allocation, and the state makes it.
The record in the BGE case is open right now.
Evidentiary hearings are set for November 2026 and the Public Service Commission decision is expected at the end of January 2027, which is the first month the next delegate is in office.
SOLUTIONS Convert the Public Service Commission's rate-of-return formula from cost-plus to performance-based, so a utility earns a higher return only when it hits benchmarks on outage reduction and cost containment Make any large new power user pay the full cost of connecting to the grid and the capacity it reserves, instead of spreading it across every household's bill Publish a plain-English ratepayer impact statement with every rate case, so a household can see the monthly number before the decision, not after You do not need a new power plant to lower a bill that is rising because of how the formula works.
Change what the utility earns money for, and make the customer causing a cost pay it.
Those are regulatory mechanisms already on the books in other states.
And the August ruling proves the record is worth showing up to, because the commission cut more than half of what was asked for.
Every School Dollar Accounted For THE PROBLEM Howard County Public Schools spends $21,512 per student per year and Montgomery County Public Schools spends $21,349.
They rank fourth and fifth highest in Maryland.
Across the state, counties are already contributing $1.4 billion above their required share under the Blueprint.
Neither system publishes a clear, comparable, plain-English accounting of how much of each dollar reaches a classroom.
WHAT VOTERS ARE FEELING This is not an argument about whether to fund schools, and I want to be direct about that because the question gets asked in bad faith constantly.
We fund our schools at among the highest levels in the state, most of it from local property taxpayers in both Howard and Montgomery, and I would not cut that.
It is an argument about accounting.
A parent in Glenelg or a parent in Clarksburg cannot currently answer the same simple question: of the twenty-one thousand dollars their system spends per student, what share reached the classroom their child sits in?
Neither can I, and neither can anyone in Annapolis, because no one is required to publish it in a form you can compare year to year.
That gap is what makes every budget cycle a fight over trust instead of a discussion about results.
It is also what makes it possible for a system to be both well funded and short of money in the same year without anyone being able to explain why.
SOLUTIONS Require both county systems to publish a standardized quarterly classroom-dollar tracker showing what percentage of every dollar reaches instruction, in a format that can be compared across systems and across years As your delegate, I will not vote for a state-level emergency education appropriation from either system until that transparency floor is in place Support teachers with stable, predictable multi-year funding instead of annual crisis budgets that make the profession harder than it needs to be Align curriculum with real-world skills, career pathways and modern technology, and measure whether it worked rather than assuming it did Demanding accountability for how dollars are spent and valuing public education are not opposites.
They are the same position.
Families here pay among the highest local school taxes in Maryland and have earned the right to see the ledger.
A Vote You Can Actually Check THE PROBLEM More than one million unaffiliated Marylanders, 1,005,402 of them, were locked out of the closed primary on June 23.
In District 9A 20,089 voters are unaffiliated.
That is now the second largest bloc in this district, and not one of them could vote in either party's primary.
Only 12,144 ballots were cast in this district that day, so more voters here were shut out of the primary than took part in it.
In 2022 the second District 9A delegate seat was decided by 113 votes out of 66,168 cast.
WHAT VOTERS ARE FEELING Every other candidate on this ballot reached it through a party nomination.
I reached it by gathering signatures by hand, door to door.
Four other unaffiliated candidates across Maryland tried the same route this year and none of them met the signature requirement.
I am not raising that as a boast.
I am raising it because it is the entire mechanical reason I can make a promise the others cannot: no caucus has a claim on my vote, so no leadership office can require me to kill a good bill or carry a bad one.
I will vote against a bad bill written by people I agree with, and I will say why.
The closed primary is the same problem one step earlier.
Turnout on June 23 was 24% here, and the 20,089 unaffiliated voters in this district were not among them, because Maryland does not let them be.
When the real decision happens in a room that many of your neighbors are not allowed to enter, the general election becomes a formality and the people locked out are the ones with no party.
That is a structural problem, not a partisan one, and it is fixable by statute.
SOLUTIONS Open Maryland's primaries so no voter is shut out of the election that actually decides the seat Publish the reason for every vote I cast, in plain English, in the week I cast it, including the votes constituents will disagree with Require fiscal notes on every new revenue bill to show three spending reductions considered and rejected, so spending discipline comes before new revenue Sunset new fees at five years with mandatory legislative reauthorization, so fees stop becoming permanent through administrative renewal An independent answers to one group, and it is the one that lives here.
That is worth something only if you can check it, which is why the commitment to publish every vote matters more than any slogan on this page.
