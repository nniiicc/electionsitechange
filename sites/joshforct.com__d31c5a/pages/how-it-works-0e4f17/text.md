How it works
The party has rules, and almost nothing is written down.
Endorsements, delegate slates, caucuses and primaries determine whose name appears on the ballot.
Everything in politics runs on a schedule, and that schedule is difficult to navigate from the outside.
What follows is how it all comes together in Connecticut politics, and how to get engaged.
The barrier is low, and understanding the process is the first step.
The part nobody explains
How a party endorsement actually works.
Town committee members do more than local organizing.
They elect the delegates who attend nominating conventions, and those conventions decide which candidate starts with the party behind them.
It works this way at every level.
- Your town Local The town committee endorses candidates for municipal office directly — first selectman or mayor, town council, board of education, board of finance.
The endorsement carries real weight in a local race without settling it.
These are also the offices that decide what your property taxes actually pay for.
- Your district and the state State and district Town committees elect delegates to district conventions, which endorse candidates for state representative, state senator, and Congress, and to the state convention, which endorses the six constitutional offices: governor, lieutenant governor, attorney general, secretary of the state, treasurer, and comptroller.
How is a town's delegate count calculated?
Take the number of registered Democrats in the town and the number of Democrats who voted in the last presidential election.
Average those two figures, then divide by 500.
That is the town's delegate allocation — which is why smaller towns carry more weight than people expect.
- The country National In presidential years, Connecticut's delegates to the Democratic National Convention are chosen at caucuses, and any registered Democrat can put their name forward.
Here is the part worth knowing: unlike a town committee caucus, there is no endorsed slate.
Everyone starts on equal footing.
If you can organize enough people to turn up and vote for you, you can go.
What an endorsement means, and what it doesn't
An endorsement carries real weight, but it does not decide a race.
Endorsed candidates can still lose.
- What it changes
- How you get onto the ballot in the first place.
- If you have the endorsement
- You are on the primary ballot, with the party's organization behind you.
- If you don't
- In multi-town districts — every state Senate district, and some House districts — a convention is held.
Take 15 percent of the vote on a roll call and you qualify for the ballot anyway.
Below that, you petition on by collecting signatures.
Petitioning is not all bad.
Gathering signatures puts you in front of voters months before you would otherwise meet them, and it is the first honest test of whether people will listen to you.
It is also tedious, and it is where a lot of first-time candidates stumble.
If you cannot gather the signatures, you probably cannot win the race either — but that is an argument about candidates, not a defense of the rule.
Ballot access in this state should be easier, not harder.
So the inside game does not decide the election.
It decides whether you start on the ballot or fight your way onto it.
How to join
Four steps to a seat.
Every municipality runs differently.
Some committees have open seats and are actively looking for people.
In some cities you will have to work for one.
Either way, turning up and being useful is how you get taken seriously.
Register as a Democrat
You have to be an enrolled Democrat in the town where you live.
You can do it in a few minutes at voterregistration.ct.gov if you have a Connecticut driver's license or state ID, or through your town's registrar of voters.
One catch worth knowing: if you are switching from another party, you do not get full party privileges for three months.
Do it early.
Find your town committee and turn up
Some committees meet monthly, with breaks through the year.
Others meet a couple of times a year.
Search your town's name plus "Democratic Town Committee," or ask the registrar of voters who the chair is.
If you cannot work out who to contact, sign up and reply to any email from us — we can point you to the right person.
Going to two or three meetings tells you more about local politics than anything you will read.
Ask the chair how it works, and whether there are openings
Committees have vacancies more often than you would think, and many can appoint someone between elections by a vote of the members.
Ask what the process looks like in your town — plenty of people join this way rather than waiting for a caucus.
Get on the slate at the caucus
Members are elected at a caucus in January of even-numbered years, open to every enrolled Democrat in town.
It works like a small in-person election.
If you are on the endorsed slate and nobody petitions against you, you are in automatically.
If someone does, there is a primary about six weeks later — and the signature threshold to force one is generally low.
Some towns elect members by district, and a few cities skip the caucus and petition straight onto a primary ballot, so ask how yours works.
Finding the caucus is genuinely hard
Party rules require a committee to publish the time and place ahead of the caucus, and state law sets that bar at a newspaper notice.
There is no central website listing them.
Some committees post on social media or their own site.
Some do nothing beyond the minimum.
You will not stumble across it.
That is usually habit and thin volunteer capacity rather than anything sinister, but the effect is the same: people already inside know when the caucus is, and people who aren't, don't.
These are volunteer organizations, mostly run by people with day jobs.
They need help — and the skills that make you useful to one are the ordinary ones.
If you can build a spreadsheet, write a decent email, run a social account, or organize a room, you are already more useful than you think.
A party grows by taking more people in and making it easier to join.
The cycle
It runs on a two-year clock.
January, even years
Caucuses.
Enrolled Democrats meet to elect town committee members for the next two years.
March, even years
New members' terms begin, and a primary is held if the endorsed slate was challenged.
In late March, seated committees choose their delegates to the state convention and to the state representative and senate district conventions.
Spring
The conventions meet, and those delegates endorse candidates for state and district offices.
The next round of caucuses is January 2028.
You do not have to wait for it — the fastest way in is to start attending meetings now and ask about a vacancy.
