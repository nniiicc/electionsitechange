Housing will continue to be a major issue for DeKalb County, Georgia, and the nation.
I've spent time this summer reviewing the progress that's being made and highlighting issues that need to be brought before the Georgia General Assembly in its 2027-2028 session that convenes on January 11, 2027.
A DeKalb County Comprehensive Housing Plan is being developed under the leadership of CEO Lorraine Cochran-Johnson.
This month I attended the Community Showcase Town Hall at the Porter Sanford Community Center, where the team described many of the activities and plans that are being developed.
Here are the slidesthat were presented at the August 17th community meeting.
You can provide additional input virtually.
I applaud CEO Cochran-Johnson for her leadership and particularly for her efforts to bring the development authority, businesses, county resources, and Housing Authority resources together for major development plans including affordable housing options.
DeKalb County and the Housing Authority have moved 60 families into apartments in Stone Mountain for their first effort in transitional housing for vulnerable families living in extended stay hotels.
This project is a step toward more stable housing, and I hope this effort will be expanded to other parts of the county to assist the thousands of working parents trapped in badly managed and dangerous hotels.
I anticipate the hotel industry will again be at the Capitol to further their legislative agenda to make evictions more efficient for tenants in their hotels.
Among the facts I learned about the issues of housing facing DeKalb County are:
- 44% of homes in DeKalb were built before 1979, and 56% of current housing is single-family detached: DeKalb needs more middle housing availability
- Median home values have increased by 115% in the past 10 years, and the price of rent has increased 86% in that time
- DeKalb residents report that “quality homes are out of reach”·
On the federal level the bipartisan ROAD Act was passed, and despite the President’s threats to veto, the bill became law without his signature.
One of the issues included in the ROAD Act is a cap on the number of single-family homes that can be owned by out-of-state corporate investors.
I have reached out to Commissioner Christopher Nunn at Georgia Department of Community Affairs, real estate lobbyists, and others to evaluate the bill’s provisions and any loopholes that Georgia should consider closing.
A summary of the ROAD Act can be reviewed here – there is clearly more work to do.
I am meeting with many colleagues, including housing advocates at the Georgia Supportive Housing Association and HouseATL and also my co-sponsoring legislators from the 2025-2026 session to review the Housing package the Democratic Caucus introduced and discuss what bills will be introduced again along with new legislative proposals.
Again, we will look for bipartisan efforts that were sorely missing in 2026.
In the coming months I will detail these legislative proposals and ask for your opinions and help.

The Behavioral Health Reform and Innovation Commission is ongoing and is preparing a summary report of all the legislative and budget improvements that have been made since enactment of HB 1013.
I am also spending time this summer with this Commission and its Hospital and Short-term Care Facilities Subcommittee led by Dr.

I am hopeful the study committee on DFCS cost overruns will include an analysis of federal money that is not being applied for or used by DFCS leadership.
In particular, I do not believe we have implemented the federal Family First Prevention Services Act completely and we have left money on the table by this failure to concentrate our resources on prevention.
The Commissioner of DHR continues to focus on increased cost of serving children with the most extensive needs, which is relevant, and complains about unlawful orders issued by juvenile court judges which I am not certain is a true legal factor in the cost overruns.
There is a great deal of energy behind this study effort, and I am optimistic we will have proposals to hand to whomever is elected governor in November.
There is no doubt that there is a need for adjustments in the DFCS leadership in order to create a realistic and accurate budget for the 10,000 children in Georgia's foster care system.

We recently received additional bad news from the Trump Department of Justice relative to its lack of interest in enforcement of the civil rights guaranteed by the United States Supreme Court decision of Olmsted v LC.
Thank you, Georgia Recorder for this good analysis and heads up about what additional problems the current President intends to create.
My concerns are detailed in the article, specifically my worry that the Justice Department officially states it has little or no interest in protecting the civil rights of Georgia citizens with disabilities.
Next governor will shape future of disability care.
State Rep.
Mary Margaret Oliver, a Decatur Democrat, said she was disappointed by the federal memo and called it a “blatant disregard” of civil rights.
Oliver, a practicing attorney and a lawmaker who has been influential on mental health issues, said the memo creates new uncertainty.
“It’s a direct message to the state that the Department of Justice is not going to enforce the civil rights of Georgia citizens,” Oliver said.
“We cannot count on politicians, even on a good day, to always be mindful of the constitutional rights of the people they represent.”
Oliver said many lawmakers are committed to helping people with disabilities but it’s difficult to balance funding priorities.
This year’s gubernatorial race could lead to policy changes across Georgia’s mental health and disability care system, she added.
