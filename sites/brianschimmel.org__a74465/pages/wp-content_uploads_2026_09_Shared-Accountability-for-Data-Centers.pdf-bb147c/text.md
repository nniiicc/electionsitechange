Data Centers, Political Accountability, and the Danger of Finding One Person to Blame Senator Janeen Sollman has been made the sole proxy for a decade of policy.
This is not a defense of every policy choice she has made.
It is merely important to distinguish documented facts from political interpretation—particularly on matters that require a majority of legislators to approve.
Public frustration over the rapid expansion of data centers in Washington County is real, understandable, and deserving of serious attention.
The impacts on land, electricity infrastructure, tax policy, water resources, and the character of our communities warrant scrutiny.
But accountability requires an honest accounting of how we arrived here.
Over the past decade, Oregon did not become one of the nation's significant data-center markets because of the actions of one state senator.
State legislators, governors, local governments, economic-development agencies, utilities, and regional leaders collectively established and maintained the land-use policies, development framework, tax incentives, enterprise zones, and infrastructure that helped make this expansion possible.
Yet increasingly, Senator Sollman has been made the singular face of that history.
That is neither accurate nor fair.
The Oregon JOBS Act was not a one-person proposal The most recent controversy centered on Senate Bill 1586, the Oregon JOBS Act, which proposed bringing 373 acres into Metro's urban growth boundary for advanced manufacturing while establishing a longer-term process for approximately 1,400 additional acres.
The revised proposal expressly prohibited stand-alone data centers on the newly added land.
Sollman described the legislation at the time as a bipartisan effort intended to address Oregon's shortage of large industrial sites.
The proposal generated substantial public opposition, and Sollman ultimately stopped advancing it.
Contemporary reporting confirms that the legislation had support beyond Sollman, including bipartisan legislative support and advocacy from local and regional elected leaders.
That history matters.
If elected officials supported the proposal when it appeared politically viable but distanced themselves when public opposition intensified, accountability should not suddenly attach only to the legislator whose name appeared most prominently on the bill.
Nor should the broader Democratic legislative establishment escape examination simply because one member became the focal point of public anger.
This history didn't begin in 2026 Data centers did not suddenly appear when SB 1586 was introduced.
Oregon's Legislature has spent years shaping the tax, land-use, infrastructure, and economicdevelopment environment in which these facilities expanded.
Many of the same lawmakers now expressing concern about data-center growth served throughout that period.
Representative Susan McLain, whose House District 29 lies within Sollman's Senate District 15, has served in the Oregon House since 2015.
Her campaign now highlights her support for legislation addressing the shifting of data-center electricity costs onto ordinary consumers.
That recent work deserves acknowledgment.
So does the preceding decade.
And corporate contributions to legislators should also be evaluated for potential undue influence consistently across all elected officials.
Accountability cannot depend upon whose name is politically convenient to highlight.
Accountability must be shared I share the concerns about Oregon's approach to data-center development.
Forest Grove has responded within the authority available to us by updating development standards, establishing discrete utility-rate structures, and allowing our Enterprise Zone participation to expire while stronger public-benefit standards are evaluated.
Those experiences have reinforced my belief that state policy must provide communities with better tools to determine where these facilities belong, what infrastructure they require, who pays the associated costs, and what measurable public benefit justifies tax incentives.
But fixing the policy begins with understanding its history honestly.
Senator Sollman should answer for the legislation she sponsored and the votes she cast.
Representative McLain should answer for hers.
So should governors, legislative leaders, Metro, Washington County, Hillsboro, and every other institution that participated in creating the environment in which data-center development accelerated.
That is accountability.
Instead, Senator Sollman has been left carrying a disproportionate share of the visceral reaction to decisions made over many years by many elected officials.
What we should reject is the easier political alternative: allowing an entire system to participate when policies are popular, disappear when they become unpopular, and leave one elected official standing alone to absorb the consequences.
We can scrutinize voting records and campaign contributions without implying corruption.
And we can debate data centers without rewriting a decade of Oregon public policy as though one senator created it alone.
Sollman could not have enacted Oregon's data-center policy by herself.
Our communities deserve that more complete conversation—shared institutional accountability, not selective political accountability.
