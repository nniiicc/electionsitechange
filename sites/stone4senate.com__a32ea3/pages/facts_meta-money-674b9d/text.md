Meta Platforms, Inc. — the parent company of Facebook and Instagram — contributed $540,000 in cash to a super PAC called "Forge the Future Project." The receiving PAC is registered at 1 Meta Way, Menlo Park, CA — Meta's own headquarters. [1] 2026-05-12.
That super PAC spent $70,302.64 on mailers, digital ads, phone calls, and texting in support of Jabo Waggoner's re-election campaign.
Six line items to a single vendor — FP1 Strategies, LLC of Arlington, VA. [2] 2026-05-19.
Waggoner won the Republican primary by 1,180 votes. [3] Meta's $540,000 is the only Alabama contribution "Forge the Future Project" has ever received. [5] Why it matters.
Meta has invested approximately $3 billion in Alabama data centers (Montgomery and Huntsville). [16] This session, the Alabama Legislature passed HB399/SB265 — a bill limiting state tax abatements on future data centers.
It went to Governor Ivey in April 2026 with her stated support and was enacted 04-17-2026. [17] Waggoner is Chair of the Senate Rules Committee — the seat that decides what reaches the Senate floor. Alabama is not where this playbook started.
Three months before the $540,000 landed in Alabama, the exact same super PAC — Forge the Future Project — dropped approximately $1.3 million into the 2026 Texas Republican primary ahead of the March 3, 2026 election.
Meta transferred $1.365 million into Forge in February 2026 alone. The $3 billion reason Meta cares about Montgomery Meta is one of the largest single-corporate capital investments in Alabama's history.
Two campuses are already built or committed: Montgomery data center — approximately $1.5 billion , ~1.3 million square feet after the September 2025 expansion announcement, coming online at the end of 2026. [16] Huntsville data center ("Project Ferris") — approximately $1.5 billion , expanding to 2.5 million square feet in 2026. [16] Combined announced investment: approximately $3 billion .
Both campuses receive state and local tax abatements under Alabama's Chapter 9B tax-incentive framework. [19] The 2026 Alabama Regular Session took the first real step to limit those abatements.
HB399 (Rep.
Hulsey, R-Helena) and its companion SB265 (Sen.
Andrew Jones, R-Centre) cap data-center tax abatement periods at 20 years (down from 30), require large data centers (100+ MW capacity) to start paying state non-educational property tax and sales/use tax on certain purchases once operational, and allow an additional 10 years of abatement only if operators invest in local roads, broadband, water, or education. [17] The bill went to Governor Ivey in April 2026 with her stated support for the utility-affordability package, and was enacted 2026-04-17. [17] Effective January 1, 2027.
Notably: Waggoner co-sponsored SB265.
The Senate vote was 30-0. [17] That's on the record and it should be said.
The question isn't what Waggoner did on this bill.
The question is what a $540,000 check from the company most affected by the next round of data-center legislation does to what any senator does in 2027 — when Meta's lobbyists sit in his office and the next abatement fight lands on the Rules Committee calendar.
Waggoner is Chair of the Alabama Senate Rules Committee — the seat that stacks the Senate floor calendar and decides what reaches a floor vote. [10] [11] He is also co-leader of the Alabama Artificial Intelligence Commission — the state's front door for how Alabama regulates AI and the data centers that run it. [10] A trillion-dollar company doesn't write a $540,000 check into an off-year Alabama primary because it likes the weather.
It writes it because Alabama is where a $3 billion investment is going, because the Legislature is starting to write the rules on data-center tax breaks, and because one of the senators who decides what reaches the Senate floor is Jabo Waggoner.
A note on kids online safety Meta's national super-PAC branding names "parental control over children's online activities" as a stated priority.
That framing has been used elsewhere to argue Meta funds Waggoner because of Alabama's HB173 (a 2026 bill that would have imposed platform-level controls for minors).
Here's what actually happened on the record: HB173 died in the House Children and Senior Advocacy Committee on 2026-02-05 — chaired by Rep.
Ginny Shaver (R-Leesburg), not by Waggoner and not in the Senate.
HB173 never reached the Senate, so the Rules Committee never had jurisdiction over it. [12] [13] HB161 — the App Store Accountability Act — passed the same day (2026-02-05, Senate 35-0), was signed by Governor Ivey on 2026-02-18, and takes effect 2027-01-01. [20] Waggoner voted YES. [20] HB161 puts age-verification and parental-consent requirements on the app-store gatekeepers (Apple and Google) rather than on individual platforms like Instagram.
Meta's national policy position favors this framework. [8] Meta's Alabama check was written three months after HB173 was already dead.
On the kids-online-safety bill that actually passed the same session, Waggoner voted yes.
The story on this page is not that Meta paid Waggoner to kill kids-safety legislation — that framing doesn't survive the record.
The story is a $540,000 check into an Alabama Senate leader's PAC in the same year Alabama began writing the rules for the tax breaks Meta relies on.
That question stands on its own.
Where Spencer stands The issue on this page isn't personal — it's structural.
Elected officials should raise money from the people they represent.
Not from a super PAC that gets 100% of its Alabama funding from a single California company with $3 billion of infrastructure and open regulatory questions in the state.
Spencer doesn't have a problem with data centers.
Alabama benefits from them.
He doesn't have a problem with Meta doing business here.
He does have a problem with a $540,000 check from Menlo Park getting more attention in Montgomery than a Hoover parent gets.
And he believes Alabama voters — not California investors — should decide what Alabama's tax code looks like.
