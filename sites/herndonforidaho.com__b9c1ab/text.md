My #1 Priority
No Idahoan Should Ever Lose Their Home to a Tax
As long as the property tax exists, no one in Idaho truly owns their home.
You rent it from the government, and the year you cannot pay, they can place a lien on it and take it.
A tax that can seize a paid-off home is at odds with the right to own property itself, and ending it is my number one priority.
The property tax has almost nothing to do with your ability to pay.
A local district sets its budget, then divides it among property owners by assessed value.
Your neighbor on the same street, with the same fire protection, can pay double or half of what you pay for the very same services, based only on what someone else might pay for your house.
When values spike, your share of the bill climbs even if the budget barely moves.
That is how a homeowner in Oldtown who has lived on the river for 34 years watches her tax bill triple.
Not because spending went up, but because her slice of the pie grew.
The money to end it is already in the budget.
Over the last six years, Idaho grew state spending more than $1.2 billion faster than population and inflation, enough to cover roughly half of the entire property tax burden statewide.
Zoom out to the full decade and the picture is the same story only larger: straight from the Legislature's own budget book, the total budget has doubled, from about $7 billion to more than $14 billion, far outrunning the cost of keeping up.
Hold spending growth to population plus inflation, bring every appropriation under real legislative review, and redirect that growth to local governments.
No new tax.
No new rate.
Schools, cities, counties, and fire districts keep every dollar, from a steadier source.
We have already proven it works.
Through HB 292 in 2023, which I helped pass by overriding the Governor's veto, and HB 521 in 2024, the state already buys down about $328 million, roughly 13 percent, of the property tax every single year.
The money goes straight to local governments to lower your bill, with no rate increase and no service cut.
That is the model, scaled up and paced so that no community is ever left short.
No Idahoan should ever lose their home to a tax.
The math works.
We just need the discipline to advance the line.

The Plan to End the Property Tax
Your Questions, Answered
These are the fair, serious questions I hear most, from homeowners, from council members, from skeptics.
They deserve straight answers, so here they are.
Will you just have to raise another tax to replace it?
No.
No new tax, and no rate increase.
The replacement is two disciplined levers, neither of which raises a rate.
First, hold state spending growth to population plus inflation instead of letting it outrun them.
Second, dedicate the natural, year-over-year growth in the sales and income taxes Idaho already collects to buying down local property tax.
We are not creating revenue.
We are aiming revenue that already exists.
The money has to come from somewhere.
Where, exactly?
From the gap between what the state spends and what it should spend.
Over the last six years, Idaho grew state spending more than $1.2 billion faster than population and inflation, enough to cover roughly half the entire property tax burden on its own.
Hold spending to the cost of keeping up, and that gap becomes relief.
It is not a new pot of money.
It is discipline applied to a pot we already have.
How will my city still pay for police, fire, roads, and snow plowing?
With the same dollars, for the same services, from a steadier source.
Property tax is just one stream feeding a local budget.
The plan replaces that stream with a state remittance that grows under the very same formula cities use today (Idaho Code 63-802: the prior year, plus up to 3 percent, plus new construction and annexation).
Nothing is cut.
Police, fire, plowing, and roads are funded at the same level, indexed the same way.
Cities still plan and bond for long-term projects exactly as they do now.
Will the state take over local budgets and decide what cities can spend?
No, and this is the heart of it.
Your city and county keep setting their own budgets exactly as they do today.
The state does not review, approve, cap, or second-guess a single line.
There is no state gatekeeper deciding what your town may spend.
The state's only job is to fill the revenue pipe that property tax used to fill, on an automatic formula.
We change who fills the pipe, not who runs it.
Isn't this like school funding, where the state pays part and you levy for the rest?
No.
Schools are partly funded and then forced to run levies for the gap.
This does the opposite: it replaces the local property-tax stream in full and grows it on the formula, so there is no gap left to levy for.
And here is the clean part: with the property tax gone, there are no property-tax levies to enforce and no lien on your home, because the tax that creates that lien no longer exists.
What if a city genuinely needs to spend more down the road?
Then it asks its own voters.
New local spending above the formula would run through a local-option sales tax, approved by the city's own citizens with a 60 percent supermajority, restricted to its stated purpose, with an automatic sunset.
That is consent at the ballot box instead of an automatic escalator on your home, and it is not a lien on anyone's property.
Nobody ever loses a home over a sales tax.
You oppose federal overreach into the states.
Isn't this state overreach into local government?
That is the right instinct to hold, and the plan honors it.
Funding a service is not controlling it.
The state writes a check and walks away, gaining no say over how your town sets its budget.
If anything, the plan expands local control: new local spending would require a direct vote of local citizens, which is more self-government than today's system, not less.
I would be the first to kill this plan if it ever turned into Boise dictating local budgets.
It does not.
How many people really lose their homes to property tax?
I will not inflate the number.
Outright tax-deed seizures are relatively rare in any given year, and the case against this tax never rested on mass foreclosures.
It rests on two things families feel every year: the standing power of a government to place a lien and take a paid-off home the year you cannot pay, and the quieter losses that never show up in a statistic, the retiree on a fixed income who sells and leaves because the bill tripled, the young family priced out by the carrying cost.
A right to property that disappears the year you fall behind is not really ownership.
Even one home lost this way is one too many.
