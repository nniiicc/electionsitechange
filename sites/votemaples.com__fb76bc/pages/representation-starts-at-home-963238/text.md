It is easy to think of state government as something that happens somewhere else.
Tallahassee is hundreds of miles away from Palm Beach County, and much of what happens there can feel disconnected from the routines and responsibilities of everyday life.
In reality, some of the most consequential decisions made in Tallahassee eventually find their way directly into our homes, businesses, schools and neighborhoods.
The cost of property insurance, the quality and safety of our schools, the infrastructure supporting growing communities, the resources available to law enforcement and first responders, the protection of our waterways and the taxes families pay are not distant political issues.
They are local issues shaped, in part, by decisions made at the state level.
That connection is exactly why the role of a State Representative matters.
So, what does a State Representative actually do?
Florida is divided into 120 State House districts, with one representative elected to serve each district.
Members of the Florida House help develop and vote on state laws, serve on legislative committees and participate in decisions involving Florida’s budget and public policy.
Those are the formal responsibilities of the office, but they only tell part of the story.
Effective representation begins long before a vote is taken in Tallahassee.
It begins with understanding the people, communities and circumstances that will ultimately be affected by that vote.
That belief has shaped the way Jon Maples has approached his campaign for Florida House District 87.
Throughout the district, Maples has made a point of meeting people where they are.
He has sat with residents in their neighborhoods, spoken with parents and business owners, met with law enforcement and first responders, attended community gatherings and listened to voters with different backgrounds and political perspectives.
Those conversations provide something that cannot be learned from a briefing document or committee meeting.
They provide context.
A discussion about property insurance changes when you are sitting across from a homeowner wondering whether another increase will make it impossible to remain in the community they have called home for decades.
Conversations about education take on greater meaning when they include the parents, teachers and students living with those decisions every day.
Infrastructure policy looks different when you understand how development and traffic are affecting a particular neighborhood.
Public safety decisions carry greater weight when they are informed by the men and women responding to emergencies in our communities.
This is what it means to bring politics back to our own backyards.
It does not mean making our neighborhoods more political.
It means making government more connected to the people it serves.
A State Representative will not agree with every constituent on every issue, nor should anyone expect that.
Representation does, however, require a willingness to listen, to remain accessible and to understand that an elected official represents an entire district, including the people who may not have voted for him.
For Maples, that distinction matters.
The people of House District 87 should not have to travel to Tallahassee to feel heard in Tallahassee.
Their representative should be doing the traveling, the listening and the work necessary to carry their concerns with him.
When the Legislature is considering decisions about insurance, education, public safety, taxes, infrastructure or Florida’s future, the voices influencing those decisions should include the people who will live with their consequences.
That is ultimately what representative government is supposed to accomplish.
The votes may be cast in Tallahassee, but their impact is felt much closer to home.
That is why good representation begins in our own backyards.
