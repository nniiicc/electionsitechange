Water Security · Arizona 2026 Voters searching for politicians who support water security in Arizona usually find plenty of slogans and very few real plans.
Rick Spargo is running on the opposite idea: that water is not a seasonal talking point, but the single resource that decides whether Arizona keeps growing responsibly or hits a wall within a decade.
Spargo is the Democratic candidate for the Arizona House of Representatives in Legislative District 3, and he advanced to the November 3, 2026 general election ballot.
Water security sits at the center of his platform — and he arrived there by studying the details, not by reading a script.
Why Water Security Matters Now in Arizona Arizona's supply is finite, but the demand on it keeps climbing.
A growing population, expanding agriculture, and a new wave of water-hungry industry are all drawing from the same limited resource at the same time.
Spargo's view is direct: water security cannot be treated as a partisan fight.
It reaches every family, farmer, and small-business owner in the state, regardless of party.
Planning early is far cheaper — and far less painful — than reacting after a shortage arrives.
His Position Where Rick Spargo Stands on Water Spargo spent months studying Arizona's growing water challenges before shaping his platform.
His approach is practical and long-term, built around a few clear commitments: Responsible conservation — treating Arizona's water as a shared, long-term asset with enforceable, common-sense policy.
Strategic infrastructure — investing in the same bold spirit that built the state's water system, planned for the next generation rather than the next budget cycle.
Careful review of heavy users — a rigorous evaluation standard for new high-water-use developments, including AI facilities and data centers, before they are approved.
Protecting quality of life — safeguarding the water that supports agriculture, small business, tourism, and the outdoor recreation Arizona is known for.

Arizona Has Met This Challenge Before The state has a proven record of bold water planning.
In 1903, leaders built the Salt River Project and turned an unreliable desert supply into the foundation for decades of growth.
In the 1960s, the Central Arizona Project carried Colorado River water hundreds of miles across the state.
Both were expensive, long-horizon decisions made by people willing to look past their own time in office.
Spargo argues that same vision is exactly what Arizona needs again today.
Close To Home What Water Security Means for District 3 Families Legislative District 3 runs across Scottsdale, Fountain Hills, Cave Creek, Carefree, Rio Verde, Anthem, New River, and parts of Phoenix — desert communities where reliable water is part of everyday life, not an abstract policy debate.
Strong water policy keeps home utility costs stable, supports local agriculture and small businesses, and protects the tourism and outdoor recreation the region depends on.
A single large data center can use millions of gallons a day while creating relatively few permanent jobs — and Spargo believes economic growth should never quietly trade away a community's access to affordable, reliable water.
Data Centers & Water Arizona Data Centers and Water Use: Rick's Op-Ed Data centers have become one of the most debated water questions in the state.
Spargo laid out his full argument in an op-ed, The Problem with AI-Centers and Data Centers in Arizona , explaining why water-intensive facilities deserve a much harder look in a drought-prone state.
The debate sits on top of a larger reality: Arizona has already absorbed cuts to its Colorado River supply under federal shortage declarations, so every new large water user competes for a shrinking share.
For Scottsdale, Fountain Hills, Cave Creek, Carefree, and the rest of LD3, that makes careful planning a local issue, not just a statewide one.
