Israel & moral clarity Does Adam Hamawy’s Humanitarianism Include Israeli Children?
A humanitarian standard that protects only one population is not a humanitarian standard.
It is a political preference dressed in moral language.
Adam Hamawy’s campaign platform calls Israel’s conduct “genocide,” describes Israel as practicing “apartheid,” supports cutting military aid, and advocates a Palestinian “Right of Return.” Yet the indictment on his priorities page does not name Hamas or the October 7 massacre that began this phase of the war.
The omission becomes more serious when civilian defense is at stake.
Jewish Insider reported that Hamawy opposed Iron Dome and argued that Israelis should “feel the effects of war.” Iron Dome does not select military targets in Gaza.
It intercepts rockets aimed toward people in Israel—Jewish, Muslim, Christian, Arab, and Bedouin alike.
Hamawy’s campaign answered that he knows the cost of war, saved children in Gaza, and wants wars to end.
His medical work deserves recognition.
But that answer does not resolve the policy question: How does withholding defensive protection from Israeli families advance humanitarian principles?
Gregg Mele’s position is morally coherent: protect civilians, defeat terrorism, support humanitarian relief with safeguards, preserve Israel’s ability to defend its people, and demand accountability for American assistance.
Palestinian children matter.
Israeli children matter.
A doctor—and a Congressman—should be able to say both without hesitation.
Gregg’s challenge: If a defensive system saves civilians from rockets, explain why denying that protection is humanitarian.

Show Voters the Lease.
Public accountability begins with your own accounts.
Hamawy for New Jersey’s April 15 FEC filing records a March 9 payment of $7,900 to Princeton Plastic Surgeons with the stated purpose “Rent.” Jewish Insider reported that Hamawy is the practice’s sole owner and that his campaign intended to continue paying the practice $7,900 each month for campaign office space.
Hamawy’s campaign says it consulted a compliance firm, that campaigns must pay market rate to candidate-linked businesses, and that the space was needed for volunteers in an expensive market.
Those explanations deserve to be included.
They also make the next step obvious.
Publish the lease.
Describe the space.
Show the comparable rents used to establish the price.
The FEC payment and reported ownership do not, by themselves, establish excessive rent, personal use, or illegality.
But a candidate who campaigns on transparency should not ask voters to accept a recurring payment to his own business on trust alone.
Gregg’s challenge: Do not merely say the arrangement is compliant.
Give voters the documents needed to judge it.

Public benefits & transparency A Lawful Veterans’ Benefit—and a Fair Request for Transparency Veterans deserve every benefit the law promises.
Candidates seeking federal office also owe voters a clear account of significant public benefits they receive.
South Brunswick records reviewed by the campaign identify exemption-related adjustments connected to Hamawy’s residence.
New Jersey’s disabled-veteran property-tax exemption is a lawful benefit requiring qualifying service and discharge, residence, ownership and occupancy, and VA certification of 100% permanent and total service-connected disability.
The state’s published criteria do not include an income test.
The available municipal ledger does not establish the complete VA benefit category, a reliable current annual savings figure, cash refunds, or wrongdoing.
That means the honest case is not that Hamawy “refused to pay” taxes.
The honest case is that a candidate asking to write federal law should answer a narrow transparency request.
Will Hamawy voluntarily disclose the exemption’s approval date, its financial value, and confirmation that the required eligibility documentation was supplied—with private medical information redacted?
The standard: Respect the benefit.
Protect medical privacy.
Disclose the public facts.

Why Would Anyone Oppose It?
A defensive system that intercepts rockets aimed at population centers is not a blank check for any government.
It is protection for human beings.
Jewish Insider reported that Adam Hamawy opposed Iron Dome and argued that Israelis should “feel the effects of war.” That is a policy position with a human consequence: fewer intercepted rockets and greater danger to civilians.
Oversight of foreign assistance is legitimate.
Debate over military strategy is necessary.
But defensive protection should not be confused with approval of every action taken by an allied government.
Iron Dome protects families while leaders argue, negotiate, and make policy.
Gregg’s position is clear: stand with Israel, protect civilians, insist on accountability, and never treat exposure to rocket fire as a moral lesson for children.
The question Hamawy should answer: What humanitarian purpose is served by making civilians less safe?

Terror groups must not be allowed to exploit that protection—and public officials should follow the evidence wherever it leads.
Reuters reported in June 2025 that Israel revealed a tunnel beneath Gaza’s European Hospital and said the body of Hamas military leader Mohammed Sinwar was found there.
The report is a warning against absolute claims made before facts are fully established.
Two principles can be true at once: medical patients and personnel deserve protection, and armed groups must be condemned when they use protected sites or nearby infrastructure for military purposes.
Serious leadership does not erase either truth for political convenience.
Gregg will support humanitarian aid with safeguards, defend Israel’s right to defeat terrorism, and demand evidence-based oversight of every American commitment.
The standard: Protect patients.
Expose terror infrastructure.
Follow verifiable facts—not slogans.

Public safety What Voters Should Know About Adam Hamawy’s Radical Ties Candidates for federal office should answer plainly for their associations, endorsements, and public record—especially when public safety and extremism are involved.
The campaign believes voters deserve a direct accounting of the people and organizations surrounding every candidate.
Scrutiny is not guilt by association; it is the ordinary transparency owed by anyone asking to exercise federal power.
Gregg’s standard is straightforward: condemn terrorism and antisemitism without qualification, support law enforcement, and tell voters where you stand before Election Day—not after.
Gregg’s line: Public office requires clarity, judgment, and accountability.
Results over ideology.
Campaign contrast Stop the Radical Agenda: Vote Gregg Mele for Congress New Jersey families should not be forced to choose between affordability, safety, and basic individual liberty.
Washington’s ideological extremes create expensive mandates, weaken public confidence, and distract from the practical work families need: lower costs, safer communities, better schools, and accountable government.
Gregg offers a different approach—country before party, results before ideology, and a willingness to challenge any policy that fails the people of the district.
The choice: Practical representation rooted in the district, not a national ideological script.
Reform what is broken.
Protect what works.
Government reform A Chainsaw Is No Substitute for a Scalpel Broken institutions need reform, accountability, and competent leadership—not slogans about abolishing the people and systems that keep the country safe.
Federal agencies should never be beyond criticism.
Waste should be cut.
Abuses should be investigated.
Missions should be clear, measurable, and constitutional.
But eliminating a necessary function without a workable replacement is not reform.
It is abandonment.
Gregg will approach government like a taxpayer and a problem-solver: identify what is failing, protect what is essential, fix what can be repaired, and end what cannot justify its cost.
Gregg’s approach: Reform with precision.
Protect essential missions.
Measure the result.
Every promise has a price.
Promises & price tags “Free” Is Not a Funding Plan Every federal promise is paid for—through taxes, borrowing, inflation, reduced choice, or some combination of all four.
Campaigns too often advertise benefits without disclosing the bill.
New Jersey families deserve the whole proposal: its ten-year cost, who pays, what choices disappear, and how success will be measured.
Gregg will not confuse compassion with an unlimited federal credit card.
He will support practical help that reaches the people it is meant to serve and insist that every major program publish honest costs and measurable outcomes.
Before Congress votes: Publish the price.
Name the payer.
Define the result.

It is to the Constitution and the people.
Gregg believes federal power should be limited, legible, and accountable.
Congress should write clear laws, defend civil liberties, protect national security, and stop handing hard decisions to unelected bureaucracies.
That standard does not guarantee easy votes.
It guarantees honest ones: read the bill, disclose the cost, respect the limits of federal authority, and explain the decision directly to the district.
Country before party.
Results before ideology.
The Constitution before both.

Civic participation If You Don’t Vote, Someone Else Signs the Check Government keeps spending, taxing, and making decisions whether you participate or not.
Every election determines who writes the laws, approves the spending, and answers for the results.
Staying home does not stop those decisions; it simply hands your share of the decision to everyone who does vote.
Check your registration, learn the deadlines, decide how you will vote, and encourage the people around you to do the same.
Participation is how taxpayers turn frustration into accountability.

Affordability Washington Can’t Spend Its Way to Affordability Another federal promise is not relief if it arrives with more debt, higher prices, and no measurable result.
New Jersey families feel federal policy in grocery aisles, utility bills, rent, mortgage payments, and taxes.
Real relief starts with spending discipline, reliable American energy, fewer barriers to building and business, and competitive markets that give families choices.
Gregg will judge legislation by its full cost and its real-world effect—not by the size of the press release attached to it.
Bottom line: Lower the cost of living by addressing the sources of cost, not by sending taxpayers another bill.
Taxpayers deserve an itemized bill.
Accountability Don’t Give Washington a Blank Check Every proposal should disclose its cost, its funding, its owner, and the result taxpayers should expect.
Congress too often measures commitment by dollars announced instead of outcomes delivered.
Programs expand, deadlines move, and responsibility disappears.
Gregg believes legislation should be written so voters can understand what was promised and later determine whether government kept that promise.
That means plain-language cost estimates, measurable goals, regular audits, and the courage to repair or end what does not work.
Gregg’s test: What will it cost?
How will it be paid for?
Will it actually work?
