Nothing Wasted Plan Turning Food Waste into Energy, Compost, and Community Opportunity A Massachusetts initiative for local energy, healthy soils, and lower municipal costs Prepared for Lori Childs | Expanded policy, business plan, and shared-savings analysis Executive summary Nothing Wasted is a voluntary statewide initiative to help municipalities, schools, hospitals, universities, food manufacturers, farms, and other businesses turn unavoidable food scraps into useful compost, soil products, and, where appropriate, locally produced energy.
It preserves the original plan’s four phases, three processing approaches, municipal incentives, and economic, environmental, and community goals.
The expansion adds a business model, Massachusetts and interstate examples, financial tests, and accountability requirements.
The recommended starting point is not a new digester in every town.
It is a practical combination of food-waste prevention, safe food recovery, school and institutional collection, convenient community drop-off, and contracts with qualified existing processors.
New composting, vermicomposting, or anaerobic-digestion facilities should follow demonstrated demand, engineering review, and a viable operating budget.
The strongest opportunity is to buy and operate together, not build the same system four times.
The revised shared-service model compares the same four drop-offs and one six-school cluster at the same 860 annual tons.
If the specified procurement and staffing targets are achieved, cooperation lowers five-year costs by approximately $70,000–$131,000 compared with separate programs.
This includes annual efficiency gains and lower startup spending; it is not an observed saving or a guaranteed return.
Using a $105-per-ton disposal benchmark, modeled mature annual net savings are approximately $31,600 in the shared base-target case and $42,400 in the favorable-target case, versus $20,600 if programs operate separately.
Five-year net savings after startup and ramp-up are much smaller: approximately $8,500 and $69,300, respectively; slower enrollment can eliminate them.
The $105 benchmark is the cooperative’s reported 2025 member-average disposal cost, not a verified marginal 2026 price for any individual town.
(SSRC 2025 annual report1) There is a separate, substantial resident opportunity in SSRC’s published town-by-town biweekly pricing schedule: at 1,000 qualifying subscribers in each of Hanson, Hanover, Norwell, and Rockland, annual subscription charges would total $525,000 instead of $720,000 at the $180 entry-tier rate, a $195,000 annual fee reduction.

The problem and opportunity MassDEP’s 2025 program and policy assessment reports approximately 930,000 tons of food material still disposed annually, 370,000 tons diverted in 2024, and a 2030 annual diversion goal of 780,000 tons.
These figures establish a substantial remaining opportunity; they do not show that every additional ton can be diverted profitably.
(MassDEP assessment3) Food waste sent to landfills generates methane, while both landfilling and incineration lose opportunities to recover nutrients; EPA also identifies prevention and food recovery as preferable to managing waste after it occurs.
The revised plan therefore addresses food waste sent to either disposal pathway, rather than assuming all Massachusetts trash goes to landfills.
(EPA Wasted Food Scale4) The original problem statement remains central: disposal uses capacity, requires transportation, costs money, and discards potential energy and nutrients.
The proposed response is to reduce waste first and then choose the lowest-cost, environmentally sound outlet for the remaining material.
Preserve all three processing options ●​ Composting: Support municipal programs, community hubs, regional partnerships, agricultural inputs, landscaping material, and community gardens.
Require appropriate feedstocks, contamination controls, and product quality. ●​ Vermicomposting: Retain school demonstrations, municipal pilots, and agricultural partnerships producing worm-based soil amendments.
Treat premium product prices and faster processing as hypotheses to test, not guaranteed revenue or performance. ●​ Anaerobic digestion: Retain agricultural digesters, regional energy facilities, and municipal partnerships producing biogas, electricity, useful heat, or renewable natural gas where 3 MassDEP assessment: https://www.mass.gov/doc/2025-swmp-program-and-policy-assessment/download 4 EPA Wasted Food Scale: https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/wasted-food-scale September 25, 2026 • 2 technically and financially justified.
Require a separate feasibility review for each energy configuration.
Composting is an aerobic process, whereas anaerobic digestion produces biogas; these are distinct treatment pathways, not interchangeable names for one process.
EPA’s assessment also distinguishes digestion with beneficial use of digestate from digestion followed by disposal of residuals.
(EPA composting guidance5; EPA Wasted Food Scale) Goals retained and made measurable Nothing Wasted seeks to reduce disposal and methane emissions, expand appropriate renewable-energy production, improve soil health, reduce municipal costs, create local jobs, and strengthen resilience.
Every pilot should report progress against the goals relevant to its design; a compost drop-off should not be expected to generate electricity, and a digester should not claim a marketable soil product without a safe, permitted outlet.
The business plan Customers, service, and ownership The first customers would be willing municipalities and public institutions with identifiable waste costs and reliable quantities of recoverable material.
Private businesses and hospitals may join compatible routes or regional procurement, but their savings belong to their own budgets unless a contract expressly shares them.
The recommended initial business is a service partnership rather than a speculative processing venture.
Towns provide convenient collection access and oversight; school districts manage cafeteria implementation; competitively selected vendors collect and process material; a lead municipality or regional entity coordinates procurement, invoices, and reporting. ●​ Municipal value: Lower the combined cost of trash and organics service where possible, while improving access and reducing disposal. ●​ Institutional value: Reduce excess purchasing and wasted meals, then right-size dumpsters and pickup frequency. ●​ Processor value: Offer predictable, clean feedstock and clear payment terms rather than unverified volume guarantees. ●​ Community value: Return usable nutrients to farms, gardens, and landscapes where product standards allow. ●​ Workforce value: Budget for paid operations, training, equipment maintenance, and safe handling rather than assuming volunteers can sustain essential daily work.
Start with existing capacity Issue a regional request for information before buying equipment.
Ask haulers and processors for separate prices for collection, processing, contamination, rejected loads, container rental, fuel adjustments, and service changes.

Initial financial model retained for comparison Scope and assumptions This is the initial incremental, pre-financing municipal cash-cost model, retained in constant 2026 planning dollars as a $125 disposal-price sensitivity case.
All rates in this initial model are analyst-selected assumptions, not vendor quotes, appropriations, or prices established by the case studies.
The revised regional comparison below uses a documented $105 historical disposal benchmark and supersedes this initial model as the lead planning illustration.
Figures use U.S. short tons of 2,000 pounds; a processor quoting metric tons must be converted before comparison.
The model assumes usable existing collection sites and outsourced processing.
It does not include a new processing plant, land purchase, or new municipal collection truck.
If any is required, its acquisition, operation, permitting, financing, and replacement costs must be added before approval.
The illustrative $110–$150 trash-disposal range, $40–$70 processing range, and collection budgets are sensitivity inputs only.
Historical municipal examples inform the cost categories and operating choices, not validation of those assumed 2026 rates.
Obtain current local bids before using any dollar amount publicly as an expected saving.
Calculation Annual net operating saving equals gross avoided trash tipping fees, plus contracted trash-hauling reductions, plus verified revenues or displaced purchases, minus all added organics expenses.
The expenses include collection, processing, labor, rejects, administration, maintenance, outreach, insurance or testing allowances, and replacement reserves.
In symbols for implementation: net saving = Q × D + H + R − [Q × P + Q × r × D + C + L + O]. ●​ Q: Gross tons collected for organics processing. ●​ D: Avoidable trash disposal price per ton, excluding hauling already counted in H. ●​ H: Actual reduction in trash-hauling or dumpster-service invoices. ●​ R: Verified revenue or displaced purchases; zero in the core model. ●​ P: Processing charge per gross incoming ton, including material later rejected.

Nothing Wasted Plan Turning Food Waste into Energy, Compost, and Community Opportunity A Massachusetts Initiative for Local Energy, Healthy Soils, and Lower Municipal Costs Executive Summary Every year Massachusetts disposes of hundreds of thousands of tons of food scraps and organic waste.
Much of that material ends up in landfills where it decomposes and releases methane, a greenhouse gas many times more potent than carbon dioxide.
At the same time, communities are seeking ways to lower disposal costs, strengthen energy resilience, improve soil health, and create local jobs.
Nothing Wasted is a voluntary statewide initiative that helps municipalities, businesses, and institutions convert food and organic waste into renewable energy, compost, and valuable soil products through composting, vermicomposting, and anaerobic digestion.
Instead of paying to throw organic materials away, communities can put them to work.
The Problem Food and organic waste represent one of the largest portions of the waste stream.
When buried in landfills, these materials: ●​ Generate methane emissions ●​ Increase disposal costs ●​ Consume valuable landfill space ●​ Require expensive transportation ●​ Waste potential energy and nutrients Communities lose opportunities to create local value from materials they already collect.
