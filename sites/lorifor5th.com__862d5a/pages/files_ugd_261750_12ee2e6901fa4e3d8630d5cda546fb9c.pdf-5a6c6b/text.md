The Care Stability Plan Preventing cost spikes by fixing fragile systems before families pay the price OVERARCHING GOAL Reduce the cost of essential care by preventing system failures that push households into more expensive options — without expanding benefits during a period of fiscal constraint.
Core strategy: - Use enforcement, early warning systems, and low-cost stabilization tools to keep care available, affordable, and local — for young children and for aging parents alike.
ISSUE AREA 1: CHILD CARE SUPPLY & STABILITY Problem ● ● ● ● Child care is one of the largest household expenses in suburban Massachusetts.
Provider closures often happen suddenly, shrinking supply and driving prices up.
Existing state investments focus on stabilization after damage occurs, not prevention.
Suburban communities are especially vulnerable: fewer providers, fewer backups.
Result: Parents are forced into longer commutes, higher-cost care, or leaving the workforce.
Solution Policy Components 1) Child Care Early Warning & Supply Monitoring What ● ● Require the state to track regional indicators: ○ seat availability ○ provider closures ○ workforce vacancy/turnover ○ waitlist growth Publish a quarterly regional supply dashboard.
Why ● ● 1 You can’t prevent closures you don’t see coming.
Early data allows intervention before prices spike.
2) Closure Prevention & Continuity Requirements What ● ● Require advance notice of planned provider closures when feasible.
Pair notice requirements with temporary bridge supports (short-term grants or loans) tied to staying open during transition.
Why ● ● Sudden closures cause immediate cost shocks for families.
Advance planning protects parents and workers.
3) Shared Services to Reduce Provider Costs What ● ● Expand shared services hubs offering: ○ payroll & HR ○ billing ○ substitute teacher pools Focus on small and mid-sized providers.
Why ● ● Administrative overhead is a major driver of instability.
Shared services preserve supply without raising tuition.

Price increases are opaque, especially involving intermediaries.
Enforcement and reporting lag behind legislation.
Result: Families see higher co-pays despite “reform,” eroding trust and affordability.
Solution Make recent reforms real, measurable, and enforceable — without rewriting the law.
Policy Components 1) Enforcement & Implementation Accountability What ● ● Require agencies to meet clear timelines for: ○ rulemaking ○ public reporting ○ compliance audits Use oversight hearings to enforce follow-through.
Why ● ● Laws don’t lower costs unless enforced.
Transparency changes behavior without new spending.
2) Price Transparency Focused on the Middle What ● ● 3 Standardized reporting showing: ○ list price vs. net price ○ what insurers pay vs. what patients pay ○ administrative and intermediary fees Focus on public and employer-based plans.
Why ● ● Many cost increases occur between manufacturer and patient.
Sunlight is one of the strongest cost controls available.
3) Targeted Cost-Saving Pilots What ● ● Voluntary pooled purchasing pilots for: ○ municipalities ○ school districts Test whether aggregation lowers net prices.
Why ● ● Suburban towns and employers feel drug cost pressure acutely.
Pilots allow savings without statewide mandates.

Families want dignity and independence for aging parents.
Solution Invest in small, fast, practical supports that prevent unnecessary institutionalization.
Policy Components 1) Fast Home Modification Support What ● ● Expand grants and revolving loans for: ○ ramps ○ grab bars ○ bathroom modifications ○ stair access Prioritize speed and simplicity.
Why ● ● Minor fixes prevent major costs.
This is among the highest return-on-investment interventions in care policy.

Why ● ● 5 Many nursing home placements begin as “temporary” and become permanent.
Short-term help prevents long-term costs.
3) Aging-Friendly Housing Implementation What ● ● Ensure towns implement new ADU rules clearly and consistently.
Promote accessibility features in ADUs and renovations.
Why ● ● Housing is health policy.
Multigenerational living supports families and reduces system strain.

Problem ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ● Elder care providers can fail with little public warning — and when they do, families get weeks, not months, to find new care for a parent.
34 nursing facilities have closed in Massachusetts since 2020, eliminating 3,515 beds — including nearby closures in Bridgewater, Wareham and Quincy.
Finances are fragile: more than 58% of nursing facilities reported losses in both 2022 and 2023, and the median facility was still in the red in 2024.
Ownership is churning: nine out-of-state chains that owned 12 Massachusetts nursing homes in 2019 now control 61.
This month the state moved to revoke the licenses of all 12 homes run by one of them.
Home care is stretched thin: non-certified home care agencies report a 19% direct care vacancy rate and 40% turnover.
Massachusetts has never licensed these agencies; a new law takes effect November 4, 2026, but full licensing rules are not due until November 2027.
Closure protections are uneven across settings: nursing homes must give 120 days’ notice, but newly licensed home care agencies have no closure-notice requirement in statute, and adult day closure rules are written for MassHealth members.
Skilled home health agencies have a state licensing law on the books since 2017, but the regulations to carry it out were never issued.
The warning signs already exist — in cost reports, inspection findings, staffing data, complaints, and ownership filings — but they sit in separate agencies.
We found no state law or published program that combines them into a routine early warning of which providers are at risk; intensive monitoring typically begins after problems surface, as it did with RegalCare.
Result: Families scramble, seniors are moved farther from home or into higher-cost settings, hospital patients wait longer for discharge, and care workers lose their jobs.
Solution See trouble before it becomes a closure.
Combine data the state already collects into an early warning system, step in early with targeted help, and make sure any closure that does happen is orderly — in every elder care setting, not just nursing homes.
7 Policy Components 1) Elder Care Early Warning System What ● ● ● Establish in statute an interagency early warning system led by EOHHS with DPH, the Executive Office of Aging & Independence, MassHealth, CHIA, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman.
Track provider-level risk indicators: ○ financial: losses, missed payroll or vendor payments, loan or lease defaults, liens, bankruptcy ○ ownership: rapid resales, sharp increases in rent or management fees paid to related companies ○ staffing: vacancies, turnover, reliance on temporary agency staff, staffed vs. licensed beds ○ quality: immediate-jeopardy findings, repeated complaint investigations, falling ratings ○ access: admission freezes, home care hours authorized but not delivered, program withdrawal notices Use a tiered response: monitoring → technical assistance → rapid-response team visit → temporary manager or receivership, using powers already in law.
Why ● ● Financial distress and staffing collapse usually show up before care fails or doors close.
Other states already do this: Florida requires an early warning system with rapidresponse teams; Minnesota and New Jersey monitor at-risk nursing homes.
2) Regional Elder Care Capacity Dashboard What ● ● Publish a quarterly regional dashboard showing: ○ nursing and rest home beds (licensed vs. actually staffed) ○ assisted living units ○ home care hours authorized vs. delivered ○ adult day program openings ○ closures, pending closures, and waitlists Keep public reporting at the regional level; protect individual provider risk scores while the state is working with a provider, so the system does not trigger the closure it is meant to prevent.
Why ● ● 8 Families, Councils on Aging, and hospital discharge planners can plan ahead.
Pairs with the child care supply dashboard in Issue Area 1 — one approach to care stability across generations.
3) Closure Prevention & Continuity Standards What ● ● ● Set consistent closure protections across all elder care settings: ○ home care and home health agencies: advance notice to clients, the state, and local Aging Services Access Points, with a warm handoff to new providers ○ adult day programs: 90-day notice and transition help for every participant, not only MassHealth members ○ nursing and rest homes: keep the 120-day notice and add a regional capacity check before closure plans are approved ○ all settings: notify local Councils on Aging and town officials Pair notice with short-term bridge loans from the existing Long-Term Care Workforce and Capital Fund, tied to staying open through a transition or a sale to a suitable operator.
Offer shared services — payroll, billing, background checks, and training compliance — to small home care agencies and adult day programs facing new licensing requirements.
Why ● ● Sudden closures cause immediate cost shocks for families and push seniors into more expensive care.
Orderly transitions protect residents, clients, and workers.
4) Implementation Accountability for Recent Reforms What ● ● Use oversight hearings and budget language to hold agencies to clear timelines for: ○ the 2024 long-term care oversight law, whose ownership rules were approved in October 2026 — more than two years after enactment ○ the 2026 home care licensing law (temporary-license rules by May 2027; full licensing rules by November 2027) ○ home health agency licensure, enacted in 2017 but never carried out because regulations were never issued Require an annual public report on ownership reviews, license denials, and enforcement actions.
Why ● ● 9 Laws don’t protect families until they are implemented.
Transparency changes behavior without new spending.

Why ● A closure costs families, hospitals, and MassHealth far more than catching the problem early — though that savings has not yet been measured in Massachusetts.

Nursing Home & Rest Home Closure Rules M.G.L. c.
111 § 71 and 105 CMR 153.023 (Voluntary Closure) ● ● ● At least 120 days before closing, a facility must notify DPH, residents, families, staff, unions, the Long-Term Care Ombudsman, local legislators, and town officials, and submit a draft closure plan.

DPH closure process and pending closures: https://www.mass.gov/info-details/information-about-nursing-home-closures Regulation text (105 CMR 153.023): https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/105-CMR-153-023 ● Federal rules separately require nursing homes to give at least 60 days’ notice of closure to the state, the Ombudsman, and residents (42 CFR 483.70). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/483.70 Why this matters Massachusetts already requires more notice than federal law — but the process starts only after an owner decides to close.

1.
No Unified Early Warning System ● ● ● Financial, staffing, quality, and ownership signals are spread across DPH, MassHealth, CHIA, the Executive Office of Aging & Independence, and the Ombudsman.
We found no Massachusetts statute or published state program that combines these signals into a routine, provider-level early warning.
Chapter 197 directs DPH to study industry-wide cost trends, not to flag individual providers at risk.
The closest existing tools look backward or start late: CHIA’s facility dashboard is built from annual cost reports (the January 2026 release runs through 2024), and DPH’s enhanced monitoring, as with RegalCare, begins once serious problems are already known. https://www.mass.gov/info-details/dph-nursing-home-actions 2.

How This Could Be Solved Legislatively Proposed bill: An Act Establishing an Elder Care Early Warning and Continuity System (to be filed early in the 2027–2028 legislative session) Section-by-section outline 1.
Early warning system.
Directs EOHHS to establish an interagency early warning system with DPH, the Executive Office of Aging & Independence, MassHealth, CHIA, and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman; authorizes data sharing among them; defines core financial, ownership, staffing, quality, and access indicators.
2.
Coverage.
Applies to nursing homes, rest homes, assisted living residences, home health agencies, licensed home care agencies, and adult day programs.
3.
Tiered response and rapid-response teams.
Modeled on Florida’s early warning statute: monitoring, technical assistance, and on-site rapid-response teams before temporary management or receivership.
4.
Confidentiality and public reporting.
Protects individual provider risk scores while the state is working with a provider; requires a quarterly public regional capacity dashboard and an annual report to the Joint Committee on Elder Affairs, the Joint Committee on Health Care Financing, and House and Senate Ways and Means.
5.
Closure continuity.
Adds closure-notice and client-transfer requirements for home care and home health agencies; extends adult day closure protections to every participant; requires a regional capacity review before DPH approves a nursing or rest home closure plan; requires notice to local Councils on Aging.
6.
Bridge stabilization.
Authorizes short-term loans from the existing Long-Term Care Workforce and Capital Fund to keep a provider open through a transition or a sale to a suitable operator.
7.
Home health licensing deadline.
Requires DPH to issue regulations carrying out the 2017 home health licensing law (M.G.L. c.
111 § 51K) by a fixed date.
8.
Implementation reporting.
Requires agencies to report publicly on implementation of the 2024 long-term care law, the 2026 home care law, and home health licensure, including suitability reviews, denials, and enforcement actions.
9.
Cost controls.
Directs EOHHS to build the system from existing data, to seek available federal administrative matching funds, and to report its operating cost annually.
