THE GOVERNOR’S “104 PERCENT” IS A PER-STUDENT FIGURE The Governor has said that EPISD’s administrative costs rose 104 percent from FY2015 to FY2025 while its enrollment fell 21 percent.10 He has identified neither the source nor the measure.
The figure matches TEA’s financial data exactly when measured one way: General Administration spending per student.11 EPISD spent $9,872,122 on General Administration (function 41) in FY2015 and $15,964,257 in FY2025, an increase of 61.7 percent before inflation.
Over the same decade its enrollment fell from 60,556 to 47,994.
Divided among 47,994 students instead of 60,556, the larger total becomes a 104 percent increase per student, from $163 to $333.
More than 40 points of the Governor’s figure measure children who left the district, not dollars it spent.
Declining enrollment is a genuine fiscal problem, and EPISD must solve it.
But the cost of a payroll office, an external audit and state compliance reporting does not fall in proportion to enrollment.
Presenting that result as proof of waste misstates what the number measures.
III.
UNDER THE STANDARD THE STATE BUILT WITH YOUR OFFICE, EPISD EARNS FULL CREDIT Texas already measures a school district’s administrative cost.
The Legislature directed the Commissioner of Education, “in consultation with the comptroller,” to develop the financial accountability rating system for every school district in the State.12 That system, School FIRST, measures administrative cost in indicator 13: Instructional Leadership (function 21) plus General Administration (function 41), divided by total expenditures net of recapture.
For a district of EPISD’s size, full credit is awarded at or below 4.50 percent.13 EPISD’s FY2025 ratio is 4.20 percent.
TEA did not score indicator 13 for any district in the 2025-26 ratings, so this figure is my office’s calculation, applying TEA’s formula to TEA’s own financial data.14 Among the 114 districts in TEA’s largest size class, EPISD ranks 30th lowest; the median is 4.59 percent, and 84 districts spend a larger share on administration.15 Tested against a ratio TEA published for another district, this method produced a figure about 0.4 points higher than TEA’s own.
In that test it erred against the district, not in its favor.
The same system grades financial management as a whole.
For rating year 2025-26, based on FY2025 data, TEA rated EPISD “A = Superior Achievement,” with a score of 92.16 The State graded EPISD’s finances superior for FY2025, the final year of the period the Governor cited.

IV.
NO SPENDING CRITERION EXPLAINS THE SELECTION If administrative cost were the test, EPISD would not have been chosen.
Of the 84 large districts that spend a larger share on administration than EPISD, your office selected one: Austin ISD.
It passed over the other 83.
Four of them, Houston ISD, Beaumont ISD, La Joya ISD and Fort Worth ISD, are led by state-appointed boards and fall under your office’s exclusion.17 No announced rule excludes the other 79, including Crowley ISD (7.17 percent), Frenship ISD (6.56 percent), Abilene ISD (6.04 percent), Waco ISD (5.84 percent), Tomball ISD (5.55 percent), Grapevine-Colleyville ISD (5.54 percent), Goose Creek CISD (5.24 percent) and Garland ISD (5.16 percent).
Nine of those 84 districts also hold a lower 2025-26 TEA financial rating than EPISD’s A: they spend more on administration and score lower on the State’s own measure of financial integrity.18 For 46 of the 84, the House member who represents the largest share of the district is a Republican.
Not one of those 46 was selected.
The spending data does not distinguish EPISD from the 79 eligible districts your office passed over.
The calendar does: the Governor campaigned in El Paso on the day he requested this investigation.
V.
SECTION 111.003 IS A REVENUE-LAW STATUTE, AND NO REVENUE LAW HAS BEEN IDENTIFIED Section 111.003 appears in the Tax Code’s chapter on collection procedures, and the Governor invoked two of its subsections.
Subsection (a)(1) authorizes your office, at the Governor’s request, to “investigate the books and accounts of assessing and collecting officers of the state and other officers or persons receiving, disbursing, or possessing public funds.”19 Subsection (a)(2) adds that your office shall “perform other duties and make investigations in relation to public funds as requested by the governor.”20 The Governor did not invoke § 111.003(a)(3), the only provision that reaches an institution’s “policies, management, and operation.”21 Whichever subsection is invoked, the report the statute requires is the same, and it is built around the revenue laws.

The concern the Governor’s letter describes is whether dollars “directly reach students, classrooms, and teachers, instead of being absorbed by central-office growth, administrative bloat, consultants, and the web of noninstructional bureaucracy.”25 That is a question about how a district allocates its budget, not about the assessment or collection of state revenue.
When a district does not amend its budget as Education Code § 44.006 requires, that is a governance lapse, and EPISD’s is documented below.
I am aware of no authority treating it as a violation of the revenue laws.
Your office’s announcements call this a performance review.
The statute it rests on describes an investigation that ends in the naming of individuals and a mandatory referral for prosecution.
Either your office believes a school district’s budget practices are a matter for criminal prosecution, or it is using a prosecutorial statute to conduct a performance review.
The Legislature has addressed the second case directly.
VI.
THE LEGISLATURE HAS ALREADY PRESCRIBED HOW THE STATE REVIEWS A SCHOOL DISTRICT Since January 2004, the Government Code has assigned performance reviews of school districts to the Legislative Budget Board.
Section 322.016 authorizes the Board to “review the effectiveness and efficiency of the operations of school districts,” at its discretion or on the request of the school district, and a district may make that request “only by resolution adopted by a majority of the members of the board of trustees of the district.”26 The Board must file its report with the district, the Governor, the Lieutenant Governor, the Speaker, the Commissioner of Education and the chairs of the House and Senate committees with jurisdiction over public education, and it must make the entire report available to the public on the Internet.27 Section 322.016 gives the Governor no role in initiating a review.
Only the Board or the district’s own trustees may do so.28 None of those protections applies to an investigation under § 111.003, which reports to the Governor alone.
When a general provision conflicts with a special one, the Code Construction Act requires that the two be construed, if possible, so that effect is given to both; if the conflict is irreconcilable, the special provision prevails unless the general provision is the later enactment and the manifest intent is that the general provision prevail.29 Section 322.016 is both the special provision and the later enactment.
Read together, the two statutes divide the work: § 111.003 reaches violations of the revenue laws, and § 322.016 governs reviews of the effectiveness and efficiency of school district operations.

THE LEGISLATURE HAS BARRED A FIXED CLASSROOM-SPENDING TEST The Governor’s letter frames this investigation around whether dollars reach classrooms instead of administration.
The Legislature has written that test out of the State’s financial accountability system.
The system “may not include an indicator . . . or any other performance measure that” requires a district to spend “at least 65 percent or any other specified percentage of district operating funds for instructional purposes,” or that lowers a district’s rating for failing to do so.34 If your report measures EPISD against a classroom-spending percentage, it will apply a standard the Legislature has barred from the State’s own rating system.
VIII.
EPISD IDENTIFIED ITS OWN LAPSES BEFORE THE STATE ARRIVED EPISD’s budget problems are already on the public record, because the district’s own oversight put them there.
The district’s FY2025 audit found spending in excess of the board-adopted budget, without the amendments Education Code § 44.006 requires, in eight general-fund functions: instruction; instructional resources and media services; curriculum and instructional staff development; school leadership; guidance, counseling and evaluation services; student transportation; extracurricular activities; and facilities maintenance and operations.35 General administration is not among them, and the general fund as a whole finished the year $5.4 million under budget.36 The district’s outside finance consultants projected a $47.6 million decline in fund balance for FY2026, against the $6.1 million decline in the adopted budget.37 The superintendent has said that the district “was not rightsized to align with enrollment trends” and that “we have taken a close look at every central office position and made significant reductions.”38 Those lapses were identified locally, by the district’s auditors, its consultants and its board, and the corrections began before your office was asked to act.
