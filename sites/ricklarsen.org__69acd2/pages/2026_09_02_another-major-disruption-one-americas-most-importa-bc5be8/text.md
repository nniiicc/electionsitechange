This follows the collapse of trade negotiations between the United States and Canada and represents another major disruption to one of America's most important economic relationships.
So, what does this mean for Northwest Washington?
First, tariffs can raise prices.
A tariff is essentially a tax on imported goods.
When businesses have to pay more to bring products into the United States, those higher costs can move through the supply chain and affect consumers.
That matters in Northwest Washington, where families are already working to manage the rising cost of gas, groceries, housing, and other essentials.
Second, our economy depends on a stable relationship with Canada.
Over the past year, my office has held more than 100 meetings and conversations with farmers, businesses, municipalities, ports and other stakeholders across Northwest Washington.
I heard a consistent message: a stable economic relationship with Canada and Mexico is critical to keeping local businesses afloat, lowering costs, and creating good-paying jobs.
Our region's farmers, manufacturers, ports, and other businesses depend on being able to trade reliably across the border.
When trade policy changes abruptly and negotiations break down, that uncertainty has real consequences for workers and businesses here at home.
Third, this could make a trade agreement that has worked for Northwest Washington less effective.
For years, the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement, known as USMCA, provided a framework for trade among the three countries.
Many goods that were protected from import taxes under that agreement are now subject to these new tariffs.
In July, I sent a report to the U.S.
Trade Representative outlining what I heard from people across Northwest Washington and urging the continuation of USMCA.
I have also worked with my colleagues on the New Democrat Coalition Trade and Tariffs Task Force to call for the agreement to be renewed and strengthened.
There is still disagreement over why the latest negotiations between the United States and Canada broke down.
But there should be no disagreement about the importance of getting our economic relationship back on stable ground.
Canada is our neighbor, our ally, and one of our most important trading partners.
As the top Democrat on the U.S.-Canada Interparliamentary Group, I have seen firsthand how much both countries benefit when we work together to solve shared challenges.
The bottom line is simple: trade can create jobs and lower costs.
Reckless tariffs can do the opposite.
I will keep working to end these tariffs, bring down costs for families and businesses, and restore a stable economic relationship with Canada.
And, frankly, I would like to make the U.S.-Canada relationship boring again.
