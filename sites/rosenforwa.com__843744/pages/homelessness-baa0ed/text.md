Different problems require different solutions.
Intervention Enforce the law to connect people with the help they need.
Treatment Match the response to addiction, mental illness, or temporary hardship.
Recovery Help people reach stable housing, independence, and a better future.
A six-part plan Identify the problem.
Match the solution.
Housing matters.
But for someone suffering from severe addiction, housing alone is not the solution.
Recovery is.
01 01.
Enforce the law so we can intervene Public use of controlled substances is already illegal in Washington.
Having a law on the books is not enough — when someone is using drugs openly, we should intervene.
Current law encourages law enforcement and prosecutors to connect people with assessment, treatment, and services rather than rely exclusively on the traditional criminal-justice process.
Enforcement should create a pathway toward treatment and recovery — not simply punishment.
02 02.
Expand court-supervised treatment King County's Drug Diversion Court shows how treatment and accountability can work together.
Participants attend treatment, undergo frequent random drug testing, appear regularly before the court, and comply with program requirements.
The current program is voluntary and serves eligible adults charged with felony property and other drug-related crimes.
Washington should expand court-supervised treatment beyond that traditional felony model, with access to inpatient and outpatient care, medications, housing, transportation, peer support, vocational support, and family counseling.
03 03.
After repeated drug offenses, court-supervised treatment should be required Washington law currently increases the maximum jail sentence from 180 to 364 days after two or more prior convictions under the statute occurring after July 1, 2023.
I would take a different approach to that escalation.
Court-supervised treatment should be required, with drug testing, support, and consequences for noncompliance.
This is not about punishing addiction.
It is about intervening before addiction destroys someone's life.
Recovery is the goal.
04 04.
Serious mental illness requires serious care Some people experiencing homelessness suffer from severe mental illness that makes independent living unrealistic or unsafe.
Washington needs sufficient long-term treatment capacity and appropriate residential care — not an endless cycle between the street, emergency rooms, shelters, and jail.
Facilities receiving public money should face rigorous independent inspection and outcome reporting.
05 05.
Temporary hardship requires a different response Someone who loses a job or is temporarily unable to afford housing does not need addiction treatment.
They may need short-term housing assistance, employment services, job training, and temporary rent support to regain independence.
The right response starts by identifying why someone is homeless and matching the intervention to the actual problem.
06 06.
Local communities need a voice I support increasing Washington's housing supply, including appropriate supportive housing.
But Olympia should not treat every community as identical.
HB 2266 imposed statewide permitting requirements for permanent supportive housing, transitional housing, emergency housing, and shelters in urban areas, while allowing objective and nondiscriminatory standards needed to protect public health and safety.
Cities still need meaningful flexibility to account for geography, infrastructure, public safety, and local circumstances.
Treatment with accountability works King County's Drug Diversion Court reports measurable results King County reports significant reductions in recidivism, homelessness, and substance use among participants — along with increased employment.
90% had no new felony at 36 months 107% increase in employment at 18 months Figures reported by King County in its Drug Court outcomes materials.
The principle Different causes.
Different responses.
Measurable results.
Temporary hardship Temporary assistance and a path back to independence.
Serious mental illness Appropriate long-term treatment and care.
Severe addiction Intervention, treatment, recovery, and stable housing.
We should measure success by results — not simply by how much government spends.
Recovery is the goal.
Stable housing is part of the destination.
Sources & outcomes This plan is grounded in Washington law, local treatment-court outcomes, and the final statewide supportive-housing legislation.
