A plan to protect Washington's power, water, and taxpayers.
Data centers are booming across Washington — and the deal we've made with them is a raw one.
Trillion-dollar tech companies get our clean energy, our water, and tens of millions in tax breaks.
In return, Washington families get higher costs, a dirtier grid, and a strained water supply.
I think that deal should change.
To be clear: I'm not against data centers or the jobs and technology they can bring.
I'm against a system that lets the richest companies on earth take our most precious resources on the cheap while the rest of us pay for it. $42M Tax revenue given up Washington's data-center tax preference cost the state millions.

A trillion-dollar company just outbid your own utility In early 2026, Amazon outbid Puget Sound Energy — the utility serving much of the 41st District — for the Sunstone solar project, one of the largest planned solar farms in the country.
PSE was buying that clean energy to serve its customers and meet Washington's clean-energy targets.
Amazon took it instead, to power its AI data centers.
Why did your utility lose?
Because PSE plays by rules that protect you.
Its rates are regulated, and half its customers are low-income — so it can only bid so high.
Amazon has no such limit.
When a trillion-dollar company and your regulated utility compete for the same clean power, your utility loses — and when it has to go buy pricier power somewhere else, that cost lands on your bill.
That's the whole problem in one story: Big Tech can outbid the people, and the people pay the difference.
They take the power — and we go backwards on clean energy Data centers consume enormous amounts of electricity, and their demand in Washington is exploding.
Here's what that does to our grid: Washington still relies on fossil fuels for a meaningful share of its power — and our district's own utility, Puget Sound Energy, gets about half of its electricity from natural gas and coal.
When enormous new demand hits the grid without enough new clean generation to match it, we rely more heavily on fossil power to fill the gap.
So every megawatt of existing clean power a data center takes is one that isn't cleaning up your grid.
The clean energy goes to Big Tech's AI; the rest of the grid is left relying more heavily on fossil generation.
Washington already ranks near the bottom of the country for building new clean energy.
Handing our limited clean power to one of the most electricity-intensive industries sends us backwards, not forwards.
They take the water — in the middle of a drought Washington is in its fourth straight year of statewide drought.
Our snowpack — a critical source of our water — is shrinking, and the science says it will keep shrinking.
Water is going to be one of the most precious things this state has.
Large data centers can consume enormous amounts of water to cool their servers.
That's water that should be going to our families, our farms, and our future — not evaporating to keep Big Tech's machines cool.
A finite, shrinking resource shouldn't be handed to a private industry for private profit, especially when we can't even get straight answers about how much they use.
Before we approve a single new facility, we should know exactly how much water it will use — and what that will cost the surrounding community.
The tax giveaway We're paying them to do it.
Washington doesn't just let data centers take our power and water — we pay them to.
The state hands data centers a sales-and-use tax exemption.
When Washington's own independent auditor studied it, the results were damning: about $42 million in tax revenue given up and just 53 permanent jobs reported in return.
That's roughly $792,000 in tax breaks for each permanent job beneficiaries reported adding.
And because no new data centers were actually built under the urban version of this exemption, the state's legislative auditor recommended letting it expire.
I agree — and I'd go further.
We should end the giveaway, not wait years for it to disappear.
We should not be handing millions of dollars to the richest companies in the world for a handful of jobs.
Accountability Olympia had a chance to fix this — and didn't.
To their credit, some in Olympia say the right things about protecting people and the environment from unchecked data-center growth.
But when they finally acted in 2026, they eliminated the exemption for refurbishments and replacement servers — while leaving the tax break intact for new data-center construction.
They trimmed a sliver and moved on.
That's not a fix.
It's the appearance of one.

My plan Protect our resources.
Make Big Tech pay its own way.
Here's what I'll fight for.
01 Communities get the final say.
No town should be forced to host a data center it doesn't want.
The people who live with the impact should decide whether the trade-off is worth it — with a binding community agreement in place before any state permit is granted.
02 100% clean power — bring it or build it.
Data centers should be required to add enough new clean generation to the grid to cover their own demand — not simply outbid utilities for clean power that was already going to serve Washington customers.
If they can't bring new clean power with them, they don't build here.
03 Hard limits on water and pollution — or no permit.
Before a large facility is approved, it must meet enforceable caps on water use and emissions, independently verified and made public.
Miss the standard, and there's no permit.
No exceptions, no waivers.
04 End the giveaway.
No more tax breaks for trillion-dollar companies.
The state's own auditor recommended letting the urban exemption expire.
I'd go further and end the giveaway altogether.
Washington needs its power.
Washington needs its water.
And Washington taxpayers should not be subsidizing Big Tech to take either one.
I'm not anti-technology, and I'm not anti-growth.
I'm for a Washington that welcomes investment on fair terms — where the companies that profit here pay their own way, protect our resources, and answer to the communities they operate in.
That's not too much to ask.
It's just leadership Olympia hasn't been willing to provide.
