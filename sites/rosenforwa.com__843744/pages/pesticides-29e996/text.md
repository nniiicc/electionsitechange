I don't believe the future of farming is an endless chemical war against nature.
Pesticides are designed to kill living things, and we've built much of our food system around using them — spreading them into the soil, the water, and the food we all share, and onto the pollinators, birds, and wildlife we depend on.
There's a better way, and my long-term goal is clear: a Washington that steadily moves off synthetic chemical pesticides and toward farming that works with natural systems instead of against them.
We won't get there overnight.
But we should be honest about the direction we're heading — and start moving now.
The destination A future where Washington farms thrive without dependence on synthetic chemical pesticides.
Long-term agricultural research shows that farming systems built around crop rotation and diversification can maintain strong yields while improving soil health, biodiversity, and resilience.
The path is to phase out dangerous chemicals, help farmers succeed with proven alternatives, and refuse to treat chemical dependence as permanent.
Immediate protections But we protect people now A long-term vision is no excuse for inaction today.
While we work toward that future, Washington should protect people from the dangerous pesticides already in use — and stop new ones from slipping through.
Independent science, not industry influence.
Pesticide safety should be judged by independent scientists with no ties to the pesticide industry.
Manufacturers should pay the full cost of the system — not taxpayers — but have no role in choosing the researchers, designing the studies, or shaping the conclusions.
The industry pays for the system.
It does not purchase its own review.
Act on credible evidence — quickly.
We shouldn't wait decades, or wait for a scheduled review cycle, to act on serious warning signs.
When credible science raises a reasonable concern of serious or irreversible harm, Washington should be able to move fast to protect people while the full review proceeds.
Put the burden where it belongs.
A company that wants to sell a pesticide here should bear the burden of demonstrating, through independent science, that it does not pose a serious threat to human health or the environment.
When significant uncertainty remains, public health should get the benefit of the doubt.
No loopholes.
When the science shows a pesticide poses an unacceptable risk, economic dependence shouldn't override that finding.
There should be no special exemption because a dangerous chemical is profitable, familiar, or hard to replace.
Accountability Hold the chemical companies accountable When a company's product harms you, you should be able to hold them accountable.
But the system is moving the other way — toward protecting the manufacturers, not the people.
In June 2026, the U.S.
Supreme Court ruled that if the EPA approved a pesticide's label, you generally can't sue the manufacturer for failing to warn you about its dangers — even if it harmed you.
At the same time, chemical companies have been pushing state after state to pass laws giving themselves legal immunity.
This all comes after more than $10 billion in jury verdicts holding one manufacturer accountable for cancer linked to its weedkiller.
That's backwards.
Here's where I stand: Ban the worst chemicals — the ones the rest of the world already has.
Pesticides like paraquat — linked to Parkinson's disease and banned in more than 70 countries — are still used here.
Washington shouldn't wait for a federal government that keeps looking away.
Protect your right to hold manufacturers accountable in court.
If a company's product harms you, a corporation shouldn't get a free pass because of a label technicality.
I'd fight to protect and restore that right.
Oppose special legal immunity for chemical companies.
No industry should be able to write itself a shield from responsibility for the harm it causes — and I'll oppose every attempt to do it.
The companies that make these chemicals should answer for the harm they cause — not be handed immunity from it.
A fair transition Help farmers get there Taking a chemical away without helping farmers change how they grow isn't a solution — it's a burden dropped on the people who feed us.
Washington should help farmers transition toward healthier soil, natural pest management, and the regenerative methods that reduce the need for chemicals in the first place — through research, technical assistance, and real transition support.
And the pesticide industry should help pay for it.
The goal isn't to swap one chemical for another.
It's to make the chemical unnecessary.
Prevention is cheaper — a city proved it When farm chemicals threatened Munich's drinking water, the city faced a choice: build an expensive filtration plant, or pay local farmers to switch to organic farming and stop the contamination at its source.
Munich did the math.
The farmer-conversion program cost about €0.01/m³ — one cent per cubic meter.
By the case study's comparison with water treatment, that was a small fraction of the cost — dozens of times cheaper.
So the city paid the farmers.
Today, more than a million people there drink clean water straight from the tap — and one of Germany's largest organic farming regions grew up around it.
Protecting people from these chemicals isn't just the right thing to do.
It's often the cheaper thing to do.
We'd rather spend a fortune cleaning up contamination than a fraction of that preventing it.
That's backwards.
The bottom line Protect people now.
Follow independent science.
Act on credible warning signs instead of waiting for proof that may never come.
Help farmers move to something better.
And keep our eyes on a future where Washington doesn't depend on poisoning the land to grow its food.
We don't have to accept chemical warfare against nature as the price of feeding ourselves.
Washington can lead toward something better: farming that works with the soil, water, pollinators, and living systems we all depend on.
Let's start now.
