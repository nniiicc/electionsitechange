They've also become a major concern for pollinators and the ecosystems that depend on insects.
Washington left agriculture out.
It's time to finish the job.
Not just the bees You've probably heard about neonics and honeybees.
But this is bigger than honeybees.
These chemicals are systemic: plants absorb them, meaning the pesticide can reach pollen and nectar rather than simply remaining on the surface.
That can expose wild bees and other pollinators.
And the concern doesn't end there.
Neonics can reach soil and water, affecting other insects and potentially the animals that depend on them for food.
Pollinator declines are a global concern.
A global expert assessment identifies pesticide use among the major pressures on pollinators, and Washington's 2024 SB 5972 report describes neonicotinoids as a major driver of those declines.
The effects can linger A 2025 study in France tracked insect-eating birds across more than 1,900 sites.
The study linked imidacloprid — a common neonic — use with lower insectivorous-bird abundance.
Even after France banned it, recovery was limited.
The lesson: ecological effects don't necessarily disappear when the spraying stops.
Recovery can take time.
Washington left out the biggest problem In 2024, Washington restricted neonic use on outdoor ornamental plants, trees, and turf, beginning in 2026.
But it left agriculture — where the vast majority of neonics are used — completely untouched.
That includes pesticide-coated seeds, which can qualify for an exemption from FIFRA registration under EPA's treated-article exemption, though the pesticide used to treat them still requires registration.
The small stuff got addressed.
The biggest problem didn't.
What I'd do Phase neonics out of agriculture.
Washington restricted these chemicals on lawns and gardens but left out farming — where the vast majority are used.
I'd close that gap and move our agriculture off neonics.
Don't trust the "safer neonic" label.
Each time the industry offers a "bee-safe" alternative, the research catches up and shows it harms pollinators too.
The burden should be on the chemical to prove it's safe for bees — not on our bees to keep paying the price.
Close the treated-seed loophole.
The single biggest route for neonics in farming shouldn't get a free pass because the pesticide was applied before the seed reached the farm.
Protect the whole food web — pollinators, birds, soil, and water — not just honeybees.
Help farmers make the switch — with technical assistance, proven alternatives, reasonable transition time, and real support, and the pesticide industry helping bear the cost.
Because our bees and pollinators are the foundation of our entire food supply — and no chemical is worth losing them.
Washington did the easy part.
Now let's do the real work — for the bees, the birds, and the food web we all depend on.
