It has a spending and accountability problem.
The money is here.
What is missing is the discipline to manage it, protect it, and prove to taxpayers that every dollar is being spent wisely.
Even Democratic leaders are sounding the alarm.
This isn't a partisan argument.
The people raising it are the ones who have run Olympia for years.
Former Governor Christine Gregoire (D): “We don't really have an income problem, we have a spending problem.” Former Governor Gary Locke (D): Olympia “simply needs budget discipline” — and hasn't shown it in years.
State Treasurer Mike Pellicciotti (D): calls it a “check engine light” moment for our finances.
When leaders from the party in charge are the ones warning about the spending, it's time to listen.

We're not poor—we're mismanaged.
Washington is a great place to headquarter a company and a punishing place to operate one.
Employers are facing rising costs, and nearly one in four said in a spring 2026 survey that they were considering moving out of state.
Strong economic fundamentals do not excuse weak stewardship.
When spending rises faster than revenue and job creators are treated as a bottomless source of new money, the result is predictable: less investment, fewer opportunities, and higher costs for families.
How we got here A budget that grows by raiding its own safeguards Over the last decade, Washington's budget more than doubled—from roughly $30 billion to more than $80 billion.
Instead of controlling spending, Olympia has repeatedly used money dedicated to one purpose to cover another. $1B Rainy-day reserves Withdrawn from the fund that protects Washington during the next downturn, leaving reserves among the lowest in the country. $2.5B Police and firefighter pension surplus Pulled from a surplus built through decades of disciplined stewardship.
Retiree benefits are not cut, but the cushion was used for current spending. $546M Climate accounts Swept away from accounts created for climate and environmental work and redirected to general uses. $375M Public works Redirected from local roads, water, and sewer projects to day-to-day operations. $240M College building accounts Moved from capital projects into operating expenses instead of building for Washington's future. $395M School construction Capital-gains revenue intended by law for school construction was redirected to operations.
Dedicated funds are protected by ordinary statute, not the state constitution.
That means the same majority that makes a promise can break it with a simple budget vote.
A promise is only as good as the next vote.
The warning lights are already on.
In April 2026, two of the three major credit-rating agencies placed Washington on a formal negative outlook.
Moody's pointed to spending outpacing revenue year after year despite solid economic and revenue conditions.
A downgrade could cost taxpayers an estimated $60 million a year in higher borrowing costs—money that should help families, not pay interest.
Accountability should be mandatory We pay to find waste.
Fixing it should not be optional.
Voters created an independent performance-audit program to identify savings in state government.
Olympia has swept money from the very fund created to fund those audits, and lawmakers are only required to consider the findings—not act on them.
Michael's plan Financial responsibility is not complicated.
It is the same discipline families and businesses already practice every day.
Live within our means Do not create permanent spending commitments that cannot be sustainably funded.
Families and businesses have to make tradeoffs; Olympia should too.
Make audits matter When the state auditor identifies waste or inefficiency, Olympia should formally respond: implement the recommendation, modify it, or reject it publicly with a reason.
Protect dedicated funds Money set aside for reserves, pensions, schools, and public works should be used for those purposes—not treated as a plug for unrelated budget holes.
Rebuild our reserves Restore the cushion that protects families from the next recession, so the next downturn does not become the next round of tax increases.
Before Olympia asks for more, it should prove it is spending what you already gave wisely.
Washington is not a revenue problem.
It is a responsibility problem.
When we genuinely cannot afford something, Michael will vote no.
