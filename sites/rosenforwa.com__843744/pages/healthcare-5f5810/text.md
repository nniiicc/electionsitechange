A universal healthcare plan for Washington.
Washington is on track to spend more than $60 billion a year on healthcare.
We're not short on money.
We're wasting too much of it — on a broken system that ties your coverage to your job, buries doctors in paperwork, and pays a middleman to stand between you and your care.
The money is already coming out of your pocket — through premiums, deductibles, copays, prescriptions, and the wages your employer diverts to cover your health plan instead of paying you.
The question is whether we keep paying this much for a system that leaves people uncovered and businesses buried, or build something that costs less and covers everyone.
The honest comparison We already pay for this — we just pay in the most wasteful way possible.
A unified system could replace today's maze of premiums, deductibles, surprise bills, and insurance paperwork with comprehensive coverage and one predictable, progressive contribution.
It would replace your premiums, deductibles, and copays with one simpler payment, and a middle-class family could come out ahead by paying less overall for better coverage. $55B Washington healthcare spending in 2022 $60B+ Projected annual spending 5% Approximate annual cost growth The savings aren't theoretical Spend less on the system.
Put more into care.
A systematic review of 22 single-payer studies over 30 years found that 19 — 86% — projected net savings in the first year, and 91% projected savings over time.
Washington's own Universal Health Care Work Group projected roughly $2.5 billion in first-year savings and $5.6 billion a year once fully transitioned.
Cut the administrative maze This is the single biggest source of savings across studies — a median of nearly 9% of total costs.
Strip out billing departments, prior-authorization fights, and insurance paperwork, and most of that money becomes care.
Negotiate lower prices — drugs, procedures, and hospital care Negotiate lower prices for prescription drugs, medical procedures, and hospital care — using the buying power of millions of Washingtonians, the way other wealthy countries do.
Americans routinely pay far more for the same care — an MRI can cost roughly three times what it costs abroad — not because the care is better, but because no one is negotiating on our behalf.
Cover people before the ER We already pay for the uninsured when they show up too sick and too late.
Covering people up front with primary care is far cheaper than catching them at the emergency-room door.
Cut fraud, waste, and duplication One streamlined system can replace dozens of overlapping systems and reduce the duplication that adds cost without adding care.
A separate Medicaid-style analysis projected higher costs — which is exactly why the design choices matter.
The savings come from aggressively negotiating lower prices for prescription drugs, medical procedures, and hospital care — and cutting administrative waste, not from assuming they will happen on their own.
A different design My plan gets healthcare off employers.
Most universal-healthcare plans try to fund coverage with a big tax on employers — or by forcing employers to keep paying in.
I reject that model.
Healthcare should not be a tax on hiring.
No business should have to become a health-insurance administrator just to employ people.
Tying coverage to your job is a historical accident: it traps people in jobs they'd leave, punishes small businesses, and strangles anyone trying to start something new.
Take the healthcare anchor off employers' backs, and Washington becomes one of the best states in the country to start, grow, and staff a company.
That competitive advantage is the point.
How I'd fund it The order matters.
Every failed plan started with a giant tax and worked backward.
I'd build it the way any responsible budget is built: first what we spend, then what we can save, then what we can redirect, and only then what new revenue is needed.
01 Use the money we already spend.
Washington already pays billions through Medicaid, public-employee coverage, and other programs.
We capture every federal dollar available through every waiver before asking anyone for a dollar more.
02 Capture the system savings.
Cut the administrative maze, negotiate lower prices for prescription drugs, medical procedures, and hospital care with the buying power of millions of Washingtonians, and cover people up front instead of at the emergency-room door.
03 Get employers out of it entirely.
No business should have to buy and administer health insurance just to hire someone.
I won't fund this with an employer payroll tax — that's a tax on hiring, and it's exactly the model I reject.
Getting healthcare off employers' backs makes Washington one of the best states in the country to start and grow a company.
04 Replace your premiums with one simpler, fairer payment.
Here's the honest part: after we redirect what we already spend and capture the savings, the rest is funded by a broad, progressive contribution from individuals — not employers.
But this is not a new bill on top of what you pay.
It replaces your premiums, deductibles, and copays — and the wages your employer quietly diverts to cover your insurance instead of paying you.
Lower-income families pay little or nothing; those who can afford more contribute more; and no regressive sales taxes.
My commitment: the financing has to be actuarially sound.
I will not support a final plan unless the numbers show a typical Washington family comes out ahead — paying less overall for better coverage.
Every wealthy country that covers everyone does it for less than we pay now.
There's no reason we can't.
The honest part Washington can start building now.
I won't pretend this is simple.
A 1970s federal law called ERISA limits how far any state can go in folding employer health plans into a unified system, and capturing the federal dollars we already pay in requires federal waivers.
The fullest version needs federal cooperation that does not yet exist.
But Washington doesn't have to wait for Washington, D.C.
There is already vetted, introduced legislation — the Washington Health Trust — that shows a state can create the framework now, begin enrolling residents, and seek the federal waivers needed to fold in Medicare and Medicaid dollars over time.
My design would differ from theirs in a key way: the Washington Health Trust relies heavily on an employer payroll assessment.
I want to get healthcare off employers entirely and turn that into a competitive advantage for Washington.
That approach could also avoid one of the hardest legal questions facing state universal-healthcare plans: how ERISA limits a state's ability to capture money from self-funded employer plans.
That's not a reason to study this for another decade.
It's a reason to build the framework now, capture every dollar of savings we legally can today, and be ready to move the moment federal law allows.
Answering the hard questions Any honest plan should face its toughest critics head-on.
“Isn't this just government-run healthcare?” No — and the distinction matters.
The government would not run your care.
It would run the billing.
Your doctor stays your doctor.
Hospitals stay independent.
Every medical decision stays where it belongs — between you and the people who treat you.
The state wouldn't employ physicians, own hospitals, or decide your treatment.
What a unified system replaces is the insurance bureaucracy — the dozen competing companies whose business model is denying claims, and the mountain of billing, prior authorizations, and paperwork that adds cost without adding care.
In its place: one simpler payer that pays the bills and, with millions of residents in one pool, negotiates fair prices for prescription drugs, medical procedures, and hospital care.
It's not government-run healthcare.
It's government-run billing — and it's a lot better at it than the middleman we pay now.
“Won't the best doctors leave, or refuse to practice here?” American physician practices spend an estimated $83,000 per physician every year dealing with insurance paperwork.
My plan doesn't cut what doctors earn.
It cuts the insurance maze that eats their time and revenue, so more of every dollar reaches the doctor instead of the billing department.
We protect physician pay by eliminating the paperwork tax, not by slashing rates.
“You can't have the best care and lower costs.” Other wealthy countries cover everyone while spending substantially less per person than the United States, and many outperform us on major health outcomes.
The United States pays the most in the world and doesn't get the best results.
The trade-off critics describe is the one we already live under — we just happen to lose on both ends.
“The real problem is overregulation, not private insurance.” I agree we should cut any regulation that adds cost without adding care.
But an enormous source of waste is the private insurance bureaucracy: billing, claims, prior authorizations, denials, appeals, and administrative staff that do not provide healthcare.
Simplifying the system is how we remove that waste.
“The cost would be astronomical — basic economics.” A systematic review of 22 single-payer studies over 30 years found that 19 projected net savings in the first year.
Washington's own actuaries projected billions in annual savings because eliminating administrative overhead and using the purchasing power of millions of residents to negotiate prices is exactly what basic economics predicts.
We already spend more than enough.
We already pay for the uninsured — the expensive way.
We already hand a fortune to a middleman that adds cost without adding care.
I want to take healthcare off your employer's back and off yours — cover everyone, pay less overall, and make Washington the best place in the country to build a business and a life.
Not a new burden.
A better deal.
