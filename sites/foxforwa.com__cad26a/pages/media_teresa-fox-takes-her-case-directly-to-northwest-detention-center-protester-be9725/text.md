Emily Randall emerges over immigration, public safety and the role of a congressional representative TACOMA, Wash. — Teresa Fox, candidate for Congress in Washington’s 6th Congressional District, went directly to the Northwest Detention Center in Tacoma to do something increasingly rare in American politics: walk into a crowd that did not agree with her, ask questions, listen to the answers and explain exactly how she believes a member of Congress should make decisions.
The resulting exchange presents a striking contrast between Fox and incumbent Rep.
Emily Randall over immigration enforcement, political rhetoric and what it means to represent Washington’s 6th District.
Randall has made the Northwest Detention Center a recurring focus of her tenure in Congress, visiting the facility 9 times in her first term and publicly accusing the Trump administration of waging a “war on brown people” and calling ICE agents “masked murderers.” Fox arrived with a different question: What do the people gathered there actually want Congress to do?
“As your representative, it’s going to be my job to represent all of the voices in our district,” Fox says at the beginning of the video .
“I want to hear what they have to say, if they have any proposed legislation.” Fox also made clear that she wanted the conversation to include something she believes is too often missing from the national immigration debate: crime victims.
And the experience of families and communities whose loved ones have been victims of preventable crimes committed by criminals in the country illegally.
She then walked into the demonstration.
Rather than delivering remarks from a distance, Fox approached activists and introduced herself as a candidate for the United States House of Representatives.
“I’m running for Congress, and I want to hear what you have to say,” Fox told members of the group.
“What’s your objective?” When protesters described alleged conditions inside the detention facility, Fox did not end the conversation.
She asked them what they wanted their congressional representative to do.
“What kind of legislation would you propose?” Fox asked.
“What kind of changes would you like to actually see?
I was elected, what could I do in office?” That exchange quickly became more confrontational.
Fox stuck with it even as a man with plate armor marched confrontationally toward her team, and then circled her team menacingly.
Some demonstrators compared the detention facility to a concentration camp.
One participant invoked the Holocaust.
Others repeatedly pressed Fox to declare whether she personally believed the facility should exist and challenged her when she declined to answer the question on their terms.
Fox instead returned to the principle she says is a cornerstone of her campaign.
“Here’s the deal, and this is a cornerstone of my campaign,” Fox said.
“I’m going to represent the people.” She then described the standard she says will guide her in Congress: “Number one, I’m going to do what’s legal under the Constitution.
And number two, I’m going to represent the majority of the voices in our district.
That’s my job.” When activists continued pressing Fox for her personal opinion, she rejected the idea that the personal preferences of a member of Congress should override the people that member was elected to represent.
“My personal opinion is absolutely zero in this,” Fox said.
“I have no dog in the fight.
Because I’m supposed to represent the citizens of my district.” She repeated the point moments later: “What the majority of people in our district want to do is what I’m going to do.” The encounter underscores a fundamental difference emerging in the race for Washington’s 6th Congressional District.
Randall has repeatedly used her congressional office to obstruct federal immigration enforcement and scrutinize conditions at federal facilities across the country.
Randall recently stated she has been to the Tacoma facility nine times while she fanned the flames of violent political rhetoric.
Randall calls for the abolishment of ICE and accuses immigration authorities of targeting people based on the color of their skin.
Fox is presenting voters with a very different conception of the job.
Her message at the detention center was not that a member of Congress should arrive with all the answers already decided.
It was that a representative should ask questions, listen to competing voices, stay within the Constitution and ultimately represent the citizens of the District.
The differences were visible in real time.
Fox asked the activists where they were from.
All in the group were from Oregon.
One protestor described the demonstration as an expression of solidarity across the Pacific Northwest.
Fox continued the conversation anyway.
“I’m crossing the aisles,” Fox said during the encounter.
“My platform is people over politics.
I’m here to represent the citizens in my district.
And I want to hear the voices of everybody.” Even as the discussion became increasingly hostile, Fox continued answering questions and asking for specific policy proposals.
Near the end of the exchange, protesters again demanded that she take their position on the detention facility.
Fox gave them the same answer.
“As a representative in the federal Congress, I am going to do what’s legal under the Constitution, first and foremost.” That may be the clearest distinction in the race.
One candidate has characterized current immigration enforcement in sweeping terms involving race, fascism and a war against immigrant communities.
The other walked directly into a protest, asked what people wanted their government to do, listened while they challenged her and refused to substitute political pressure for the obligations she believes come with representative government.
For Fox, the contrast between the Fox for Washington Campaign for the People and the incumbent could not be more clear.
Law and order, with justice for victims or chaos on the streets.
The choice is yours in November.
