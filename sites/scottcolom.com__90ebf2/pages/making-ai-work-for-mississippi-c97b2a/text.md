A record 27 percent of American workers now say they worry technology will make their job obsolete , more than double the share in 2017, and the worry runs highest among workers without a college degree.
But so far, the people writing the rules for artificial intelligence are the people who own it .
District Attorney Scott Colom’s position is that Mississippi deserves investment, but the rules should be written for the people who have to live with them.
In Mississippi that is not a theory, and it is not a question for ten years from now.
AI is showing up here as a line on a power bill and a building going up on the other side of a treeline.
That is where he is starting.
Mississippi already has a senator in position to do something about this, but she has refused to act.
Cindy Hyde-Smith sits on the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee .
A bill to stop data center power costs from being pushed onto ordinary customers was introduced on July 16 and referred to that committee , where it still sits.
Its House companion passed 417 to 3 on September 16 .
Five senators have since signed onto the Senate bill, every one of them a Republican , four of them the day after that House vote.
John Kennedy, right next door in Louisiana, is one of them; Cindy Hyde-Smith is not.
That is not an oversight.
It is a choice about who she answers to.
Cindy Hyde-Smith would not put her name on a bill to keep these costs off our power bills.
The industry noticed, and its super PAC is spending on her behalf in Mississippi right now .
We are paying a senator who works for somebody else.
“I prosecute cases for a living, so my instinct is to follow the money and make people show their work.
Mississippi has been overlooked for too long to turn our backs on anybody willing to create jobs here.
But everybody in this state pays their own way, and rich tech companies should not be the exception,” said District Attorney Colom.
“That is why I am calling for an immediate pause on data center construction until tech companies sign commitments that they will pay their fair share, including ensuring jobs are not temporary and paying for the energy that these data centers require without jacking up our utility bills.
Mississippi can do well in this economy, but only if somebody from here is in the room when the terms get written.
We already have a seat in that room.
Cindy Hyde-Smith just has not been using it.” Scott's Plan: District Attorney Scott Colom is calling for a temporary pause on data center construction until these five rules are met: The companies pay their own way.
Mississippians do not.
A company that builds in our state pays for the electricity and the water it uses.
Right now the opposite is happening.
An independent analysis found the average Entergy Mississippi household is now paying about $10.60 more every month because of data center investments.
That is roughly $127 a year, and about $38 million taken from Mississippi households statewide through March, on track for $74 million by the end of this year .
That is money out of Mississippi families’ pockets for somebody else’s power, and nobody asked us.
Show us the deal before the concrete is poured.
Make public what a project will draw from our grid and our water system, and what it will pay — in plain language and public forums — while local communities can still do something about it.
Today we are not allowed to see it — state law lets these agreements stay confidential , and in 2024 the Legislature waived parts of Public Service Commission oversight for the Amazon projects .
The same fight is playing out over taxes: Madison County’s assessor valued five Amazon buildings at about $292 million each, Amazon said $69 million and would not open its construction costs, and when the county backed its assessor and denied Amazon’s appeal, millions of dollars stayed with local schools.
One assessor doing the job was all it took.
Mississippi should not have to get lucky to keep our dollars where they belong: in our communities.
Hire Mississippi workers, and put it in writing.
The good jobs are the whole reason to want these projects here.
The construction jobs and the permanent jobs must go to people who already live here, at a wage you can raise a family on.
And not simply a promise at a ribbon cutting — in the contract, with a penalty for breaking it.
If AI touches our lives, a real person must answer for it.
What crosses a prosecutor’s desk today is AI used to steal — scam calls that empty a retirement account, fake images made of real people, including children.
That is already a crime, but most local prosecutors do not have the tools or the training to make the case.
District Attorney Colom will fight to get it to them.
And when an automated system is handling our money, our health care or our benefits, we should be told, and a real person should have to answer for the decision.
Mississippi decides what gets built in Mississippi.
Clarksdale rezoned land for a data center and set its own terms ; Jackson put a six-month hold on data centers while it works out its own rules.
Both decisions are right, because both are theirs to make.
There is a push in Washington to take that authority away from states and communities, and District Attorney Colom will oppose it.
