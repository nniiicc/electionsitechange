Jackson, MS – In case you missed it, MS NOW political reporter Nnamdi Egwuonwu just wrote a new story spotlighting District Attorney Scott Colom’s U.S.
Senate campaign and his path to victory this November as Mississippi voters grow increasingly angry with incumbent Hyde-Smith’s record of raising costs for Mississippi families, supporting the Iran War, and blocking the release of the Epstein files.
On National Voter Registration Day, Egwuonwu joined Colom on the campaign trail at two Mississippi HBCUs for the launch of Mississippi Matters Mobile, the campaign’s aggressive new voter registration and mobilization campaign.
Egwuonwu spoke to attendees who voiced support for Colom’s plans to lower costs for families, save our healthcare and hospitals, and bring good jobs home, and why his campaign is motivating them to vote this November, with one student saying that his conversation with Colom reminded him that “a vote can change anything and everything.”

Key highlights:
- District Attorney Scott Colom believes he can become Mississippi’s first Democratic senator in nearly 45 years.
His confidence, he told MS NOW, comes down to math.
- In 2023, Democrat Brandon Presley came within 27,000 votes — less than three percentage points — of unseating Gov.
Tate Reeves.
Two years later, Democrats broke a supermajority in the state Senate by flipping two seats in newly drawn, majority-Black districts following court-ordered special elections.
- Then came Colom’s own primary in March, when Democratic turnout rose by 70% — an increase of more than 62,000 raw votes — compared to 2018, the last time a Senate seat was up during a midterm year.
- Taken together, he argued, the trend lines point to an energetic Democratic base slowly waking up after being dormant for decades when it had largely resigned itself to Republican control of every statewide and federal office.
- Colom has held more than 50 town halls.
He has appeared at conservative gatherings like the Neshoba County Fair, canvassed public housing complexes and worked music festivals and sporting events in search of low-propensity voters.
- The current political environment, Colom said, has placed the “dream” of a blue Mississippi within reach.
- Republican candidates have been left to defend an unpopular war that has driven up fuel prices and a tariff regime that could cost the average Mississippi household more than $2,000 a year.
In a state with one of the nation’s highest poverty rates and the lowest per capita income, Colom is betting that the cost of living can move apathetic voters — and even some committed Republicans — to consider his campaign.
- “There’s a lot of working-class people — small, small business owners, farmers that are getting really, really hurt because of these tariffs,” Colom said.
“People of Mississippi are getting betrayed, and they know it, and they’re looking for the direction.”
- Riding in a food truck emblazoned with “Scott for Senate,” he toured two historically Black colleges and universities, passing out tacos while staff registered students and alumni to vote.
The food truck allowed students to pick their own protein — a reference to a widely criticized remark by Hyde-Smith, who said residents worried about rising beef prices should “choose” an alternative source of protein.
- Jayse Thompson, a sophomore at Alcorn State University who hopes to become a radiation technician, registered after meeting Colom and picking up a taco.
The conversation, he said, reminded him that “a vote can change anything and everything.”
- “When the country ignores places like Mississippi, inevitably, it costs the country,” Colom told MS NOW.
“For too long, we’ve had people in D.C. — national Democrats — that have ignored Mississippi.
They gave it up.
They said we’re not going to even try to compete there.
And the result of that has been terrible policies.”
- Less than 50 days out from the November midterms, Colom said he is as optimistic as he was the night he won his primary.
It is, he insisted, all in the numbers.
- “Go do the research.
The math makes sense for Mississippi.
We have to compete in places like this, because we’re not going to be a national party if we don’t understand that Mississippi has to matter,” Colom said.”
