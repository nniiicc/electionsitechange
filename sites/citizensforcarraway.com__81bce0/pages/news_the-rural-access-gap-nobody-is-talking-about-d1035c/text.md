Not just Democrats, but rural Republicans also worked session after session, voting the same way on the same bills.
The Democrats opposed vouchers on principle.
The rural Republicans opposed them on math.
In a district with no private school within an hour's drive, a voucher isn’t a choice.
It’s a line item that moves money out of the only school in town.
Rural Republicans understood this because their constituents told them so, and for years they voted accordingly.
They weren’t ideologically opposed to school choice.
They were opposed to a policy that took funding from the schools their communities actually depended on and offered nothing usable in return.
That coalition is the reason vouchers failed in Texas for as long as they did.
It is also the coalition Governor Abbott had to break apart in the 2024 Republican primaries to finally pass SB 2.
Now that the program is operating, the concern those rural Republicans raised has a number attached to it.
More than 150 of Texas's 254 counties have no enrolled school in the TEFA program.
Not one elementary school.
Not one junior high.
Not one high school.
According to data from the Texas Comptroller's office, which administers the program, families in the majority of Texas counties have no practical way to use a voucher even if they qualify for one.
The legal framework calls TEFA a universal eligibility program.
The marketing language used by the program's supporters has been built around that universal framing, and the application data released by the Comptroller's office shows the message landed.
More than 274,000 families applied in the first cycle.
But eligibility isn’t the same as access.
For a family in Lipscomb County or Loving County or any of the more than 150 Texas counties without an enrolled school, the voucher exists in theory.
But the school it would pay for doesn’t.
The public school in town remains the only option, and the funding pressure created by the program continues to fall on that public school regardless.
This isn’t an oversight in the program design.
It is a structural feature of how vouchers work in a state the size of Texas.
Private schools cluster in cities and suburbs because that is where the demand and the tuition base support them.
They do not exist in most of rural Texas because the population and the economics do not sustain them.
A voucher program does not change that math.
It just sends public dollars to families who happen to live where private schools already are.
The application data underscores the geographic concentration.
Of the roughly 1,700 schools enrolled in TEFA at launch, the overwhelming majority are located in or near the state's major metropolitan areas.
The Dallas-Fort Worth, Houston, San Antonio, and Austin regions account for the bulk of participating schools.
Wide stretches of West Texas, the Panhandle, East Texas, and the Rio Grande Valley have few or none.
The numbers also show what happens when a program is built around a market that does not exist everywhere.
Forty-two percent of enrolled private schools have a 2026-27 tuition that exceeds the $10,474 voucher award, meaning even families with geographic access face a tuition gap they have to cover themselves.
For rural families, the geographic gap comes first.
For urban and suburban families at higher tuition schools, the affordability gap comes second.
For most Texas families, one or both gaps are the actual experience of the program.
Not much of a choice, is it?
The rural Republicans who blocked vouchers in 2021, 2022, and 2023 were warning about exactly this.
They weren’t making an abstract argument.
They were describing the districts they represented and the schools their constituents sent their children to.
They were the closest thing this debate had to a built-in reality check, and they were politically removed from the conversation before the bill passed.
I have taught middle school in Collin County for fourteen years.
Collin County is not rural.
We have private schools, including ones now enrolled in TEFA.
The geographic concern in this post is not my district's concern in the same direct way it is for a teacher in Childress or Presidio or Newton.
But I know what a single public school means to a community that has only one.
I know that no voucher program in the world is going to put a private school in a county that does not have one.
The legislators who warned about this were not wrong.
They were inconvenient.
And they were replaced.
Universal eligibility was the language used to sell this program.
The county map is what the program actually looks like.
