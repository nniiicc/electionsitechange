Texas' budget has grown faster than population and inflation combined — expanding like the most progressive states in the country.
State Budget Growth under the incumbent's tenure: • 2014–2018: up 9.9% • 2018–2022: up 15.2% • 2022–2026: up 42.7% Since 2022–23 alone, total spending is up more than 27% — and state-funds spending is up more than 42%.
Government has only a few ways to fund that growth: tax more, borrow more, or grow the economy enough that existing rates bring in more revenue.
Lower tax burdens tend to grow revenue over time, not shrink it — because lower rates fuel economic growth, and growth expands the tax base.
Every dollar the state doesn't take is a dollar a Texan gets to decide how to use.
Every tax increase, however small, is a small transfer of freedom from citizen to state.
Property tax is the clearest example: money spent by government is spent once, by people who didn't earn it and don't feel its cost.
Money left with individuals and businesses is spent by the people who earned it — on what they actually need — and does more for growth and liberty in private hands than sitting in a government account.
This isn't a plan to cut services — it's a plan to fund government the way our Constitution already allows, grow the economy instead of the tax bill, and let Texas families keep more of what they earn.
The plan: phase out property taxes in 3–5 years.
The Plan Replace repetitive annual taxation — Texans shouldn't pay a tax on the same home every year for as long as they own it.
Move to a one-time transaction tax — collect revenue only at the point of sale, taxed once, rather than billing homeowners annually for what they already own.
Fund schools constitutionally, not through property tax — enforce Article 7, §3 (automatic occupation-tax revenue to schools) and use the existing $# billion Permanent School Fund, so classrooms are funded without leaning on homeowners.

Data Centers and Infrastructure Texas is becoming the nation's data-center hub — and ordinary Texans are paying the price.
These facilities strain the power grid and water supply, can displace rural communities through aggressive land acquisition, and raise real privacy and surveillance concerns when run by corporations or foreign-linked entities with little state oversight.
The Plan Land & water: Thousands of acres pulled out of production, wildlife displaced, and citizens reporting compromised water and power on their own property.
Hidden records: A public-records exemption (Gov't Code §552.1331) is being used to withhold data centers' water-use records — the public can't see what's happening to its own water.
Empty economic promises: Many facilities receive major tax abatements and sales-tax exemptions while creating few permanent jobs or lasting tax revenue.
Financial exposure: Data-center lending has reached roughly $1.3 trillion — about 3.4% of the national debt — much of it tied to optimistic demand forecasts.
If that debt turns bad, are taxpayers left holding it?
Foreign components either way: ~5,000 planned U.S. data centers rely on Chinese-made components — undercutting the argument that opposing these projects is itself "foreign influence." Health impacts ignored: Residents near facilities report vertigo, nausea, insomnia, and headaches from constant low-frequency noise.
An unprepared grid: Developers lean on taxpayer-funded, Chinese-made transformers instead of building their own power capacity — with no honest accounting of water use.
The solution — a moratorium and a special session: Pause new and in-progress data-center approvals so Texas can honestly assess actual power and water consumption, taxpayer burden (abatements, exemptions, debt exposure), health and environmental impacts, and what data is being stored, and for whom.
