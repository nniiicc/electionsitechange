Home Hot Topics About Doug In the News Endorsements Contact More Home Hot Topics About Doug In the News Endorsements Contact Colchester Property Taxes: What Happened in 2024 and 2026 Property taxes are one of the biggest financial pressures facing Vermont families — and Colchester has felt that strain more than most.
For years, Montpelier has allowed government spending to grow faster than Vermonters’ paychecks, and the result has been predictable: higher taxes, higher costs, and fewer families able to afford to stay in the communities they love.
In 2024, lawmakers pushed through a major property tax increase by overriding Governor Scott’s veto of the statewide yield bill.
That decision locked in a 14.1% increase for Colchester , one of the largest jumps in recent memory.
It was a clear signal that the Legislature was more committed to expanding spending than protecting taxpayers.
Now, just a short time later, Vermonters are facing another significant increase — this time a proposed 7% statewide hike .
And once again, all four of Colchester’s State Representatives have supported the legislation moving this increase forward.
Even though the second increase is still proposed , the message is the same: when costs rise, Montpelier’s first instinct is to take more from Vermonters rather than reform the system, control spending, or prioritize affordability.
These back‑to‑back tax pressures show exactly why so many families, seniors, and young people feel squeezed.
Vermont’s affordability crisis isn’t an accident — it’s the direct result of policy choices that prioritize government growth over household stability.
Colchester residents are living with the consequences every time they open their tax bill or try to plan for the future.
Many Vermonters believe it’s time for a different approach — one that respects taxpayers, reins in spending, and recognizes that working families cannot be treated as an endless source of revenue.
The decisions made in Montpelier matter, and they have real impacts on the people who call Colchester home.
2024: A 14.1% Increase After the Veto Override In 2024, Colchester residents experienced a 14.1% increase in property taxes .
This happened after the legislative supermajority voted to override Governor Scott’s veto of the statewide “yield bill,” which determines school property tax rates.
The override locked in one of the largest statewide increases in years, and Colchester taxpayers felt the full impact.
2026: Another Increase — 7% average Statewide During the 2026 legislative session , lawmakers approved another property tax increase, with the statewide average rising by 7% .
Importantly, all four of Colchester’s State Representatives voted in favor of this increase .
In my district that's Reps Pezzo and Bishop.
This means that in just two legislative cycles, Colchester residents could be hit with another substantial tax hike , both supported by the town’s full democratic delegation.
Why This Matters Property taxes affect every homeowner, renter, and business in our community.

A UNH team, led by political science professor Andrew Smith, surveyed 981 Vermonters from across the state and various political and educational backgrounds.
The results showed that Vermont’s departure rate among young people is higher than in any other New England state surveyed — 28% in New Hampshire, 44% in Connecticut, and 63% in Vermont.

Implications of 30% (Not 63%) of Vermont’s Population Leaving in 5 yrs If 30% of Vermont’s population were to leave in the next five years, the state would face profound demographic, economic, and social consequences —far beyond the current 0.3% annual decline.
1.
Demographic Collapse Rapid aging : Vermont already has the third-highest median age in the U.S.
(43.5 years) A #% exodus would remove a large share of working-age and younger residents, accelerating the shift toward a population dominated by older adults.
Workforce shrinkage : The prime working-age group (20–64) drives most economic activity Losing 30% of the population would mean a massive drop in labor supply, reducing productivity and economic output.
Dependency ratio : More elderly people would require more healthcare and social services, while fewer young people would mean fewer future workers to support them.
2.
Economic Impact Reduced consumption : Fewer residents mean less demand for goods, services, housing, and infrastructure, leading to slower economic growth and potential business closures.
Labor market strain : With fewer workers, wages could rise for remaining jobs, but productivity per worker would fall, and industries reliant on a large labor pool (e.g., manufacturing, agriculture) could contract.
Tax base erosion : Lower population means fewer tax revenues, straining public services and limiting investment in housing, education, and infrastructure.
3.
Housing and Infrastructure Housing demand collapse : Fewer residents would reduce demand for housing, potentially leading to vacancy rates and declining property values.
Infrastructure underuse : Roads, schools, and utilities would face underutilization, increasing per-capita costs and reducing efficiency.
4.
Social and Cultural Effects Community decline : Towns and rural areas could shrink to the point of becoming economically unviable, leading to depopulation and loss of local culture.
Service shortages : Healthcare, childcare, and retail would struggle to maintain adequate coverage.
Loss of diversity : Vermont’s population is already aging and increasingly from “blue” states; a 30% loss could further reduce diversity and alter the state’s cultural fabric.
5.
Policy and Governance Challenges Regulatory paralysis : Vermont’s housing supply is constrained by strict land-use rules, making it harder to attract new residents to offset losses.
Migration patterns : Current data shows most Vermonters move to “red” states, so reversing this trend would require targeted incentives and economic competitiveness.
Long-term planning : State and local governments would need to rethink growth strategies, possibly shifting toward remote work, regional economies, and intergenerational housing models.
Bottom line: A 30% population loss in five years would transform Vermont from a relatively stable, aging state into a rapidly depopulating, economically strained, and socially fragmented region .
It would require unprecedented policy intervention, economic restructuring, and possibly a redefinition of what “Vermont” means in the next generation.
