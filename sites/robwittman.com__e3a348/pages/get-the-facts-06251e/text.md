LOOK AT THE FULL RECORD
When deciding who should represent Virginia’s first district, it is crucial to have all the facts and not just political talking points spun by Shannon Taylor.
HEALTH CARE COSTS, MEDICAID AND ACA PREMIUM TAX CREDITS
Rob repeatedly fought to protect Medicaid for vulnerable Americans, fought to secure a major new investment in rural health care that is delivering nearly $190 million to Virginia, and was one of just 17 House Republicans — and the only Virginia House Republican — to vote to extend the enhanced ACA premium tax credits through 2028.
Throughout consideration of H.R. 1, Rob repeatedly urged congressional leaders to protect both vulnerable Medicaid beneficiaries and the hospitals and health care providers that serve them.
In April 2025, Rob joined colleagues in telling House leadership that “balancing the federal budget must not come at the expense of those who depend on these benefits for their health and economic security.” The letter called for protecting Medicaid coverage for seniors, children, pregnant women, parents with young children, individuals with disabilities, and other vulnerable Americans while pursuing reforms to improve program integrity and strengthen Medicaid’s long-term sustainability.
Rob intervened again in June as the House and Senate negotiated the final legislation.
In a second letter to congressional leaders, Rob and his colleagues raised concerns about proposed changes affecting hospitals and Medicaid beneficiaries, including provider taxes, state-directed payments, implementation timelines, community-engagement requirements, and emergency Medicaid funding.
They made clear that they could not support a final bill that threatened access to coverage or jeopardized the stability of hospitals and providers.
The final legislation included reforms focused on eligibility verification, program integrity, preventing improper enrollment, and community-engagement requirements for certain able-bodied adults without dependent children or caregiving responsibilities.
Those requirements do not apply to every Medicaid beneficiary and include exemptions and exceptions for specified populations.
H.R. 1 also created the $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program to address longstanding challenges facing rural health care.
Unlike programs that primarily reimburse providers based on the volume of services delivered, the program allows states to make strategic investments in workforce recruitment and retention, technology, facility modernization, behavioral health, maternal health, primary care, and new models of care designed to strengthen rural health care over the long term.
Virginia received $189,544,888 for 2026 through the program.
Rob also joined other members of Virginia’s congressional delegation in formally supporting the Commonwealth’s application for the funding.
Virginia’s approved plan is expected to improve health care access and outcomes for more than 1.5 million rural Virginians through workforce development, technological innovation, wellness initiatives, and sustainable access to care.
Rob also broke with most House Republicans on ACA premium assistance.
In January 2026, he voted for H.R. 1834 to extend the enhanced ACA premium tax credits through 2028.
He was one of only 17 House Republicans nationwide — and the only Republican in Virginia’s House delegation — to vote for the extension.
Before serving in Congress, Rob earned a master’s degree in public health and spent more than 26 years working for the Virginia Department of Health.
Throughout his time in Congress, he has maintained regular engagement with hospitals, physicians, community health centers, behavioral-health providers, nurses, EMS personnel, and other health care professionals across the First District.
Rob has also supported greater health care and prescription-drug price transparency, reforms to prior authorization and insurance-claim denials, lower-cost biosimilars, telehealth expansion, and stronger rural and community health care systems.
His approach is focused on protecting access for vulnerable patients while pursuing reforms intended to lower costs, increase transparency, strengthen providers, and keep patients and their doctors at the center of health care decisions.
THE HOSPITAL CLOSURE CLAIM
Rob’s opponent claims Rob’s vote for H.R. 1 is putting hospitals in Virginia’s First District at risk of closing.
The facts require important context.
No hospital in Virginia’s First District has closed because of H.R. 1.
A 2026 report from Virginia’s Joint Commission on Health Care identified two rural hospitals in the First District, Bon Secours Rappahannock General Hospital and VCU Health Tappahannock Hospital, as financially vulnerable under a national rural-hospital risk methodology.
The analysis relied on FY 2024–25 financial data from before H.R. 1 became law.
The risk designation therefore does not establish that H.R. 1 created the underlying financial condition of either hospital.
Hospital leaders have also publicly affirmed their commitment to these facilities.
VCU Health CEO Dr.
Marlon Levy has stated that VCU Health does not intend to close its rural hospitals, including VCU Health Tappahannock.
Bon Secours has likewise publicly affirmed its commitment to Rappahannock General, which remains open and providing care.
The financial pressures facing rural hospitals are real and have been an issue for years.
Rural providers have faced declining patient volumes, workforce shortages, rising operating costs, reimbursement pressures, aging infrastructure, and changing demographics for years.
H.R. 1 sought to address those longstanding structural challenges by creating the $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program.
Unlike programs that primarily reimburse providers based on the volume of services delivered, the program gives states flexibility to invest in workforce recruitment and retention, technology, facility modernization, behavioral health, maternal health, primary care, and new models of care intended to strengthen rural health care over the long term.
Any reduction in local health care services is concerning.
Rob will continue working with hospitals and other providers, CMS, state officials, and local communities to address workforce shortages, reimbursement pressures, access to care, and the other challenges facing Virginia’s health care system.
STOCK TRADING CLAIM
Rob has never personally selected, purchased, sold, or directed the timing of an individual stock trade.
His investments are managed by a financial adviser and independent third-party investment managers who make those decisions without consulting him.
Rob’s opponent suggests that he “may have” had confidential information about companies held in his managed accounts.
But that suggestion does not identify what information Rob supposedly possessed, when he possessed it, or how that information was connected to any particular investment transaction.
Rob has not used nonpublic congressional information to make an investment decision.
The “millions” figure cited by Rob’s opponent is not an exact amount reported on Rob’s financial disclosures.
Congressional financial disclosures report individual transactions in broad dollar ranges rather than precise amounts.
Any attempt to calculate a cumulative transaction total therefore requires assumptions about where individual transactions fall within those ranges.
A cumulative figure constructed from disclosure ranges is an estimate, not an exact amount Rob traded — and it is not an amount Rob earned.
There is also an important distinction between transaction volume and profit.
When an investment is sold, and the proceeds are reinvested, both transactions can be reflected in cumulative transaction activity even though the same money is simply moving from one investment to another.
If the new investment is later sold, those dollars can appear again in the cumulative total.
Over years of professional portfolio management and rebalancing, reported purchases and sales can therefore accumulate without representing an equivalent amount of income, profit, or new wealth.
Trading volume is not income.
Trading volume is not profit.
And trading volume is not an increase in net worth.
Rob has also supported stronger restrictions on congressional stock trading.
In July 2026, he voted for H.R. 7008, the Stop Insider Trading Act, legislation restricting stock trading by Members of Congress, their spouses, and dependent children and establishing penalties for violations.
As Rob said when he voted for the bill, “Members of Congress were elected to serve their constituents, not their stock portfolios.” He supports stronger transparency and accountability standards for public officials.
THE STOCK ACT CLAIM
Rob’s opponent says he “violated an anti-insider trading law.” The violation at issue was a late financial disclosure — not a finding of insider trading.
In 2023, Rob learned that the accounting professional responsible for his financial disclosures had failed to file a required Periodic Transaction Report by the deadline.
Rob replaced that professional, instituted a new compliance process, and retained a new accountant who works closely with the House Ethics Committee.
What happened: A required financial disclosure was filed late.
What did NOT happen: Rob was NOT found to have engaged in insider trading.
He was NOT found to have used nonpublic congressional information to make stock trades.
And he was NOT found to have personally traded based on information obtained through his official duties.
A late filing is a compliance violation.
But the distinction matters: a disclosure violation is not a finding of insider trading.
THE “VOTED TO RAISE HIS OWN PAY 10 TIMES” CLAIM
Rob’s opponent claims he voted to raise his own pay 10 times.
But the easiest way to test that claim is to look at Rob’s actual congressional salary.
Rob’s salary in 2009: $174,000.
Rob’s salary today: $174,000.
Under federal law, congressional salaries are subject to an automatic annual pay adjustment unless Congress acts to deny or modify it.
Congress has repeatedly prevented those scheduled adjustments from taking effect, leaving rank-and-file Member pay frozen at $174,000 since 2009.
Rob has voted multiple times to prevent Member pay from increasing, including as recently as the Fiscal Year 2026 Legislative Branch Appropriations Act.
Those pay-freeze provisions have frequently been included in much larger appropriations bills rather than considered as standalone votes on congressional salaries.
A vote against an entire appropriations bill containing a provision blocking an automatic adjustment is not the same thing as a standalone vote to increase congressional pay.
THE NET-WORTH CLAIM
Rob’s opponent claims he became “up to forty times richer” during his time in Congress.
But the “40x” figure is not an actual net-worth figure reported by Rob.
It is an outside estimate constructed from congressional financial disclosures that report assets and liabilities in broad ranges rather than precise amounts.
To calculate a specific “net worth” from those disclosures, an outside organization has to make assumptions about where each asset and liability falls within its reported range.
Selecting values near the high end of those ranges can produce a dramatically different estimate than selecting values elsewhere within the same ranges.
An estimate constructed from disclosure ranges is not the same thing as a documented net-worth figure, and the disclosures themselves do not establish that Rob became “40 times richer.”
Changes in Rob and Kathryn’s reported assets over nearly two decades also reflect retirement savings, long-term investments, real estate, market appreciation, and family inheritances dating back well before Rob entered Congress.
Rob spent more than 26 years working for the Commonwealth of Virginia before entering Congress and earning and saving for retirement.
Rob and Kathryn still live in the same Montross home they purchased in 1985, more than two decades before Rob entered Congress.
Rob drives himself between Washington and Montross in his Toyota RAV4.
Rob’s opponent also conflates the value of Rob’s reported assets with the value of transactions reported by his managed investment accounts.
Those are not the same thing.
Transaction volume is not income, profit, or an increase in net worth.
An increase in estimated household assets over nearly two decades does not establish what caused that increase.
Rob’s opponent provides no evidence establishing that Rob’s congressional actions caused the increase in his reported household assets or that congressional stock trading was the source of that increase.
An estimated increase in family assets over nearly 20 years is not evidence that Rob used his congressional office for personal enrichment.
THE “PERSONALLY PROFITING WHILE VIRGINIANS PAY MORE” CLAIM
Rob’s opponent claims he was “personally profiting while we pay more.” But that allegation attempts to connect several separate things: an investment held in Rob’s managed accounts, a congressional vote, a company’s subsequent pricing decisions, and personal financial profit.
Congressional financial disclosures do not establish that connection.
They generally report transactions and asset values in broad ranges, and the existence of a sale does not establish how much profit, if any, was realized from the transaction.
The suggestion that Rob has been making Virginians “pay more” also leaves out relevant parts of his record.
On data-center costs specifically, Rob cosponsored and voted for the bipartisan Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340), legislation intended to ensure that large customers bear the incremental infrastructure costs required to serve them rather than shifting those costs onto families and small businesses.
Rob does not personally select individual stocks, decide when they are bought or sold, or direct his investment managers to make particular trades.
THE “TAX BREAKS TO COMPANIES HE OWNS” CLAIM
Taylor claims Rob “pushed billions in tax breaks to companies he owns, like utility monopolies and data centers.”
Rob does not personally select the individual stocks held in his managed accounts, direct their purchase or sale, or control the business decisions of companies whose shares may be held in those accounts.
Rob’s legislative record on data-center costs also includes direct action intended to protect residential and small-business ratepayers.
On September 16, 2026, Rob voted for the Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340), bipartisan legislation he also cosponsored.
The bill passed the House 417–3.
The legislation establishes a federal standard concerning recovery of the full incremental costs of infrastructure upgrades serving large energy customers and requires state regulatory authorities and certain utilities to consider that standard.
That record is directly relevant to the suggestion that Rob has supported shifting data-center costs onto Virginia families.
Rob has supported legislation intended to ensure that large energy customers bear the incremental infrastructure costs associated with serving them rather than shifting those costs onto residential and small-business ratepayers.
THE “$60,000 BEACH-HOUSE TAX BREAK” CLAIM
Rob’s opponent claims Rob voted to give himself a tax break worth approximately $60,000 on his Outer Banks rental property.
That figure is not taken from Rob’s tax return, is not a documented amount of tax savings, and is not a calculation of the tax benefit attributable specifically to the Outer Banks property.
The figure comes from an estimate by the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP) that Rob’s total potential tax reduction under the 2025 tax law could range from approximately $19,900 to $59,300.
That is a range of nearly $40,000.
Taylor takes the highest number in that estimated range, rounds it to approximately $60,000, and presents it as though it were a documented tax benefit tied specifically to Rob’s Outer Banks property.
Reducing tax liability is not the same as a check from the IRS.
Rob is subject to the same tax code as any VA-01 resident.
The ITEP estimate relies in part on congressional financial disclosures, which report income and assets in broad ranges rather than precise amounts.
Those disclosures do not establish Rob’s actual taxable income or provide enough information to determine his precise tax liability.
The provision Taylor cites is Section 199A, the Qualified Business Income deduction.
It is a generally available provision of the federal tax code that allows eligible taxpayers to deduct a portion of qualifying business income, subject to applicable requirements and limitations.
It has been in place since 2017, and the 2025 law made the Section 199A deduction permanent.
Congress did not create a special tax provision for Rob or his Outer Banks property.
Section 199A is broadly available to qualifying taxpayers, and qualifying rental real estate may constitute a trade or business for purposes of the deduction when federal requirements are met.
The Outer Banks property itself is a rental investment, not Rob’s personal vacation residence.
The capital used to acquire it came from the sale of inherited family property.
Rob and Kathryn continue to live in the same Montross home they purchased in 1985.
The distinction is straightforward: an estimated overall tax reduction ranging from $19,900 to $59,300 is not evidence that Rob received a $60,000 tax break on one rental property.
