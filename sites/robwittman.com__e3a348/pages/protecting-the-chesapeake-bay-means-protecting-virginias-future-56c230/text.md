Few places are as central to Virginia’s identity as the Chesapeake Bay.
For generations, the Bay has shaped who we are.
It supports our watermen and seafood industry, sustains farms throughout the watershed, attracts visitors from around the world, provides recreation for millions of Americans, and serves as both an economic and environmental cornerstone of our Commonwealth.
Its health affects all of us.
Long before I had the privilege of serving in Congress, I spent more than two decades working in public health and shellfish sanitation with the Virginia Department of Health.
Much of that work focused on water quality, environmental health, and the safety of shellfish harvested from the Chesapeake Bay and its tributaries.
Those years taught me something I have never forgotten: protecting our natural resources is not an abstract policy debate.
It is about protecting communities, livelihoods, and the quality of life we hope to leave our children and grandchildren.
That perspective has shaped my work throughout my career in public service.
Conservation and Economic Growth Belong Together
Conservation should never be viewed as a partisan issue.
Clean water benefits every Virginian.
Healthy fisheries strengthen local economies.
Effective farmland conservation can improve water quality while supporting families who have worked the land for generations.
Wildlife habitats, wetlands, public lands, and working waterfronts enrich our communities while preserving the natural beauty that makes Virginia such a remarkable place to call home.
The Chesapeake Bay is not only an environmental treasure, but it is also an economic engine.
We should reject the false choice between economic growth and environmental stewardship.
Virginia can strengthen both.
Working Together to Get Results
Over the years, Congress has made meaningful progress because Republicans and Democrats have chosen cooperation over confrontation.
I have worked with colleagues from both parties to strengthen Chesapeake Bay restoration, improve scientific research, restore habitats, support fisheries, preserve working waterfronts, expand voluntary conservation, and make federal programs more accountable.
My Chesapeake Bay Accountability and Recovery Act became law to improve coordination, transparency, and oversight across federal Bay restoration efforts.
I also helped advance the bipartisan America’s Conservation Enhancement Act and later introduced its 2024 reauthorization, both of which became law and continued important programs supporting Chesapeake Bay restoration, wetlands, fish and wildlife habitats, locally led conservation, and public access throughout the watershed.
More recently, I introduced the bipartisan Chesapeake Bay Conservation Acceleration Act, which would expand voluntary conservation practices on farms throughout the watershed, improve soil health and water quality, strengthen the agricultural conservation workforce, and support efforts to address invasive species such as blue catfish.
I have also worked on bipartisan efforts to strengthen water research, preserve working waterfronts, improve fisheries management, and support the watermen and seafood businesses whose livelihoods depend on a healthy Bay.
And just this month, the House passed the bipartisan Chesapeake Bay Watershed Advancement for Training, Education, Restoration, and Science—or WATERS—Act, legislation I was proud to co-lead with Representatives Bobby Scott and Jen Kiggans of Virginia and Sarah Elfreth of Maryland.
The WATERS Act would reauthorize NOAA’s Chesapeake Bay Office and support the science, fisheries management, habitat restoration, monitoring, and environmental education that are essential to the Bay’s future.
It would also continue support for the Chesapeake Bay Interpretive Buoy System and reauthorize the Chesapeake Bay Watershed Education and Training program—B-WET, helping students and educators connect classroom learning with hands-on experiences throughout the watershed.
Science Should Guide Stewardship
My years working in public health taught me that good environmental policy begins with good information.
You cannot improve water quality if you do not measure it.
You cannot sustainably manage fisheries if you do not understand what is happening to the population.
And you cannot protect shellfish consumers without monitoring the waters where those shellfish are harvested.
Science gives us the ability to understand problems, measure progress, and adjust when our assumptions prove wrong.
That is why my approach to conservation has always emphasized practical, science-based solutions and measurable results.
Today, as vice chairman of the House Natural Resources Committee and co-chair of the bipartisan Chesapeake Bay Watershed Task Force, I continue working with farmers, watermen, scientists, conservation organizations, local governments, businesses, sportsmen, and colleagues from both parties to protect the Bay.
Lasting progress happens when the people who depend on these resources are part of the solution.
Protecting Virginia’s Future
Protecting the Chesapeake Bay means protecting much more than water.
It means preserving working waterfronts.
It means maintaining healthy fisheries and supporting the watermen who have worked them for generations.
It means helping farmers remain productive while improving soil and water quality.
It means protecting tourism, recreation, wildlife habitat, and small businesses.
And it means ensuring future generations inherit the same opportunities to fish, hunt, boat, farm, work, and raise families along the Bay that generations of Virginians have enjoyed before them.
The Chesapeake Bay is one of America’s greatest natural treasures.
Our responsibility is not simply to enjoy it today.
It is to leave it cleaner, healthier, and stronger for the generations that follow.
— Rob Wittman
