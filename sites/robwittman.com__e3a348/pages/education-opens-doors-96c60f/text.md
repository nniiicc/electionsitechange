My mother was a teacher.
Growing up, I saw the work that happened long after her students had gone home: papers to grade, lessons to prepare, and countless hours spent thinking about how to help each student succeed.
Years later, I married a teacher.
My wife, Kathryn, spent 41 years teaching elementary school students in Westmoreland County.
I watched her devote the same care and attention to her students that I had seen from my mother years earlier.
Living alongside two educators taught me that education is ultimately about something larger than a classroom.
It is about opportunity.
Good teachers help students discover what they are capable of doing.
They develop curiosity, confidence, discipline, and skills young people will carry with them long after they leave school.
But they also understand something policymakers sometimes forget: no two students are exactly alike, and success does not follow a single path.
More Than One Path to Success For generations, we have too often talked about education as though every successful student should follow the same route: graduate from high school, attend a four-year college, earn a degree, and begin a career.
That is an excellent path for many students.
It is not the only one.
Some students will attend a four-year university.
Others will earn an associate degree at a community college.
Some will pursue an apprenticeship, technical education, professional certification, or skilled trade.
Others will enter military service, begin working immediately, or eventually build businesses of their own.
All of those paths can lead to meaningful careers and productive lives.
Our responsibility should not be to decide which path a young person must follow.
It should be to make sure students have the information, skills, and opportunities they need to pursue the path that fits their talents and goals.
Giving Families More Flexibility That principle is why I introduced the bipartisan Freedom to Invest in Tomorrow’s Workforce Act.
For years, families could use tax-advantaged 529 savings accounts for traditional higher education expenses, but those same savings could not necessarily be used for many professional credentials, licenses, and workforce training programs.
That did not make sense.
A family saving for a child’s future should have flexibility whether that student plans to earn a traditional college degree, professional certification, industry credential, or another form of postsecondary training.
In July 2025, Freedom to Invest in Tomorrow’s Workforce Act was signed into law as part of broader legislation passed by Congress.
The change expanded eligible uses of 529 accounts to include qualifying workforce training, professional credentials, licenses, and certification expenses.
That is a relatively simple change to federal law, but it reflects an important reality: Americans learn, train, and build successful careers in many different ways.
This does not diminish the importance of a four-year college education.
Universities remain important engines of research, innovation, and opportunity.
It simply recognizes that college is one path among many.
Every Career Path Has Value Some students dream of becoming engineers, teachers, physicians, or accountants.
Others want to become welders, electricians, cybersecurity specialists, mechanics, nurses, or entrepreneurs.
Every one of those careers contributes to the strength of our communities.
I saw that firsthand earlier this year at Rappahannock Community College, where welding and mechanics programs, apprenticeships, and industry-recognized credentials are connecting students with the skills employers throughout Virginia need.
Families should not have to decide years in advance whether they are saving for “college” or saving for “career training.” They should be able to prepare for whichever path ultimately fits their child’s talents, interests, and goals.
Virginia is fortunate to have outstanding public schools, universities, community colleges, career and technical education programs, apprenticeships, and employers willing to invest in the next generation of workers.
Opening Doors When I think about education, I still think about the teachers who shaped my own life.
I think about my mother preparing another day’s lesson.
I think about Kathryn spending decades helping elementary school students build the foundation they would carry into adulthood.
Neither of them knew exactly where every child sitting in their classroom would eventually end up.
That was not their job.
Their job was to open doors.
That should be our goal in public policy as well: give students strong foundations, give families meaningful choices, and create pathways to opportunity.
Success has never followed only one blueprint.
Every student has different talents, different interests, and different goals.
Our education system should give them the opportunity to discover where those talents can take them. — Rob Wittman Support Me in Making Virginia a Better Place! $50 $100 $250 $500 Other PO Box 427, Alexandria, VA 22313 | campaign@robwittman.com Want to get involved?
