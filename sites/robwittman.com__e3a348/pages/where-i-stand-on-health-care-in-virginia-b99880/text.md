Health care has been part of my professional life since long before I entered Congress.
I earned a master’s degree in public health and spent more than 26 years working for the Virginia Department of Health.
During that time, and throughout my service in Congress, I have seen firsthand how important local hospitals, physicians, nurses, community health centers, mental health providers, pharmacists, and emergency medical professionals are to the communities they serve.
That experience continues to shape how I approach health care today.
My guiding principle is straightforward: health care should work for patients, families, doctors, and local communities.
We should lower costs, expand access to quality care, improve transparency, strengthen our health care workforce, reduce unnecessary bureaucracy, and make sure patients and their doctors remain at the center of health care decisions.
The Reality Facing Rural Hospitals
Recent reports about the financial condition of Virginia hospitals have understandably raised concerns, particularly in rural communities.
Those concerns deserve to be taken seriously, and they deserve to be discussed accurately.
There is an important difference between a projection about financial risk and an actual hospital closure.
A recent state analysis identified VCU Health Tappahannock Hospital as being at risk under the methodology used in the report.
That was a financial-risk assessment, not an announcement that the hospital would close.
VCU Health CEO Dr.
Marlon Levy has publicly disputed that conclusion and made clear that Tappahannock is not closing.
The challenges facing rural health care are real and longstanding.
Rural hospitals have struggled for years with workforce shortages, declining patient volumes, rising labor and operating costs, reimbursement challenges, aging infrastructure, limited specialty-care access, and the unique expense of serving geographically dispersed populations.
Many of these challenges have been building for years and require sustained solutions from providers, states, and the federal government.
Investing in the Future of Rural Health Care
Protecting coverage is only part of the equation.
Patients also need doctors, hospitals, pharmacies, and other health care providers there when they need care.
That is why strengthening the long-term sustainability of rural providers has been such an important part of my approach.
I supported the creation of the $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program because rural communities face challenges that traditional reimbursement systems alone cannot solve.
Rural hospitals often operate with lower patient volumes than large urban hospitals.
A system that relies heavily on reimbursement after services are provided does not necessarily give those hospitals the resources they need to recruit providers, modernize facilities, expand technology, or develop new models of care.
The Rural Health Transformation Program was designed to give states greater flexibility to make those longer-term investments and strengthen rural health systems before they reach a crisis point.
I fought to ensure Virginia was awarded robust funding from the Rural Health Transformation Program, and as a result, the Commonwealth is receiving $189,544,888 in 2026.
Virginia’s plan includes investments in the health care workforce, virtual and remote care, maternal and primary care, technology, and innovative models designed to bring more services closer to rural patients.
Of that award, CMS has already announced $122 million in investments being delivered in Virginia for initiatives including remote patient monitoring, virtual care, mobile clinics, maternal care, community paramedicine, and workforce development programs.
Those investments do not eliminate every challenge facing rural health care.
They are one part of a much larger effort that must include sustainable reimbursement, workforce development, innovation, accountability, and continued collaboration among federal and state officials and the people actually delivering care in our communities.
Making Health Care More Affordable
Affordability matters just as much as access.
Earlier this year, I voted in support of legislation to extend the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits through 2028 to help prevent sharp premium increases for individuals and families purchasing coverage through the ACA Marketplace.
But subsidies alone cannot solve the underlying problem of rising health care costs.
Congress also needs to confront the forces driving those costs higher.
That means improving price transparency so patients know what care will cost before they receive it.
It means holding insurers accountable and addressing unnecessary prior authorization delays.
It means addressing the role of pharmacy benefit managers and encouraging competition in prescription drugs.
It means reducing administrative burdens so doctors and nurses can spend more time treating patients rather than completing paperwork.
And it means strengthening the health care workforce so communities have enough physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and behavioral health specialists to meet patient needs.
Strengthening Medicaid for Those Who Depend on It
I supported the Medicaid reforms in the Working Families Tax Cuts because I believe we have a responsibility to strengthen the program’s long-term sustainability while protecting the people who depend on it most.
The reforms included measures focused on program integrity, eligibility verification, and ensuring Medicaid resources remain focused on those who truly need it.
Throughout consideration of the legislation, I repeatedly urged congressional leadership to protect both the people Medicaid was designed to serve and the hospitals and providers they depend on.
In April 2025, I joined colleagues in making clear that balancing the federal budget should not come at the expense of Americans who rely on Medicaid for their health and economic security.
We also warned that changes to Medicaid had to account for the financial stability of hospitals, nursing homes, and safety-net providers, particularly in rural and underserved communities.
As negotiations continued, I again joined colleagues in June in urging congressional leaders to protect pregnant women, children, seniors, individuals with disabilities, and other vulnerable beneficiaries while protecting access to coverage and the stability of hospitals and providers.
Beginning in 2027, the law establishes part-time community engagement requirements for certain working-age, able-bodied adults.
Community engagement can be met by spending 20 hours a week volunteering, working, or participating in job training, education, or other qualifying activity.
These requirements do not apply to everyone receiving Medicaid.
Virginia has confirmed that children, pregnant women and new mothers, parents and guardians of young children, adults 65 and older, many people with disabilities, and several other populations are not subject to the new community engagement requirement.
The goal of the reforms I supported is to strengthen Medicaid so it can continue serving vulnerable Americans for generations to come.
At the same time, legislation should always be evaluated by how it works in practice.
If access to care for vulnerable patients or the viability of local providers is threatened, Congress should act.
Keeping Quality Care Close to Home
I will continue listening to Virginia’s hospitals and health care professionals, evaluating the effects of federal policy, and working with CMS, state leaders, local communities, and providers when problems arise.
Health care policy should ultimately be measured by what it means for patients: whether someone can find a doctor, whether a mother can access maternity care close to home, whether a senior can afford a prescription, and whether families can obtain quality coverage at a price they can afford.
That is the standard I have brought to health care policy throughout my career, and it will continue to guide my work: expand access, lower costs, strengthen providers, and make sure Virginia patients can receive high-quality care close to home.
— Rob Wittman
