Every day Congress is in session, I make the same two-hour drive, both ways.
After the final votes of the day, I leave Washington and head home to Virginia.
Sometimes it is late by the time I pull into my driveway.
Then I get back on the road early the next morning and do it again.
People occasionally ask why I make that drive instead of simply staying in Washington.
The answer is easy: home is where I belong.
Kathryn and I have lived in the same house in Montross since 1985.
It is where we raised our children and where we now enjoy watching our grandchildren grow up.
It is where we have celebrated milestones, weathered challenges, built friendships, attended church, and become part of a community we have called home for more than four decades.
Washington is where I work.
Virginia is home.
I believe that distinction matters.
Staying Connected
The longer someone serves in public office, the easier it can become to spend more time talking with people inside government than with the people government is supposed to serve.
I have never wanted that to happen to me.
When I am home, I run into neighbors at the grocery store and gas station.
I talk with farmers, watermen, teachers, veterans, small-business owners, military families, first responders, and people I have known for years.
Those conversations are a constant reminder that decisions made in Washington do not stay in Washington.
They show up in the grocery bill a family pays at the end of the week.
They affect whether a small business owner can hire another employee.
They matter to the farmer calculating whether this year’s crop will cover rising expenses, the military family preparing for another deployment, and the veteran trying to navigate the benefits he or she earned.
No briefing paper can replace those conversations.
Public Service Begins With Listening
That lesson started long before I came to Congress.
I grew up working wherever I could find a job.
I stocked grocery shelves, worked in restaurants, painted houses, spent time on commercial fishing boats, worked at Kings Dominion, and spent summers in a tomato cannery.
Later, I spent 26 years working in public health throughout Virginia’s Northern Neck and Middle Peninsula.
Eventually, neighbors encouraged me to become involved in local government.
I served on the Montross Town Council, as mayor, on the Westmoreland County Board of Supervisors, in the Virginia House of Delegates, and eventually in Congress.
Through every one of those experiences, I learned the same thing: you make better decisions when you listen to the people closest to a problem.
That remains one of the most important parts of my job.
When Congress is not in session, I travel throughout the First District meeting with businesses, hospitals, schools, military installations, farmers, watermen, local officials, veterans, and families.
Our district stretches from the Northern Neck and Middle Peninsula to the Historic Triangle and the growing communities of Chesterfield, Henrico, and Hanover.
Those places are different, and the challenges facing them are not always the same.
Listening is how you understand those differences.
I learn more from those conversations than I ever could behind a desk in Washington.
Remembering Who You Work For
Public office can come with impressive buildings, titles, and committee assignments.
None of those things change who I work for.
The people I represent live in communities across Virginia’s First District.
They are raising families, running businesses, serving our country, teaching children, working the water, growing food, caring for patients, and building the future of the Commonwealth.
They live lives much like the ones I return to every time I drive home.
That is why I keep making the trip.
Every time I turn onto the road toward Montross, I am reminded that government ultimately comes down to real people living real lives.
Representing Virginia’s First District has been one of the greatest privileges of my professional life.
But I have never wanted Washington to become my life.
Because the people I work for do not live there.
They live here.
And home has a way of making sure you never forget it.
— Rob Wittman
