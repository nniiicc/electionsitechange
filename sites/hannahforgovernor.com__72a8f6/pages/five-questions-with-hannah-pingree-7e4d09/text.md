The first time I met Hannah Pingree was at a local parade in Freeport, Maine.
My daughter was working there at the time.
I saw Hannah walking around, casually chatting with folks.
I told her I wasn’t a Maine voter, but I was interested in her ideas for improving housing, which is a real issue for Mainers, as it is for a lot of people across the country.
I was impressed by how substantive her ideas were and the way she talked about them like they were real possibilities—policies she’d pursue if elected, not just talking points.
I’ve paid attention to her ever since.
Hannah, speaking at a recent gathering in Maine Hannah emerged as the winner from a Democratic gubernatorial primary in Maine that was blessed with an abundance of likable, qualified candidates who ran an incredibly collegial race, joking that they’d all work for whoever won at one event.
It was a dose of sanity in the middle of this tense political season, a real respite from the nastiness of Alabama politics.
And a good reminder that people who are in public service for all the right reasons still exist.
We need that right now, especially from America’s governors.
Something we’re seeing is that in the no-guardrails era of would-be king Trump, state leaders have the ability to prevent some of the worst abuses.
Governors have intervened on issues like using the National Guard to police protests and have exerted leadership on a range of issues where national leaders have failed to.
During the final years of Trump’s time in office, it may be governors, along with state attorneys general and secretaries of state, who protect rights like voting and access to medical care.
So I’m especially delighted we have a chance to get to know Hannah better tonight.
We should expect more of our elected officials than the low standards the last decade has left us with.
Talking with Hannah reminds me there is a generation of younger politicians coming along who understand this, and want to deliver.
I love her explanation of what it was like to be the youngest speaker of state in a state legislature anywhere in the country and what she learned from leading the Governor’s Office of the Future in Maine.

Joyce: You’re the youngest woman ever elected to serve as speaker of a state legislature.
Talk with us about that moment, and how it felt to be entrusted with that responsibility?
Hannah: Well, truthfully, I hadn’t planned to run for office at age 25 or to be serving as Majority Leader at age 30 and as Speaker at age 32.
It was an awesome responsibility – and a tremendous honor.
I grew up in a very small island community of just 400 people where you couldn’t wait for someone else to come solve the challenges we faced.
I watched my parents pitch in – on the school board, running businesses, stepping up.
And I saw everyone else in my town doing the same.
It taught me that you have to work with people of all ages, and across the political spectrum, to get things done.
And you can’t give up or wait for someone else to show up.
I also learned that you have to listen and be kind, because even when you have fights or disagreements, the people around you will continue to be your neighbors.
And learning that at a young age gave me a real sense of responsibility, an ability to work with anyone, and a confidence that I could lead other people as well.
So as a very young speaker, I knew I had to be practical, listen, do my homework and work hard, and be willing to collaborate with anyone, Democrat or Republican, who wanted to get something done.
Maine is small enough that you can’t afford to burn bridges.
Like my hometown, you’re going to see those folks at the grocery store or on the sidelines of your kids’ sports games.
I think it’s why I was elected unanimously as Speaker of the House by Democrats, Republicans, and Independents.
I’m proud to have been the youngest woman (sidenote: and the only one in the country to do so while also pregnant) to hold that role, but what I’m proudest of is what we got done together: passed bipartisan budgets, expanded broadband in rural areas, invested in clean energy, lowered health care costs, defended reproductive rights, passed marriage equality, improved housing access, and protected Maine’s working waterfronts.
And even though there were many disagreements, we maintained respect for each other.
Joyce: Under your leadership, Maine became the first state in the country to pass marriage equality.
Barack Obama still wasn’t there, but you were in Maine.
There were legislators who understood the vote might cost them their jobs but still thought it was important to do the right thing.
Can you talk about what gave you all the courage to move ahead in that moment and whether leadership like that still exists?
Hannah: I think about that moment a lot, and I often say that helping lead the fight for marriage equality taught me one of the most important lessons I’ve learned in politics: you have to be brave, especially when the decisions you’re making matter so deeply to people’s lives and families.
When I got married back in 2007 to my husband, two dear friends of mine, David and David, helped marry me.
But, they couldn’t legally get married themselves.
They could celebrate my marriage, but they weren’t entitled to the same rights and protections for their own relationship.
That was personal to me, and it was fundamentally unfair.
And here’s what makes that story especially poignant today.
David and David finally got married just this past year, not because they suddenly decided their relationship mattered more, but because they were worried about what Donald Trump and his administration might do to their rights.
After all these years, they still felt they needed to protect their family from the possibility of losing rights we fought so hard to secure.
Back in 2009, we knew passing marriage equality would be politically difficult.
There were legislators, including Democrats, who were nervous about taking that vote.
Some knew it might cost them their seats.
But they did it anyway.
I’m enormously proud of those colleagues, because they understood that there are moments when doing what’s right matters more than protecting your political future.
That experience shaped me as a leader.
It taught me that courage isn’t about being fearless.
It’s about knowing something matters enough that you’re willing to take a risk for it.
And when you’re talking about people’s families, their dignity, and their fundamental rights, that’s exactly what elected leaders should be willing to do.
Does that kind of leadership still exist?
Absolutely.
I see people in the Maine leadership and communities making difficult decisions and standing up for their neighbors – especially over the course of the last few years.
Whether it is standing up for immigrant neighbors who are being terrorized by ICE or taking politically difficult votes in the Maine legislature.
I also think this moment demands more courage from all of us.
We are watching rights that generations of Americans fought for come under threat, and we can’t afford to let political calculations determine whether we stand up for the people who need us.
I’ve always believed you can be principled and pragmatic at the same time.
You can fight hard for what you believe in and still work with people who disagree with you.
That’s how I approached my work in the Legislature, and it’s how I would approach being governor.
Joyce: You’ve also served on your local school board, a role many people think is one of the most important in government.
How did you get there, and what do you think about the role of local government in making people’s lives better, and how important you think it is to elect committed people to school boards nationwide?
Hannah: I often joke that serving as chair of my local school board was the hardest job I’ve had in politics, but it’s true!
I was elected as I was leaving the Legislature and having my first child, and I went on to serve nine years.
Now, with two teenagers in Maine public schools, I’ve experienced public education as a student, a parent, and a board chair.
And I’ve never seen the challenges facing our schools and school leaders feel more urgent.
I think school boards are one of the most important places where public service happens, because you’re entrusted with something incredibly precious: other people’s children and their futures.
And you’re making decisions that affect your neighbors every single day.
It’s an enormous responsibility.
You’re trying to make sure every child gets a great education, that students who need extra support aren’t left behind, and that teachers and school staff, who are often your friends and neighbors, are treated fairly and paid a wage that allows them to live in the communities they serve.
At the same time, you’re accountable to taxpayers, including seniors and working families who are struggling to afford their property taxes.
Those are real tensions, and there aren’t always easy answers.
And in the last few years, school boards have faced an entirely new set of challenges.
We’ve seen national political fights brought into local classrooms, debates over books and curriculum, attacks on LGBTQ students and educators, and growing pressure on people who are simply trying to do right by their communities.
All while schools are dealing with the very real challenges of student mental health, learning loss, staffing shortages, and preparing kids for a future being transformed by technology and artificial intelligence.
I worry that some of the loudest political battles distract us from what should be our shared responsibility: making sure every child feels safe, supported, and challenged to reach their potential.
You can disagree about education policy, and people certainly do, but we should never lose sight of the kids at the center of those decisions.
That’s also what I love about local government.
It’s personal.
In a small Maine town, the people making decisions are your neighbors.
You see them at the grocery store, at school events, and around town.
You have to listen, explain your decisions, and live with the consequences alongside everyone else.
So yes, I think it’s incredibly important that good people step up to serve on school boards, especially now.
We need people who care deeply about public education, who respect teachers and parents, who are willing to listen to different perspectives, and who won’t let national political divisions get in the way of doing what’s best for children.
Serving on my school board reinforced something I’ve believed throughout my career: government works best when people show up, take responsibility, and care enough about their neighbors to do the hard work.
That’s true in a small-town school board meeting, and it’s just as true in the governor’s office.
Joyce: You’ve told the story of outgoing Maine Governor Janet being so taken with the Kurt Vonnegut quote that “every government ought to have a Department of the Future” that she created one.
And you got to play a role in it.
Explain that to us; tell us what you were involved with and how this shapes your view of the role of government.
Hannah: I was the first director of the Governor’s Office of Policy Innovation and the Future or GOPIF.
Governor Mills was inspired by that Kurt Vonnegut quote and the previous governor had eliminated the state’s Planning Office, so we were essentially building something new from scratch.
But it truly was an office focused on the future and how we ensure thoughtful planning as well as collaboration, to get to policy solutions for some of the most critical issues facing Maine.
One of the things I’m proudest of is the incredible team we built.
We hired smart, creative, deeply committed people who wanted to tackle some of Maine’s toughest challenges.
And we approached the work with a pretty simple philosophy: bring people together who don’t always agree, listen to the people closest to the problems, use good data and evidence to guide decisions, and be transparent about what we’re trying to accomplish and whether it’s working.
We took on some of the biggest challenges facing Maine, from housing and workforce shortages to climate change, economic development, and the needs of children and older Mainers.
None of these problems could be solved by one agency or one political party.
We brought together local leaders, businesses and workers, advocates, scientists, and people with very different perspectives to develop new approaches and turn them into real policies and investments.
And we delivered results.
We helped make Maine a national leader in heat pump adoption, advanced climate and storm resilience efforts, developed new approaches to housing and workforce challenges, and helped communities and businesses navigate the pandemic and economic recovery.
We weren’t afraid to try innovative ideas, measure what was working, and change course when something wasn’t getting results.
But maybe the biggest lesson I took from that experience is that you can’t give up on the hardest problems.
Housing, climate change, the opioid crisis, the challenges facing working families—these aren’t things you solve with one bill, one budget, or one press conference.
They require sustained attention, honest conversations, collaboration across agencies and political lines, and a willingness to keep pushing even when progress is frustratingly slow.
And it’s how I would lead as governor.
I don’t think Maine people expect the government to solve every problem overnight.
But they have every right to expect us to be creative, accountable, and relentless about making things better.
Leaders shouldn’t just respond to the next crisis.
It should be building the future we want for our kids and our communities.
Joyce: From talking with Mainers, it seems to me that one of the secrets to the high regard you’re held in is that in an era where people tend to think of government as broken, you expect it to be functional and you get things done.
I’ve heard you say that ability makes you hopeful about Maine’s future.
Are you hopeful about the country’s future too?
Hannah: Well, that’s very kind.
I’d say I’ve been lucky to work with a lot of good people who also wanted to get things done.
And yes, I’m hopeful.
I’ll admit it’s not always easy right now.
I think Donald Trump has done enormous damage to our country, to our institutions, and to the basic sense of decency and respect that should guide how we treat one another.
But I also think his presidency has reminded millions of Americans just how precious some of our most fundamental values are: democracy, freedom, the rule of law, and the belief that everyone deserves dignity and opportunity.
These aren’t things we can take for granted, and I see people standing up to defend them every day.
I’ve always believed you can’t lead if you’re not hopeful.
How can you take on the hardest problems and ask people to join you if you don’t believe things can get better?
But hope has to be matched with action.
When you roll up your sleeves and actually solve something, whether it’s a school budget, a housing project, or helping a community recover from a storm, it gives you another reason to believe in what’s possible.
And I see that spirit in Maine all the time.
People show up for each other.
They coach the team, run the community supper, serve on the school board, and help neighbors through tough times, regardless of their politics.
That’s the country I believe in, and I don’t think Donald Trump gets to define who we are as Americans.
So yes, I’m hopeful.
Not because I think things will magically get better, but because I believe there are still far more people who want to build something better than tear it apart.
And I’ve seen what can happen when we bring those people together and get to work.
“People show up for each other.” Isn’t that what this great American experiment is all about?
There is some simple, elegant wisdom in Hannah’s words, and I appreciate that final point she makes, that Donald Trump does not get to define who we are as Americans.
We do.
