Voting Rights and Election Access in Maryland Explained
Maryland voters demonstrated remarkable civic engagement in 2024.
More than 2.7 million Marylanders cast ballots in the general election, supported by more than 23,000 election judges and 1,000 teen election aides.
This historic participation reflects a fundamental truth about voting rights and election access in Maryland: when voting is accessible, citizens engage.
Understanding voting rights and election access in Maryland begins with distinguishing between two vital concepts:
Voting Rights represent the constitutional and statutory protections that guarantee every eligible citizen the power to cast a ballot without interference, intimidation, or discriminatory barriers.
Election Access focuses on the logistical infrastructure that makes these rights a reality, including the proximity of polling locations, mail-in ballot availability, registration simplicity, and language assistance.
Together, voting rights and election access in Maryland form the foundation of representative government.
Maryland has positioned itself as a national leader in both areas by implementing the Maryland Voting Rights Act of 2026.

3.
Protection from Voter Suppression You possess legal pathways to challenge discriminatory practices, with both state and federal protections.

The Maryland Voting Rights Act (MDVRA) of 2026: Strengthening Voting Rights and Election Access in Maryland
The Maryland Voting Rights Act represents historic legislation in voter protection and a pivotal advancement for voting rights and election access in Maryland.
Governor Wes Moore signed the Voting Rights Act of 2026 (SB 255) into law on April 28, 2026, calling it legislation that "ensures that every voice will be heard.
Every voice will be counted".
What the MDVRA Actually Does
The state law applies to counties or municipalities, not to state elections, and it prohibits anything that "impairs the ability of a protected class to elect candidates of the members' choice." Protected classes under the law include members of a "race, color or language minority group".
Key provisions include:
1.
Prohibition of Vote Dilution The law removes barriers to the ballot box by prohibiting any method of electing a county or municipal governing body that creates polarized voting or dilutes the votes of a protected class.
2.
Legal Enforcement Mechanisms Under the legislation, the state attorney general or a private party can file a court action to enforce the bill's prohibitions, and the bill creates a legal framework for courts to use when reviewing those actions.
3.
Action Against Polarized Voting The attorney general or any resident can sue a county or local government if there is evidence of "polarized voting"—a difference in choice of candidates between members of a protected class and other voters—and the "method of election" dilutes or abridges the voting strength of the protected class.
Bill Sponsors and Coalition Support
The MDVRA passed through dedicated legislative leadership:
- Senate Sponsor: Senator Charles Sydnor III (D-Baltimore County)
- House Sponsor: Delegate Greg Wims (D-Montgomery), who has been advocating for voting rights since 1971, when he helped lower the voting age from 21 as President of the Maryland Youth Commission
The MDVRA coalition included the ACLU of Maryland, Common Cause Maryland, Campaign Legal Center, Everyone Votes Maryland Coalition, and Legal Defense Fund.
Historical Context: Why This Matters Now
The MDVRA took effect just one day before the U.S.
Supreme Court issued its ruling in Louisiana v.
Callais, which significantly weakened racial redistricting protections in the federal Voting Rights Act of 1965.
Governor Moore emphasized the urgency: "Maryland has a fundamental responsibility to meet this moment.
This week, I signed the Maryland Voting Rights Act into law, because we refuse to wait for Washington to do the right thing."
Expanding Language Access at the Polls
Maryland has also strengthened language access protections.
Senate Bill 685, sponsored by Senate President Pro Tem Malcolm Augustine (D47-Prince George's County), addresses the needs of Maryland's growing French, Amharic, and Arabic-speaking communities and expands Spanish language assistance.
Under Maryland Code, Election Law Article § 15.5-102(c)-(d), language assistance must be provided by a local board of elections where more than 2% of the citizen voting age population uses a single shared language other than English and has limited English proficiency, or more than 4,000 individuals meet these criteria.
You also have the protected right to bring a translator of your choice into the voting booth, provided they aren't your employer or union representative.

How do I check my Maryland voter registration status?
You can check your voter registration status through the Maryland State Board of Elections Voter Lookup tool at voterservices.elections.maryland.gov.

What is the Maryland 2026 voter registration deadline?

How do I track my Maryland mail-in ballot?
You can track the status of your Maryland mail-in ballot through the Maryland State Board of Elections website at elections.maryland.gov.
The tracking tool confirms when your ballot was sent to you, received by the local board of elections, and counted.
Your mailed ballot comes with a postage-paid return envelope, so submitting your ballot by mail is free.
Where can I find ballot drop boxes in Prince George's County?
Prince George's County offers multiple ballot drop box locations throughout the county for the 2026 Primary Election, including the College Park Community Center at 5051 Pierce Avenue, College Park, MD 20740, and the Deerfield Run Community Center.

Drop boxes remain open until 8:00 PM on Election Day.
Do I need a photo ID to vote in Maryland?
Most voters in Maryland don't need to show identification at the polls.
If you've voted in the state before, you'll simply state your name and address to the election judge.
You'll only be asked for ID if you're a first-time voter who didn't provide a driver's license number or the last four digits of your Social Security number when you registered.
Acceptable ID includes a Maryland driver's license, MVA ID card, change of address card, paycheck, bank statement, utility bill, or other government document with your name and address.
Who can vote in Maryland's closed primary?
Primary elections in Maryland are closed, meaning that only individuals registered as members of a political party can vote in that party's primary.
You can select your party affiliation when you register to vote.
Unaffiliated voters in Maryland can generally vote only for candidates running for nonpartisan offices such as the State Board of Education.
The deadline to select or change party affiliation for the 2026 primary was June 2, 2026.
Can college students vote in Maryland?
Yes.
College students from out of state studying in Maryland can vote in Maryland elections if they register to vote using their Maryland address.
Maryland college students from in-state can choose to remain registered at their permanent home address or register using their school address.
Consider where you want your vote to have the greatest impact on local issues, including District 25 races if you live in Prince George's County.
What is "mail-in voting" vs "absentee voting" in Maryland?
In Maryland, "absentee voting" is now officially called "mail-in voting." Maryland law requires the State Board of Elections and each local board of elections to refer to absentee ballots as mail-in ballots and absentee voting as mail-in voting.
This change in terminology does NOT change the process.
Any registered voter in Maryland can vote by mail without an excuse.
What if I received the wrong party ballot in Maryland?
Due to a printing error, some Maryland voters received the incorrect party ballot for the 2026 Gubernatorial Primary Election.
This error affects only voters who were mailed a ballot before May 14, 2026.
All affected voters were mailed a replacement ballot.
If you received an incorrect ballot, you should vote the replacement and destroy the original.
The Maryland State Board of Elections sent affected voters a postcard or email notification.

Can I register to vote if I have a past criminal conviction in Maryland?
Yes.
As of 2026, Maryland's HB 115 and SB 241 require automatic restoration of voter registration for individuals released from state prisons.
You're eligible to register if you're no longer serving a court-ordered sentence of imprisonment for a felony conviction.
Once you're released, your rights are automatically restored under Maryland law.
However, if you were convicted of buying or selling votes, you are not eligible to vote.
What is a provisional ballot in Maryland?
A provisional ballot is a safeguard used when your eligibility to vote is uncertain at the polling place.
You might use one if your name isn't on the precinct register or if you don't have the required ID for a first-time voter.
After Maryland polls close, election officials research your record.
If they confirm you're eligible and registered, your ballot is counted.
If you voted a provisional ballot due to a lack of ID, you have until 10 AM on the second Wednesday after Election Day to provide proper ID to the Prince George's County Board of Elections.

Early voting for the 2026 Maryland Gubernatorial Primary runs Thursday, June 11, 2026, through Thursday, June 18, 2026, including Saturday and Sunday.

Any registered Prince George's County District 25 voter can vote at any early voting center in the county.

Can I update my address on Election Day in Maryland?
Yes.
You can update your address during early voting or on Election Day in Maryland.
Go to the polling place assigned for your new address.
At your polling place, you will provide your new address, vote a provisional ballot, and election officials will update your address and count your provisional ballot.
This applies to District 25 residents who have moved within Prince George's County.
What should I do if I am harassed or intimidated at a Maryland polling place?
Report any instance of harassment or intimidation immediately to the chief poll judge at your location.
Maryland law strictly prohibits voter intimidation under the Election Law Article of the Annotated Code of Maryland.
The Maryland Voting Rights Act of 2026 strengthens protections against discriminatory practices at the county and municipal level, including in Prince George's County.
If the issue isn't resolved on-site, contact the Prince George's County Board of Elections at (301) 341-7300 or the Maryland State Board of Elections.

You may also bring your own translator into the voting booth, as long as they are not your employer or union representative.

Are there special voting provisions in Maryland for residents with disabilities?

If you're unable to enter the polling place due to a disability, you can request curbside voting from the Prince George's County Board of Elections.
