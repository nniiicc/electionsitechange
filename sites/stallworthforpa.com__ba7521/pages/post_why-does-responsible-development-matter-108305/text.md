TaWanda Stallworth Aug 7 5 min read Development can sound like a good word.
Here in Pennsylvania's 199th district, we’re hearing it a lot.
“New developments are coming!” “Look at all the progress!” Development sometimes does mean progress, growth, new buildings, new jobs, and new opportunities.
However, I would argue that development is not automatically progress.
Hard questions to ask about development are: Who benefits from it?
Who pays for it?
Who gets the contract, the profit, the ribbon-cutting ceremony, and all the accolades?
Even more importantly, who is left with the traffic, the water concerns, the higher costs, the changed neighborhood, the lost farmland, or any other consequences that no one wanted to talk about until after the decision was already made?
That is the real cost-benefit analysis.
Something can look nice on paper, a developer can make amazing promises, and numbers can be crunched to show that the project would bring in X amount of money.
However, the real cost-benefit analysis HAS to include the people who live here and the lives that will be directly impacted.
Here in Pennsylvania’s 199th District, these conversations are showing up in our townships, our boroughs, our neighborhoods, and our daily lives.
In Carlisle, we see land being turned into townhomes, apartments, and other projects that change how people live in the community.
Outside the borough, municipalities are being asked to consider turning farmland into data centers and warehouses.
In and around Newville, people have raised real concerns about water and land use.
Across Cumberland County, families are trying to understand what is happening, who is making the decisions, and whether anyone in power is listening before those decisions are final.
People deserve answers before the bulldozers show up.
They deserve to know how their water, roads, schools, farmland, property taxes, and neighborhoods may be affected—and they deserve those answers in plain language.
Not political spin.
Not “trust us.” Not a meeting where people feel like the decision was already made before they walked in the room.
Look, I am not against growth.
Nor am I against opportunity.
I am also all for building things our communities actually need.
However, I am against pretending that every project called “development” is automatically good for the people who live here—or treating rural communities like empty space on a map.
I am also against making decisions about water and land without fully listening to the people who will have to live with the results.
And I am definitely against any version of progress where some folks get richer while the rest of us are just trying to get by.
Here this clearly: Our land is not just acreage for somebody to cash in on.
It’s farms, backyards, roads, memories, and the place people are trying to build a life.
Our water is what we drink, cook with, bathe our babies in, and hand to our children before school.
It should never be treated like an acceptable risk in someone else’s profit plan.
And our neighborhoods are not chess pieces to be moved around by people who can collect the money and leave the rest of us with the consequences.
These are the places where people are raising children, caring for aging parents, building small businesses, going to church, coaching teams, attending school events, and trying to build a life with some breathing room.
That deserves respect, protection, and representation that is awake, informed, and accountable.
I know what it feels like when people in power talk around you instead of to you.
I know what it sounds like when folks use complicated language to make regular people feel like they are not supposed to ask questions.
However, you are allowed to ask questions.
In fact, I encourage you to!
You are allowed to ask what happens to your water, your roads, and your farmland.
You are allowed to ask whether a project will make it harder for your children to stay in the community where they were raised.
You are allowed to ask why decisions seem to move so quickly when money is involved, but so slowly when people need help.
If those questions make someone feel uncomfortable, well, then maybe that is exactly why they need to be asked.
I want to help residents understand what is happening in their communities.
I want to connect people to resources.
A state representative’s office should help people ask those questions and help people navigate systems, not make those systems more confusing.
It should be helping with things like rent rebates, aging services, state programs, and the everyday issues that affect whether people feel seen or forgotten by their government.
That is public service.
I believe the order of those words is extremely important.
Public.
Service.
The people come first.
The people who live here should shape what happens here.
Community input is vital.
I believe we can love this place enough to ask better questions.
We can welcome opportunity without handing working families the bill.
I believe we can make room for change without treating the people who already live here like they’re standing in the way of someone else’s plans.
That takes leadership with enough courage to tell the truth, and representation that listens before decisions are final.
The 199th District deserves that kind of representation.
We deserve someone in Harrisburg who understands that local government is not some faraway thing.
It is the water coming out of your faucet, the road your child rides to school, the rent that keeps climbing, the traffic getting heavier, the school district being stretched, and the services your parents count on as they age.
It is real life.
Development decisions are not just about buildings.
They are about people.
I am running because I believe the people of the 199th District deserve leadership rooted in service, accountability, and real life.
I understand that our communities are more than places on a map.
This is home—and our home is worth protecting.
