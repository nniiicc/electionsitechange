Ricciardi’s Work For Towns Deserves Our Support DONATE Select Page This is How We Lower Property Taxes Without an Income Tax Sep 25, 2026 This was published in the Union Leader on Friday, September 25, 2026 Matt McLaughlin’s “three-step plan” to lower property taxes may sound good as a political soundbite, but the math does not hold up.
Voters in Senate District 9 deserve real numbers that don’t slam their wallets and make higher taxes inevitable.
First: The state pays far more for education funding than McLaughlin admits.
He claims the state pays “around $4,800” per pupil instead of a $7,356 court-ordered floor, leaving a $2,500 gap that lands on your property tax bill.
New Hampshire’s actual funding formula targets aid to communities most in need based on the students receiving free and reduced lunch, special education and students who speak English as a second language.
Average per pupil education funding in New Hampshire is $7,132 and total funding exceeds $1 billion — a very different picture than the flat $4,800 figure he cites.
Second: McLaughlin’s math does not add up.
He claims raising business taxes, repealing educational choice for at-risk students and legalizing marijuana will fund the court mandate, which costs $7,356 per student or $536 million statewide.
He seems to believe that business tax hikes could raise $100 million annually — but only if tax hikes don’t cause a business slowdown.
Repealing educational choice could produce another $50 million.
Marijuana revenue at best will be small.
Even marijuana proponents estimate only $20 million in annual revenue.
So, McLaughlin’s plan is short $375 million.
What does that mean?
Simple.
McLaughlin can’t fund his plan without new taxes — lots of them.
For starters, in 2024 McLaughlin himself proposed dramatically increasing the state property tax.
That’s right — he actually proposed that — and it means higher property taxes.
McLaughlin’s plan is similar to a plan proposed on the State House steps that included a new state homeowner tax on top of local property taxes.
That homeowner tax could mean a $1,800 to $2,000 tax hike on a median-value home in New Hampshire.
Let’s not forget that homeowner tax was coupled with a 3% income tax.
A $375 million funding hole leads only one way — to the homeowner/income tax proposed on the State House steps.
Rest assured, I do not support a new homeowner tax or an income tax.
Third: McLaughlin and I have clear differences.
I’ve actually done something to combat rising property taxes rather than offering plans that don’t add up.
My legislation in 2021 that became part of the budget guaranteed towns receive 30% of the Rooms and Meals Tax revenue.
Since then, funding has doubled for towns.
The 13 towns in District 9 received $5.7 million this year.
I also fought for funding to fix municipal roads and bridges, which totaled $2.8 million.
Lastly, funding for public schools has increased $420 million over the last three budgets statewide — $47.6 million to schools in District 9 this year.
That totals $56.1 million for District 9.
Furthermore, I’ll introduce legislation in 2027 to increase the revenue sent to towns from the Rooms and Meals Tax to 40%, providing further help to local taxpayers.
Despite the millions of state funds sent to schools and towns, decisions that are made locally have led to property tax hikes.
I support legislation that gives local voters in this November’s election an opportunity to enact tax caps to curb property tax hikes.
Education spending has doubled while enrollment has declined more than 20% and test scores are flat or declining.
I support giving local voters the opportunity to adopt tax caps.
Where does McLaughlin stand on this taxpayer protection measure?
I believe we need to continue to provide resources to towns and schools without an income tax, sales tax or new homeowner tax.
The best way to do that is through a growing economy.
But McLaughlin’s plan to raise business taxes undermines New Hampshire’s ability to grow, attract and retain jobs.
Ten years ago, New Hampshire had the third-highest business taxes in the nation.
As these taxes fell, the economy grew and revenue exceeded expectations by $760 million.
McLaughlin wants to return to that third-highest rate in the nation.
That has adverse consequences as businesses can leave or expand elsewhere.
It’s certainly happening in other high-tax states.
It can happen here too.
New Hampshire is third in overall tax competitiveness, which includes property taxes, according to the Tax Foundation.
States around us have both an income and sales tax.
They also have higher property taxes than New Hampshire.
I’ll continue to fight for lower property taxes as I have done — without leading us to an income tax, a sales tax or new homeowner tax as Matt McLaughlin and his funding shortfall would do.
