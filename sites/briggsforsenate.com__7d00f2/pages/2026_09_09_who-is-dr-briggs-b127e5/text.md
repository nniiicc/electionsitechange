September 9, 2026 District 7 Briggs, who is 73, is now one of the senior members of the state Senate.
He is finishing his third term representing the oddly shaped 7th District , which in its current configuration is something of a buttonhook reaching from North Knox County through parts of East and West Knoxville before taking in most of West Knox County.
A retired U.S.
Army colonel and heart surgeon, he has at times angered cultural conservatives in his party.
He has not been a supporter of school vouchers, and he has introduced bills to allow more medical exceptions to the state’s abortion ban.
After controversy over the banning of Alex Haley’s book Roots from Knox County Schools libraries, Briggs called for revisiting the state’s Age Appropriate Materials Act to ensure an appeals process and more clearly define which materials should be excluded.
Briggs has been chair of the state Senate’s powerful State and Local Government Committee.
He attended the Legislature’s called special session in May to carve up Memphis’ congressional district into Republican-friendly chunks, but he did not vote on the final bill.
He has been one of the strongest voices in the Legislature for regulating and limiting the availability of hemp-derived THC, including the ban on THCA products that took effect July 1.
Briggs faced a primary challenge on Aug.
6 from the right — and one he was familiar with.
Kent Morrell ran against him in the Republican primary in 2022 and repeated the attempt this year, but Briggs prevailed easily both times, winning about two-thirds of the vote.
Now he has another repeat challenger in Langan, who has worked what he calls a variety of “blue-collar jobs” for most of the past three decades, including in the restaurant industry and manual trades.
Langan also ran against Briggs in 2022, in a race that Briggs won with 62 percent of the vote.
(You can read our coverage of that 2022 contest right here .) Langan is running on two broad issues, accountability and affordability, under which he advocates for a number of priorities: expanding Medicaid and public pre-K instruction, campaign finance reform, state legislative term limits, fair district maps, and ending sales tax on groceries and some clothing and footwear.
He also supports more funding for public schools and infrastructure, access to reproductive health including abortion, and legalizing cannabis.
As a Senate committee chair, Briggs is something of a magnet for campaign contributions.
He started 2026 with more than $600,000 in his campaign account, and as of the Aug.
6 primary that had risen to $661,287.11.
(That includes $110,000 in self-endorsed loans.) His donors include healthcare PACs (Blue Cross Blue Shield, Tennessee Hospital Association) as well as business groups like the National Federation of Independent Businesses and the Tennessee Bankers Association.
Langan, not surprisingly, lags far behind in campaign resources.
He started the year with $3,073.13 in his account, and as of the primary had $1,307.98 on hand — about 1/500th of Briggs’ total.
