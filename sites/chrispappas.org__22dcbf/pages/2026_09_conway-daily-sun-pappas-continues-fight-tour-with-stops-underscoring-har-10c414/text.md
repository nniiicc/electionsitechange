Gilbert said: “We look at the health of the entire population in Carroll County and try to keep tabs on the conditions that are either detrimental to people’s health or improve their health.” Gilbert said clinical care makes up about 20 percent of a person’s overall health and well-being — a critical component of health, but not the only one.
In addition, he said, there are what are called the social determinants of health, things like food, housing and transportation, and noted that many people in Carroll County have problems meeting basic needs and often have to make choices between seeing a doctor and buying medicine or putting food on the table and gas in their car to get to work.

Rep.
Chris Pappas during a visit Thursday focused on tariffs, fuel costs and workforce shortages.
Pappas, a Democrat who represents New Hampshire’s 1st Congressional District, and is running for U.S.
Senate, stopped at the Albany plastics manufacturer to hear how economic pressures have affected them.
“Small businesses have been dealing with so much uncertainty as a result of tariffs and trade policy and higher fuel prices,” Pappas said.
He said tariffs are especially difficult for small manufacturers when there is no practical domestic alternative.
“That’s why this is nuts because it’s one thing if you had opportunities to source things locally and, you know, could pivot in that direction, but you really don’t,” Pappas said.
“You’re just adding cost to your business, to your customers, for something that you can’t get here locally.” The war in Iran has made things even worse, the congressman said.
“Even if this war ends tomorrow, we’re looking at a dislocation in energy markets for months to come, and potentially years.
And we’ve already endured seven months of this,” said Pappas.
“So our country is in a really tough spot right now.” Ambix President Melissa Florio and founder Jeff Nicoll, who are husband and wife, said the company remains busy, particularly because its largest market is power distribution.
Nicoll said Ambix makes about 11 million parts a year at the Albany facility.
He said the company has seen an uptick in that work this year because of electrical infrastructure projects and storm-related repairs.
Some machines run nearly around the clock during the week, he said, and the company employs about a dozen people.
But costs have climbed sharply.
Florio said the company recently raised prices after trying for months to absorb increases from suppliers.
“We kept thinking it might get better.
It kept getting worse,” she said.
One recent material order cost 60 cents more per pound than the company’s previous order, which amounts to a 30 percent increase Florio and Nicoll said.
Surcharges have also increased, while longer lead times have made production planning more difficult.
“It just ripples everywhere,” Florio said.
“That’s why we finally have to raise our prices, which we never like to do.” Tariffs have created another challenge because Ambix has some of its molds and tooling made overseas.
Nicoll said the company had already agreed to fixed prices with customers on some projects when new tariffs took effect, leaving Ambix to absorb costs it had not anticipated, the couple said.
“I wound up losing money on the tooling,” Nicoll said.
Florio said much of Ambix’s tooling is made in China, in part because domestic toolmakers who once handled that type of work have retired or are booked with long-term projects.
Pappas said he has legislation to exempt small businesses from certain tariffs and refund tariffs already paid.
“It makes no sense to put the burden on our main street businesses, our small manufacturers, with this haphazard, reckless trade policy,” he said.
Ambix is trying to add workers.
Florio said the company actively searches for employees and has worked with adult education and other organizations to find applicants.
She said housing and child-care costs are keeping people out of the workforce.
Despite the pressures, the company is expanding its manufacturing space.
Nicoll said solar panels at the facility offset about 55 percent of Ambix’s electricity use.
Florio said the problems Ambix faces are not unique to plastics manufacturers.
“Whatever your raw material is, whether it’s fabric or steel, we’re all in the same boat as manufacturers,” she said.
