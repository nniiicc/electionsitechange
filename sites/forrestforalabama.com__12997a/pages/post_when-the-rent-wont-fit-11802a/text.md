How the math broke for renters here, and what we're building to fix it.
A steady, full-time job used to cover a roof over your head.
For a growing number of working families here, it no longer does.
The nursing assistant picking up weekend shifts and the daycare worker across the hall hand over more of a paycheck than the math is supposed to allow.
Housing is supposed to cost about a third of your income.
Ask a renter in Huntsville when they last hit that number.
How the math broke Rents in Huntsville climbed into the $1,300 to $1,400 range and stayed there, flat year over year, near the top of what hourly work can carry.
Pay for that work never caught up.
Do the arithmetic on a household earning $40,000: a $1,350 apartment eats about 40% of gross income, before groceries, child care, or a car repair.
Underneath the price is a supply problem.
Builders here put up expensive new houses and almost nothing at the affordable end.
(The new ones carry their own problem : Alabama requires no written warranty behind them, and lets a builder waive the implied one.) Vouchers and income-restricted apartments exist to bridge that gap.
The Huntsville Housing Authority has closed its voucher waitlist, so a family doing everything right has nowhere to put its name.
When supply at the bottom doesn't grow, renters compete for the same scarce units and the price moves one direction.
A person working two jobs shouldn't have to win a lottery to afford a safe place to live.
When the rent outruns the paycheck, blame the shortage of homes, not the renter.
We can build our way out of that.
The fund Alabama created and never filled Alabama created an Affordable Housing Trust Fund in 2012 , the legal vehicle to finance homes working families can afford.
The legislature has never put a dollar into it.
Forty-eight states have created one, and at least 861 operate nationwide at the state, county, and city level, most paired with a dedicated revenue source Alabama has never passed.
This year the legislature had its chance.
SB 244 would have funded Alabama's through an increase in the mortgage recording fee.
It died in committee, its House companion HB 401 died the same way, and the session adjourned in April.
The tool exists.
Creating it took one bill in 2012.
Filling it has taken fourteen years and counting.
Who absorbs it Renters pay first, one bad month from an eviction that resets everything.
Their kids pay next: a forced move in March means a new school in April, and the older, cheaper units carry the untested radon, lead, and mold that run through this county's aging housing.
Employers pay last.
The same growth pulling tens of thousands of jobs into Cummings Research Park and Redstone needs somewhere for those workers to sleep, and Huntsville is running out of places to put them.
The other lever doesn't run through Montgomery Picture the new construction you've driven past: big houses on big lots, or large complexes, with almost nothing in between that a working family could rent or buy.
The city's zoning code allows little else.
Minimum lot sizes, parking minimums, and bans on duplexes and small multi-family hold the supply down.
Loosen them and you get more homes at more price points.
Minneapolis loosened its rules and grew its housing stock 12% while rents rose 1%.
The rest of Minnesota added 4% and watched rents climb 14%.
Parking rules push the same way: the last national study to measure it found a bundled parking space added about $1,700 a year to rent, so a city that requires one prices people out of a unit they may never park at.
Huntsville came close in 2024.
The Planning Commission approved an amendment legalizing accessory dwelling units , then the Council pulled it from the agenda hours before the vote and never brought it back.
An ADU is still illegal to build here.
Zoning belongs to cities and counties in Alabama, so the Council can do this without waiting on Montgomery.
What we're building Six changes would close the gap, and we'll take whichever door opens first.
Together they make up our housing plank .
Fund the Alabama Housing Trust Fund.
Put real, dedicated revenue into the vehicle the state already created, the way SB 244 would have.
An empty fund builds nothing.
Build more of the affordable end.
Alabama's Workforce Housing Tax Credit is capped at $5 million a year statewide and expires September 30, 2027.
Extend it, raise the cap, and grow the supply of income-restricted homes so a working family has somewhere to apply.
Reform zoning at the city and county level.
Legalize accessory dwelling units, duplexes, and small multi-family, and drop parking minimums, so the market can build homes working families can afford.
This doesn't require a single vote in Montgomery.
Open a real path to ownership.
Support Community Land Trust homes and similar models that let a working family buy in at a price they can carry and start building equity, instead of renting forever.
Set basic tenant protections.
Reasonable notice, habitability standards, and a buffer against the rent shocks that turn one lease renewal into a forced move.
Alabama law is thin here.
Test rentals for radon and lead.
The state's free radon kit program is currently out of stock, and even when it is running, nothing requires a landlord to test an older unit or tell a tenant what turned up.
A renter's child breathes the same untested air as an owner's.
What to do right now The law hasn't caught up yet.
A few things help today.
Apply where the list is still open.
The Huntsville Housing Authority is taking public housing applications, and your place on the waiting list is set by the date you apply, so apply sooner rather than later.
Call HHA at (256) 539-0774 to confirm the list is still open before you count on it.
The Section 8 voucher list is closed for now, so watch for it to reopen and apply directly to income-restricted properties in the meantime.
Put repair requests in writing to your landlord and keep a copy, so you have a record if the landlord ignores them.
Test for radon.
Madison County sits in the EPA's highest-risk zone.
The Alabama Department of Public Health offers one free kit per household, though the kits are out of stock at the moment, so check back or pick up a low-cost kit in the meantime.
Stay informed and get involved If you rent in Madison County and full-time work still doesn't cover it, this is about you.
Check whether you are in District 21 and find the registration and absentee deadlines on the voting information page Share this with someone who's been priced out: a coworker, a friend, the person next to you on a double shift.
Bring a neighbor to a community event so more renters are in the room, and sign up for updates to follow the housing plank.
Volunteer.
A few hours knocking doors or making calls moves this further than anything else.
Sign up here .
