Sep 14 Written By Melody Moser UTAH GRIT Rod Moser · Candidate, Utah House District 45 Utah led on AI once.
We can lead again.
What this technology gives us, what it costs us, and twelve bills the Legislature could run next session.
Rod Moser South Jordan, September 2026 In July, about seven hundred AI agents that OpenAI was testing got out of the box they were supposed to stay in.
They had been put in isolated environments.
No internet.
No way to talk to each other.
They found a way anyway, leaving messages for one another on a shared message board nobody had authorized.
Then they went out and ran code on forty-one production servers belonging to a company called Hugging Face.
Nobody instructed them to do it.
OpenAI investigated, brought in outside researchers, and published the whole report themselves.
The July incident OpenAI technical report, August 26, 2026 July 8 Agents escape their sandbox through a vulnerability in an internally hosted server.
July 10 An agent finds publicly exposed Hugging Face credentials.
Agents begin coordinating on a message board nobody set up.
July 13 Hugging Face detects the activity and shuts it down.
By then roughly 700 agents have participated.
July 16 Hugging Face publicly discloses the security incident.
July 20 OpenAI connects unusual activity on its own network back to the same agents.
Aug 26 OpenAI publishes a 38-page technical report.
METR and Redwood Research publish an independent investigation the same day.
That one made the news.
I want to talk about the part that didn't.
I sell houses.
Thirty years, somewhere north of 1,500 transactions, most of them right here in the valley.
In that time I have watched software move from helping people to deciding about people, and hardly anybody I meet at the door knows it happened.
An algorithm sets the rent on the apartment your daughter just signed for.
A different one scored her application before a human being ever opened it.
If she got turned down, nobody is required to tell her why, and there is no person she can call and ask.
That is happening this week, in this county, to people on my walk list.
Start with what the thing actually is Artificial intelligence is software that reads an enormous pile of examples, learns the patterns inside them, and then makes something new out of those patterns.
A paragraph.
A diagnosis.
A credit decision.
A line of code.
Strip the marketing off and you have a very fast, very well-read apprentice who has never left the building and has no idea what it doesn't know.
It is saving lives.
It is also making decisions about people with no explanation and no appeal.
Both of those are true at the same time, and an honest conversation has to carry both.
What it gives us Medicine These systems read imaging and catch things that get missed.
They have pulled drug discovery timelines from years down to months.
For a county in this state with no specialist on staff, that gap is a diagnosis or a missed one.
Small business A two-person shop can now do the marketing, the books, and the customer service that used to take three more hires.
The people it helps most are the ones who never had the capital to make those hires.
Water We run one of the most contested water systems in the country.
Better modeling of snowpack, demand, and reservoir behavior is worth real money and real acre-feet on the Colorado and the Great Salt Lake.
School A kid who needs something explained a sixth way can get it explained a sixth way, at nine at night, without a tutor the family can't pay for.
My dad taught in Utah public schools for close to forty years.
He would have taken that deal.
Anybody telling you to shut the whole thing down for a decade should have to say out loud what that decade costs.
Treatments never found.
Forecasts never run.
Those are real numbers too.
Nobody writes headlines about them.
Where it goes wrong Four kinds of trouble.
Coverage tends to skip straight to the fourth.
1.
The harm already here Cloned voices calling grandparents.
Fake intimate images of real teenagers.
Chatbots that will talk to a lonely fourteen-year-old for six hours and never once suggest she go find an adult.
Utah has already legislated on some of this, and credit where it is due.
THE ONE NOBODY COVERS 2.
Decisions you can't see Rent pricing.
Tenant screening.
Insurance denials.
Resumes filtered out before a human reads them.
This is the one that touches the most people in District 45 and gets the least airtime.
3.
The jobs question The AI companies themselves are forecasting serious displacement in entry-level white-collar work inside of five years.
Maybe they are wrong.
I have six grown sons, and I am not willing to bet their first jobs on it.
4.
The frontier On September 9, Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment science at Anthropic, said publicly that he puts the odds of this technology killing everybody above ten percent within the decade, and that his company does not yet have a plan to solve it.
Take those numbers or leave them.
What stays with me is who is saying it.
The people with the most money riding on this are the ones asking for a brake pedal in the car.
You do not see that often.
Where Utah actually stands Utah has been ahead of most states.
That is worth saying plainly, and it was a Republican legislature that did it.
2024 SB 149 , Artificial Intelligence Policy Act.
The first AI-specific consumer protection law in the country.
Created the Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy and a regulatory sandbox.
2025 SB 226.
Expanded disclosure for high-risk interactions involving health, financial, and biometric data.
HB 452.
Mental health chatbot protections.
SB 271.
Extended identity protection to AI-generated likeness, voice, and image.
2026 Nine more bills.
Devices in schools, AI literacy standards, deepfake provenance and takedown, AI-generated CSAM, human oversight in insurance preauthorization, online age verification.
AND THEN ONE THAT STOPPED HB 286 (2026), the Artificial Intelligence Transparency Act.
Sponsored by Rep.
Doug Fiefia, a Republican from Herriman, with Sen.
Mike McKell, also a Republican, carrying it in the Senate.
Frontier developers would publish safety and child protection plans, disclose risk assessments, report safety incidents to the state AI office, and be barred from retaliating against whistleblowers.
I thought it was a good bill.
It cleared the House Economic Development and Workforce Services Committee without a single vote against it.
UNANIMOUS committee vote, HB 286 0 votes against House Economic Development and Workforce Services Committee, 2026 General Session.
Then this happened.
January 19, 2026 Rep.
Doug Fiefia introduces HB 286.
January 27 Fiefia testifies in committee.
The bill is not about banning AI, he tells members, it exists because some systems are powerful enough to shape a child's behavior or put the public at risk.
January 28 A substitute version is proposed, with Sen.
Mike McKell listed as Senate sponsor.
The bill clears committee unanimously and waits to be called for a House floor vote.
February 12 The White House Office of Intergovernmental Affairs writes to Utah Senate Majority Leader Kirk Cullimore Jr.
The administration is categorically opposed to HB 286 and views it as an unfixable bill that goes against the administration's AI agenda.
The letter offers no legal rationale.
February 15 Axios reports the letter.
It is the first known instance of the White House intervening directly with a Republican-led state legislature to stop AI regulation.
March 6 The session ends.
HB 286 never gets a floor vote.
A Republican transparency bill had unanimous committee support in a Republican legislature, and never got a vote after a letter arrived from Washington.
Draw your own conclusion about who is really running Utah.
Twelve bills Utah could run I have grouped these by how hard the fight is.
The order says nothing about which ones matter most.
Every one of them has to be drafted against current Utah Code first, because this Legislature has moved fast and some of the ground is already covered.
Tier one High impact, real bipartisan path 1 Utah Artificial Intelligence Transparency Act Run HB 286 again.
Frontier developers publish a safety plan and a child protection plan, disclose their risk assessments, report safety incidents to the Office of AI Policy, and cannot fire the employee who raises a hand.
Same compute threshold.
Same penalties.
It already cleared committee without a single no vote.
Utah settled the policy question in February.
What is left is whether Utah legislates for Utah.
2 Algorithmic Rent-Setting Prohibition Act No landlord may use shared pricing software that pools nonpublic competitor data to set rents or hold units off the market.
Enforcement through the Division of Consumer Protection.
This is the one I would carry first.
When three big complexes on the same road feed their numbers into the same engine and all get the same recommendation back, that is price fixing with a software license stapled to it.
Other states and cities have already moved.
3 Right to Human Review Act If an automated system made or substantially made an adverse decision about your housing, your job, your insurance, your credit, or a public benefit, you get told that, and you get to ask a qualified human with authority to reverse it to look again.
Utah already did exactly this for insurance preauthorization in SB 319.
This carries the same principle over to the decisions that determine whether a family has a roof and a paycheck.
4 AI Whistleblower Protection Act Job protection for people inside AI companies who report a safety concern to a regulator.
Written to stand on its own.
If the bigger bill draws fire, this is the piece nobody wants to be recorded voting against.
Tier two Consumers and families 5 AI Companion Chatbot Safety Act Age-appropriate design, repeated disclosure that the user is talking to a machine, real crisis-response protocols, no selling the conversation data, and limits on design built to keep a minor in the chat as long as possible.
6 Synthetic Media in Elections Act Disclosure on any political ad using AI-generated or materially altered footage of a real candidate, with a fast civil remedy in the last sixty days of a race.
Has to be drafted narrow enough to survive a First Amendment challenge and to stay clear of SB 256 and SB 271.
7 Voice Cloning and Elder Fraud Prevention Act Enhanced penalty for financial fraud that uses a synthetic voice or likeness to pose as a family member or a bank, paired with a public education push through the Division of Aging and Adult Services.
Ask anybody who works with older sellers whether this one is theoretical.
8 Automated Decision Transparency in Tenant Screening A screening company that denies an application by score has to hand over the factors that drove the denial and a way to fix the underlying data when it is wrong.
I have watched good tenants get denied over somebody else's record.
Tier three Infrastructure, workforce, public sector 9 Data Center Resource Disclosure and Ratepayer Protection Act Large computing facilities report annual water use, power draw, and any negotiated utility rate to the Public Service Commission.
Residential and small commercial ratepayers do not subsidize infrastructure built to serve one enormous customer.
We are siting a great deal of compute in this state.
The water and power arguments are already running hot in Utah County and out in the west desert.
This is an affordability bill wearing an infrastructure hat.
10 State Government AI Inventory and Procurement Standards Act Every agency publishes a yearly list of the AI systems it uses to make decisions about the public.
No public benefit gets denied by a machine alone.
Vendors disclose where their training data came from and what the system is known to get wrong.
11 Workforce Transition and AI Displacement Reporting Act Employers over a certain size notify the Department of Workforce Services when AI adoption is a substantial factor in a layoff above a threshold, which opens the door to retraining money.
Built on the WARN notification we already have rather than a new bureaucracy.
12 Critical Infrastructure Autonomous Agent Safeguards Act No autonomous AI agent gets unsupervised write access to a water system, the electric grid, or emergency dispatch.
A human authorizes consequential actions.
Incidents get reported to the state.
July is the whole argument.
Agents that were supposed to be sealed off found each other and went to work.
You do not have to believe anybody's extinction math to want a person standing between software and the culinary water.
What leading looks like now Utah went first in 2024.
We built a sandbox instead of a wall, brought the companies in, and got written up all over the country for it.
That was the right call.
Going first once is easy to be proud of.
Doing it a second time, after Washington has made clear it would rather we didn't, is the harder version.
It is also the version worth something.
One idea runs through all twelve If a machine makes a decision about your home, your job, or your child, you deserve to know about it, and you deserve to ask a human being to take another look.
This technology is coming either way.
The part still up for grabs is who writes the terms, and whether anybody in South Jordan gets a say in them.
Tell me where I am wrong.
Go check me.
