I was a member of the Association of Talent Development when I was a corporate trainer and the South Carolina Writers Association when I published my novels.
Professional organizations offer professional development, networking, and other advantages to your career.
And some are engaged in political advocacy.
Since filing for office in March, I have received a dozen surveys from various groups asking for my opinion on a range of issues.
These are special interest groups built to focus on the education and advocacy of specific issues.
Some are watch dogs, or non-partisan groups that want to reform our systems for equal access: The League of Women Voters asked me about my willingness to protect voter access.
Better Ballot SC asked about my willingness to advance ballot reform in the manner of instant runoff voting.
Kasie speaking at DKG Legislative Day in October 2025 Some other organizations have very specific political agendas: Moms Demand Action asked me about my willingness to support legislation limiting access to firearms.
STARRS (Stand Together Against Racism and Radicalism in the Services) asked if I would support or oppose DEI programs.
Some other organizations are the professional associations I mentioned above, and the South Carolina REALTORS candidate screening committee was one of those.
I completed a survey for them and when they didn’t invite me for an interview, I reached out and requested one.
It did not go well.
They sent me an email saying I’d received a “no position” rating which indicates I do not support Realtor interests.
What are those interests and why would they say that?
The question I remember best was about whether I would support legislation that makes it easier for first time home buyers to get a mortgage.
I do not support federal programs that provide taxpayer-funded subsidies to lenders.
That is how the student loan market became so distorted.
Federally-backed loans pushed money into the education market and universities raised tuition, for-profit colleges popped up everywhere, and millions of students found themselves saddled with outrageous debt for useless degrees.
The federal government did something similar in housing years ago.
Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are government-backed entities that protect banks by purchasing home loans from commercial enterprises.
They lower the risk to banks by bailing them out of risky loans.
This is government intervention that distorts the market.
If the loans purchased by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac default – meaning the homeowner can’t pay – the Treasury makes the payment so the lender doesn’t lose money.
If that sounds like taxpayers funding banks, that’s because it is.
And now, with the inventory problem spawned by decades of underbuilding and the 2020 supply chain disruptions, local regulations limiting multi-family home development, and the burdensome requirements of economic impact legislation, we have a housing shortage.
Add to that the interest rates that are higher than anyone wants them to be, and strict lending regulations that want to ensure only people who can pay back the loan are given one, and it’s hard to sell houses. ( US Chamber of Commerce Analysis resource. ) Would I support legislation that makes it easier for first-time home buyers?
First time homebuyers need three things: 1) a down payment, 2) a budget, and 3) a right-sized property they can afford.
I support reducing the national debt so that we stop the erosion of our currency and make it easier for new home buyers to save.
I support reducing taxes on social security and encouraging tax-deferred savings accounts so that workers can save their money toward big investments like education and home ownership.
I do not support expansion of federal programs that buoy banks so they can make riskier loans like the sub-prime mortgages that drove the 2008 financial crisis.
Professional association interest groups, like the REALTORS, are focused on a single perspective: making their industry flourish.
Most industries are subject to five forces: Buyer power, supplier power, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, and industry rivalry.
Interest groups lobby governments to enact legislation and regulations that affect these five forces.
For example, when the threat of new entrants goes up, lobbyists ask for regulations that make it more difficult for new businesses to come into the industry.
When the threat of substitutes goes up, lobbyists ask for legislation that makes it harder for customers to choose alternatives.
In real estate, those examples might be Zillow coming online and offering home buyers a direct view to listings without needing an agent to schedule visits.
Or AirBnB and the rise of short-term rental investment properties eating away at single-family-home inventory.
A free market system would say that innovations like Zillow and AirBnB offer opportunities to grow a market, get more people engaged, and force competition that drives prices down.
We might also see large investment companies buying up inventory and artificially inflating prices.
We might also see consolidation in suppliers that puts constraints on inventory.
Markets are living, breathing things and when they tighten, something has to give.
Change is certain.
But entities that are making money in the existing market don’t want market change, not if those changes could threaten their own prosperity.
As understandable as that resistance to change is, I believe market change is inherently good for consumers.
Influencing government to restrict, inflate, or otherwise distort a market is wrong.
Companies should evolve to meet the new challenges of their industry.
And if they fail to do so, they should suffer the consequences.
It sounds terrible to say a fifty-year-old firm that is a pillar of the community might simply go obsolete and take all of its employees and assets with it.
But that is the nature of competition.
Rigging the game to ensure you keep winning is wrong.
I am not opposed to professional organizations.
I’m not even opposed to them lobbying.
I think hearing from those organizations will be very valuable to me as your Senator.
I don’t know everything about every industry, it’s impossible to master all the nuances of the business world.
But I know my first principles on this: competition is good and companies that are afraid to compete don’t deserve to win.
I see government intervention, market distortion, especially when it’s done to benefit specific players, as unacceptable.
When the Realtors asked me if I would support a range of legislative actions to further insulate their industry from technology, innovation, and the inherent challenges of competition those enhancements would create, I said no.
I will not support legislation that distorts markets.
First time home buyers need a down payment: let’s make it easier for them to save and let’s stop eroding the value of the dollar with extraneous debt.
First time home buyers need a budget.
How many young people are taught how to measure their revenue against their expenses on a monthly, quarterly, or annual basis?
Tell the truth.
First time home buyers need to be realistic about the homes they can afford.
If agents want to sell to new home buyers, maybe start looking for right-fit properties.
Subsidizing lenders is not the way to sell more houses.
I did not tell the Realtors what they wanted to hear.
Which might have surprised them.
I am a candidate, after all; I’m supposed to say whatever it takes to get elected, right?
I think I’m proud of the “no position” designation.
I’m the only candidate in my race the association rated.
But I disagree that I don’t support Realtor issues.
I strongly support the profession of real estate agents, I have good friends who are agents.
I want their industry to flourish, I want people to prosper, and I want new home buyers to realize the benefits of investment and ownership of an appreciating asset.
I want all of that.
I just don’t want the government trying to force those outcomes.
There are always unintended consequences when the government picks industry winners.
Government intervention distorts markets.
I believe a free market will deliver opportunity, maybe not in the surpluses some groups want (think 2007), but in the end, a market that polices itself is always better than one manipulated by government.
Interest groups use their influence to gain government intervention.
The REALTORs are going to tell their membership of 30,000 professionals that I won’t work in their best interests.
And if I were going to say whatever it takes to win, I might have lied to them.
But I didn’t.
One million South Carolinians know the government isn’t working for us.
So let’s do something about it.
