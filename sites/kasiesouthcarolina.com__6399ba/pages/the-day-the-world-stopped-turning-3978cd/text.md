I wanted to be a sports writer, that was the job I’d left in Central, S.C. when Charlie and I moved to Charlotte so he could play drums in the rock band Backyard Green.
I had tried to turn my 1-year of experience into a job in sports media but it was slow going in a market where I knew nobody.
So I was temping as a marketing assistant for a company called Vehi-Care.
My first engagement with a real entrepreneurial start-up.
I basically did all the stuff my boss, a cool guy named Joe who was just five years older than me, didn’t want to do.
I was listening to sports talk radio on the way into the office, running late as usual because I was 24 and didn’t do a good job of managing myself, and was probably hungover from whatever we had gotten into the night before.
I parked, ducked into the building, and seated myself quietly – inconspicuously – at the front desk.
Not long after I got there, my boss came over.
“Did you hear?” he asked,
“Yeah, Michael Jordan is coming back to the NBA,” I said.
“No, a plane hit the World Trade Center.”
I leapt up and followed him into the conference room where the entire staff was gathered, not for a meeting like I’d thought when I snuck in, but to watch in horror as a second plane crashed into the second tower.
And we stood, as witnesses, to the live broadcast of thousands of our fellow Americans being burned alive.
That is my 9/11 memory.
Everybody has one.
The “Where were you?” moment Alan Jackson sang to us about.
How did it feel?
What did you think?
Scary.
Panic.
Devastation.
The Pentagon.
The Pennsylvania field.
The hits kept coming.
And we watched it all unfold.
And for days, we kept vigilance over the TV and waited for more.
We waited for explanations.
We listened to the stories.
We gave blood.
Then the weekend came and every sports league suspended play.
Every single one.
And some of us thought, “We should play and show the terrorists that they can’t beat us!” Because by then we knew it was terrorists.
And some of us thought, “How could I sit in a stadium and watch a game while they’re digging bodies out of the rubble in New York?” Because by then they were not finding buried survivors.
I still have the issue of Sports Illustrated with the American flag draped over the empty bleacher seat with the headline, “The Week Sports Stood Still.”
We were stunned.
Angry.
Ashamed.
Scared.
Sad.
Confused.
It’s been 25 years.
Charlie and I celebrated our 25th wedding anniversary this past July 28th.
So much has changed.
We’ve raised a daughter and sent her off to college.
We’ve bought and sold one house and then bought another and put on a screened-in porch.
We’re adulting.
We have investments.
A business.
Two published novels.
A statewide campaign for elected office.
The world has changed.
We understand and respect the threat of terrorists.
No matter how loosely the word is thrown around to justify military action or how often it’s used as a synonym for Islam.
We have accepted the surveillance state from cameras in intersections to the NSA screening cellular calls, from facial recognition technology to digital cookies and coincidental ads on Facebook.
We are complicit in our own eroded liberty.
We watch movies like Sleepless in Seattle and remember when you could meet your arriving family members at the airport gate.
We saw the rise of the Marvel Universe as Hollywood’s way of meeting our need for heroic avengers.
We held our loved ones a little tighter after 9/11, grateful we weren’t the ones burying husbands, wives, mothers, fathers, brothers, sisters, colleagues and friends.
We held our loved ones tighter and prayed they would stay safe.
We started valuing safety.
It’s why we give in to the surveillance state.
We put Life 360 on phones and track our family’s movements like creeps.
It’s why we let things like qualified immunity and civil asset forfeiture persist.
We believe there are criminals among us and law enforcement keeps us safe.
It’s why we consume hormone-boosted chicken, processed cheese, and take supplements for our health.
The FDA says they’re all okay and we will be, too, once the pharmaceuticals kick in.
In 25 years we’ve seen safety become the justification for war after war, escalating military conflicts, interventions, preventative strikes.
We have become a war mongering nation in the name of our own safety.
Because we were attacked once.
Remember?
And we were afraid.
And that fear has been weaponized against us.
Right when we seemed to be waking up from it, when we might have been getting a little bit of liberty back, COVID-19 came.
And we were afraid again.
And a population that is afraid won’t rebel.
It won’t disobey.
It won’t question the means of its own oppression.
Those who would sacrifice liberty for safety deserve neither liberty nor safety.
And yet we make the safe bet, take the safe road, buy the safe product, and listen to the safe opinions.
We blame the other for the badness and protect our own goodness by staying within the boundaries.
We pay our taxes.
Raise our children.
Build our businesses.
And when we wonder, “Is this as good as it gets?”
We remember that once we were attacked.
And then our own government took away our liberties one at the time under the guise of security.
I think about that Sports Illustrated every now and then.
I remember how it felt to get it, to see the profound effect that Tuesday morning had on our culture.
And I wonder how different our world would be now if it had never happened.
I wonder if our political machinery would be so negative, hateful, and scary if they hadn’t learned how easily fear could cow us.
I wonder if our national debt would be so gargantuan if we hadn’t decided to solve every problem with defense spending.
I wonder if Social Security would be as fiercely defended if it were called welfare for senior citizens instead of “security.”
I think about the families who were forever changed because of 9/11.
Not just the attacks, or the aftermath and illnesses that directly correspond to it.
But the soldiers who signed up in 1999 thinking they’d leverage the military for college tuition and ended up on multiple tours in Afghanistan and Iraq.
We were forever changed by 9/11 and twenty-five years later we can discuss and debate what it meant, what it did, and whether our nation has ever actually healed from that day.
We can even talk about what the younger generation needs to know and whether they seem to “get” it.
In my classroom today, I’ll compare GenX’s 9/11 with GenZ’s COVID-19 lockdowns as life changing events.
How you had a vision for your life before it, and the vision for your life was altered afterwards.
How regular things went obsolete overnight and even if you miss them, you don’t expect them to ever come back.
I’ll talk about patriotism and whether the actions of government in a time of crisis are inherently unifying or inherently divisive.
I’ll ask about freedom and liberty and how much of it they expect to have, how much of it they expect to be protected by their government.
And I’ll ask about grief.
And whether they think we ever really get over the kinds of betrayals that scar an entire generation.
And then we’ll move on.
In love for our country, love for the people who inhabit it, and love for liberty and freedom on which it was founded, I hope you all have a blessed and safe September 11th.
May your hearts be filled with gratitude and your eyes be filled with hope.
