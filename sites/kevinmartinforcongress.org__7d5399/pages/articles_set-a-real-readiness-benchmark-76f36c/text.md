Every year the defense debate runs the same way.
One side says spend more.
The other says spend less.
Both argue about a percentage.
Almost nobody asks the question I would ask first, which is - what do we actually have and how much do we need?
In my line of work, you do not start with the check.
You start with the inventory.
What is on hand, what condition is it in, what is committed, what is obsolete, and what would it take to meet the obligations we have already made.
Only then does anyone talk about buying more.
We do defense in the opposite order and then wonder why the number never seems to be enough.
What a benchmark would actually be A readiness benchmark is a plain statement of what we must be able to do, and by when.
How many aircraft are mission-capable on a given day, not how many we own.
How many months of critical munitions we hold against a defined scenario.
How long it takes to replace what gets used.
Whether the industrial base can produce a replacement at the rate we would consume it.
These are countable things.
Some are classified, and they should be, but the existence of a standard does not have to be secret.
Congress can be told the standard and told whether we are meeting it.
Without a benchmark, "are we spending enough on defense" is not a question anybody can answer.
It is a mood.
And a mood is a terrible basis for the second largest line in the federal budget.
Why I am careful with the numbers here I want to be straight about something.
When people talk about defense spending as a share of the economy, large figures get thrown around casually, including by people who support the military and mean well.
I am not going to do that.
If I use a percentage, it will be one I can source, and I would rather use none than one I cannot.
That is not a small point for me.
If I am going to run on being the person who reads the numbers, the first place that has to show up is the numbers I use about my own priorities.
Recent GAO assessments show that US military readiness remains a significant concern across multiple areas.
Without hard data to support numbers and percentage, we cannot assess what level our military strength and capacity is currently at today.
This is due to ongoing conflicts with Russia and Ukraine, unrest in the Middle East, potential issues in the South China Sea with China and Taiwan.
What Congress actually controls Congress authorizes and appropriates all of it.
It writes the National Defense Authorization Act every year, which is one of the few bills that reliably passes.
That matters because it means the vehicle already exists.
A readiness benchmark does not require new machinery.
It requires the existing annual bill to contain a standard and a reporting requirement rather than only a dollar figure.
Congress also decides what gets bought, and this is where district politics collide with readiness.
Programs survive because of where they are built, not because of what they contribute.
Everyone knows this.
Almost nobody says it during an election.
What needs to change Put a readiness standard in the NDAA.
Defined, measurable, reported annually, with the classified detail handled appropriately and the topline answer public.
Report against it before the next appropriation.
The sequence matters.
Status first, then the request.
That is how any competent organization does capital planning.
Count what we have before buying more.
If the inventory is not reliable, fix the inventory.
Which brings us to the audit, and I have written about that separately.
Like a business with inventory, we need to develop and maintain cycle counts with inventory.
Be willing to say a program is not worth it.
Including one built in Georgia.
I would rather tell you that now than have you assume otherwise.
Why this is the same argument as everything else I write about Because it is.
Know the number.
Say the number out loud.
Decide based on the number.
I am not a defense expert, and I am not going to pretend to be one.
There are people who have spent careers on this who know vastly more than I do about force structure and threat assessment.
What I know is inventory, controls, and what it looks like when an organization is buying things it cannot account for.
That skill is not specific to an industry.
And it is in short supply in the room where these decisions get made.
The world does not need to know what we have, what our capacity to consume and replace in production is.
But Congress needs to make sure we are fully ready at all times, have the capacity to replace (whether from consumption or obsolescence), and report the controls in place to limit spending to maintaining a set standard as determined by the Armed Forces and Congress together.
