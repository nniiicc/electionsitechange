Kevin Martin · Jobs and the Trades
Georgia Tech Figured This Out a Century Ago.
Study in the Morning, Do the Work in the Afternoon.
Kevin Martin, a Georgia Tech graduate, on why federal workforce dollars should be pushed toward programs where students learn and work at the same time.
I went to Georgia Tech.
I am not going to pretend that is a neutral fact when I tell you the next part.
Tech built a large part of its reputation on a simple idea.
Students studied in the morning and applied it in the afternoon and evening.
This foundation was the mirror of Worcester Polytechnic Institute — stressing combination of "Theory and Practice".
The program today is called CO-OP and is a fundamental strength of the School of Engineering consisting of - Mechanical, Chemical, Industrial, Electrical, and Civil.
They did not graduate and then start learning the job.
They graduated having already done the job.
The model is not a Georgia Tech invention, and it is not exotic.
It is how the trades have always worked, how medicine works, and how most of the world trains people for work that has consequences.
You learn the theory and you do the thing, at the same time, under someone who already knows how.
What is strange is that federal policy funds almost the opposite.
What federal money actually rewards
Follow the dollars and you find a system built around seat time.
Programs qualify based on how long they run and how they are structured, not on whether a graduate can do the work or whether anyone hired them.
The result is predictable to anyone who has run a budget.
When you fund inputs, you get inputs.
When you fund outcomes, you get outcomes.
We have spent decades funding enrollment and then acting surprised that enrollment is what we got.
Here is the part that should bother everybody regardless of politics.
An apprenticeship where somebody earns a wage while training costs the public less and produces a worker who is already employed.
It is cheaper and it works better.
It is also harder to fit into the federal boxes than a conventional classroom program.
What Congress actually controls
Congress funds the federal workforce and career-technical education programs and writes the rules for federally registered apprenticeships.
It decides what qualifies, what a sponsor has to document, and how the money flows.
Those rules determine whether a company that wants to train people finds it straightforward or finds it not worth the paperwork.
I have been on the compliance end of enough programs to tell you which way most employers go when the paperwork gets heavy.
They stop.
As of July 2026, US Department of Labor announced $162 Million in Cooperative agreements under the Pay for Performance Incentive Payments Program to increase registered apprenticeship in the following: Shipbuilding, defense industrial base, and emerging technology.
This goes with another $85 Million, in fourth round of State Apprenticeship Expansion Formula (SAEF) grants.
This is direct state-level funding based on performance metrics or apprentice growth and employer participation.
The goal is to reach more than 1 million active apprentices nationwide.
(US Department of Labor)
What needs to change
Push federal workforce dollars toward earn-while-you-learn programs.
If a program has people working and being paid while they train, that should be an advantage in the funding formula, not an inconvenience.
Make this a commitment to include retraining those displaced based on AI initiatives.
Make it easy for an employer to sponsor training.
The company that needs twenty technicians is the one best positioned to train twenty technicians.
Federal policy should make that simple.
Right now, it frequently makes it a project.
Stop changing the rules and requirements.
Establish the foundation first.
Fund on outcomes, not enrollment.
Completion and placement measured and published.
As of now, the data aggregates grantee reported data, but no breakdown of the levels or success of the programs.
The Workforce Pell rules got this part right, and the same standard should apply across workforce programs.
Stop treating this as the consolation prize.
A young person who becomes a lineman is not somebody who failed to go to college.
That framing has done real damage, and it is still buried in how these programs are described.
These should be celebrated as the FOUNDATION WORKFORCE OF AMERICA.
Why this is the one I keep coming back to
Because it is the shortest distance between a federal decision and somebody in this district making a better living.
Most of what Congress does takes years to show up in anybody's life.
This does not.
Change what qualifies, and within a couple of years there are people finishing programs and going to work who would not have been able to afford to start.
This will also eliminate the burden of outstanding student debt loans as these are paid employees working for companies and education for themselves.
And it is how you bring business back here.
Companies go where the skilled workforce is located.
They do not arrive first and hope.
Build the workforce and the jobs follow, and young people who grew up here will have a reason to stay.
We already know the model works.
It has been working ten miles from my home at Georgia Tech for well over a hundred years.
It has worked more recently for other programs like Chattahoochee Tech, and Gwinnett Technical College.
Next time you see a Georgia Power lineman or talk to an electrician at a major work site, or a certified plumber, or call the IBEW (International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers), ask them how the apprenticeship program worked for them.
