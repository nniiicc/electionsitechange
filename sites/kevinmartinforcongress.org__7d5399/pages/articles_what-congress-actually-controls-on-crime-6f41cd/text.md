Here Is the Part That Is.
Most policing in this district is a local decision, not a federal one.
Kevin Martin on the narrow part Congress actually controls, and why it should stop being an annual hostage.
I want to start by narrowing my own subject, because candidates talk about crime as if a member of Congress runs the sheriff's office, and that is not how any of this works.
Your police department is hired, funded, trained, and supervised locally.
Your sheriff answers to voters in this county.
Your district attorney is a state office.
The criminal code that covers nearly everything that happens here is Georgia law, passed in Atlanta.
Bail is set by judges operating under state rules.
A member of Congress does not touch most of that, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to sound tough.
If somebody running for federal office tells you they are going to fix crime in Cobb, Douglas, Fulton, or Fayette County, ask them which federal statute they plan to use.
What Congress does control is narrower.
It is also real.
The part Congress controls Money.
A significant share of what a local department spends on equipment, training, hiring, and technology moves through federal grant programs.
Congress decides how much and on what terms.
Federal criminal law.
Interstate trafficking, firearms offenses that cross state lines, organized fentanyl distribution, crimes on federal property.
The federal courts.
Congress confirms federal judges and writes the rules those courts operate under, including federal pretrial release.
The border and interdiction.
How much fentanyl arrives is not a Cobb County decision.
That is the honest list.
It is shorter than a campaign speech implies and it is not nothing.
Stop making it an annual hostage Here is what I actually want to say about the money.
Federal law enforcement grants get caught in the same budget process I have written about before.
They are packaged into large bills, held up as leverage, restored at the last minute, and occasionally used as a bargaining chip between people who have never been in a patrol car.
For a police chief, that means not knowing in March what the department will have in October.
You cannot hire on that.
Hiring an officer is a multi-year commitment — salary, academy, field training, equipment.
No competent manager makes a five-year commitment against a budget line that might vanish in a continuing resolution.
So, the practical effect is that departments hire too few officers and lean on overtime, which is more expensive per hour and burns people out.
I have seen the same pattern in business.
When funding is unpredictable, organizations substitute expensive short-term fixes for cheaper long-term ones, and the total cost goes up.
That is not a political observation.
It is a budgeting one.
There are two current federal law enforcement grant funding levels and recent appropriations.
They are Office of Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS) and the Office of Justice Programs (OJP).
Between the two, the budget is less than $20 Million to cover agency wide de-escalation training, train-the-trainer, and overtime costs, with partnership towards "best-practice" training nationwide.
And, for developing and distributing resources for advancement of crime fighting, common sense policing, and image improvement of law enforcement.
(From the US Department of Justice).
What needs to change Multi-year authorization for law enforcement grants.
If we want departments to hire and keep officers, the funding horizon has to match the commitment.
One-year money produces one-year decisions.
State and Local authorities need to commit to long term law enforcement policies and funding.
This funding should be supported with Federal Funding from the US Department of Justice and focused in areas of increasing criminal activity with young adults.
Stop using it as leverage.
Whatever is being negotiated, police funding should not be the thing held over the table.
That should be true regardless of which party is doing the holding.
We should never hold the protection of the citizens of the United States as a bargaining chip.
Fund the training, not just the equipment.
It is easier politically to buy a vehicle than to fund a training budget.
The vehicle is visible.
The training is what determines whether a hard encounter ends well.
Then look at the numbers.
Not "we support law enforcement." How many officers funded, how many still serving in year three, what happened to response times and clearance rates.
If the money did not move those, say so.
Why I am being narrow about this Because I would rather be trusted than applauded.
It would be easy to write a post that sounds angrier and promises more.
It would also be false, and the people reading it who actually work in law enforcement would know it was false by the third paragraph.
The people I have talked to in this line of work are not asking for speeches.
They are asking for a predictable budget, enough people, and to not be a political football twice a year.
Two of those three run through Congress.
That is the part of this I can actually do something about, so that is the part I am going to talk about.
Where Kevin stands on this issue Crime and Policing Fund law enforcement properly, fix the federal side of the pretrial release problem, and appoint judges who read the law as written rather than legislate from the bench.
