What the issue is Kevin's position on the Second Amendment is straightforward: a citizen with no criminal record and no history of threatening anyone shouldn't have to work through a pile of red tape to buy a firearm.
He isn't absolutist about it.
He supports keeping guns away from felons and from people who have shown they're a danger to others.
He understands why a firearm is traceable back to its original owner — if a stolen gun turns up at a crime scene, somebody has to be able to follow it back.
Where he draws a hard line is a federal list of who owns what.
"I do not like the idea of having a national list of gun ownership in the federal government.
Let it stay at the state level." — Kevin Martin His reasoning is historical, and it's the reason the amendment exists at all.
British troops came through the colonies, collected the firearms, and wrote down who had them — so they'd know which households were a threat to the crown.
The amendment was written by people who had watched exactly that happen.
What Congress actually controls Federal gun law is written by Congress.
Background check requirements, what a federally licensed dealer must do, whether a national registry exists — all of it is congressional.
There's a second piece most voters have never been told, and it matters here.
For most of American history, the Bill of Rights limited only the federal government.
A state could restrict speech, or searches, or firearms, and the federal Constitution had nothing to say about it.
What changed is that federal courts began applying those protections to state and local governments, one at a time, through the Fourteenth Amendment.
The Second Amendment was one of the last to get there.
Until McDonald v.
City of Chicago in 2010 — decided five to four — a city could restrict handguns and the federal Constitution offered no protection.
What needs to change Keep federal law focused on people who have actually done something.
Felons and people who have demonstrated they're a threat: keep them from buying firearms.
A citizen with a clean record and no history of instability shouldn't be treated as a suspect.
No national gun registry.
Traceability from the point of sale is one thing, and Kevin accepts it.
A federal roster of who owns what is a different thing, and he opposes it.
Be honest about red flag laws.
Kevin's concern is abuse — a process that can strip a constitutional right from someone who hasn't been convicted of anything is a process that will eventually be used badly.
Any such law has to clear a high bar for due process, and most of the proposals haven't.
Apply the Constitution consistently.
This is the part Kevin wants voters in this district to sit with, and it's aimed directly at the argument they've heard from their current representative.
The rights this district cares about most — speech, protest, protection from unreasonable searches — apply against your state and city government only because federal courts read the Fourteenth Amendment to make them apply.
That's the same interpretive path, and the same amendment, that finally extended the Second Amendment to state and local action in 2010.
You can't have it both ways.
If the method is legitimate when it protects the rights you like, it's legitimate when it protects the ones you don't.
Asking the courts to apply it selectively isn't principle — it's preference.
Why it matters This district has heard one argument about guns for a long time, delivered by someone with a personal tragedy behind it, and that argument deserves to be met with respect rather than dismissed.
But grief isn't a legal standard.
The question in front of Congress is whether the Constitution means the same thing on Tuesday that it meant on Monday — and whether a right gets less protection than the others because it's less popular in certain rooms.
Kevin's answer is that it doesn't work that way.
You either read the document consistently or you don't have a document, you have a preference with a robe on.
