Where Kevin Stands
An Arrest Doesn't Mean Much If the Courts Turn Them Loose
Fund law enforcement properly, fix the federal side of the pretrial release problem, and appoint judges who read the law as written rather than legislate from the bench.
What the issue is
Most people, when they think about crime, think about police.
Kevin thinks about the whole chain — because a system is only as good as its weakest link, and arresting somebody is only the first step.
An officer does the work, makes the arrest, files the case.
Then the person is back on the street within days.
Everybody in law enforcement has watched it happen.
Sometimes it ends the way you'd fear: someone released after a domestic violence arrest, and the next call to that address is a homicide.
That is not a police failure.
The officers did their job.
It broke somewhere after that.
"It's one thing we can do as much as possible in the policing, arresting, and prosecuting criminals.
But when we have judges that are out there saying, oh, well, we're gonna let this guy go out on bail, and wait till something severe happens."
— Kevin Martin
What Congress actually controls
Two things, and it's worth being precise about them, because most people running for Congress talk about crime as though the House can fix your county courthouse.
It can't.
The money.
Congress appropriates the federal grant funding that flows to state and local police departments — equipment, training, hiring, and the task forces that let local departments work with federal agencies.
When a department here can't afford what it needs, that's an appropriations decision made in Washington.
Federal criminal law.
Congress writes the federal criminal code, sets the sentencing framework, and sets the standards federal courts use to decide who is held and who is released while awaiting trial.
And the House holds the impeachment power over federal judges — the only direct check Congress has on a judge already on the bench.
What Congress does not control is the bail decision in a Georgia courtroom.
That's state law and a state judge.
Anyone who tells you a congressman will fix it is selling something.
What needs to change
Fund law enforcement properly, and stop treating it as a bargaining chip.
Departments in this district need equipment, training, and the people to do the work.
That funding shouldn't be hostage to whatever fight Washington is having that month.
Fix the federal side of the release problem.
Where federal law governs who gets released pending trial, the standard should account honestly for what happens when someone with a violent history goes back out.
That is a federal statute, and Congress can write it.
Appoint judges who interpret the law rather than write it.
Kevin's view is that a judge's job is to read the law as it was written and apply it — not to find room in it for the outcome they wanted.
"They're interpreting the law.
They're not setting law.
They're basically determining, based on the front face value of the law that's been presented in front of them.
And that's the way the judicial system should work."
— Kevin Martin
Follow the money on judicial appointments.
Kevin's position is that a judge who publicly aligns with a political party — either party — has told you they'll rule for a side rather than for the text.
He'd apply that test regardless of which party benefits.
Why it matters
Public safety isn't a slogan, it's a chain: patrol, arrest, prosecution, sentence.
Break any link and the whole thing stops working, and the people who pay for it are the ones who called for help.
Kevin has spent thirty years finding the link in a process where things go wrong.
It's the same discipline, applied to something with higher stakes than a payroll ledger.
