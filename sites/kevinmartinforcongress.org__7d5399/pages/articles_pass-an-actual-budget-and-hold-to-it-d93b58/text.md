Washington Would Not Survive an Audit of How It Passes a Budget.
Congress is supposed to pass twelve appropriations bills by October 1.
It almost never does.
Kevin Martin, a controller by trade, on why regular order is not a process complaint but the whole ballgame.
I have spent my career as a controller.
That means when the year ends, I am the one who has to say what the company actually spent, what it actually took in, and whether those two numbers can be reconciled.
If I told the owners "we will figure that out in a few months, just keep writing checks in the meantime," I would be looking for work.
That is roughly how the federal government does it.
What is supposed to happen There is an actual process, written in law.
Congress is supposed to pass a budget resolution, then pass twelve separate appropriations bills covering the twelve areas of government spending, and it is supposed to have all of that done before the fiscal year starts on October 1.
That is called regular order.
It exists so that each piece of spending gets looked at on its own, debated on its own, and voted on its own.
What actually happens Congress misses the deadline.
Then it passes a continuing resolution, which is a bill that says "keep spending at last year's levels for a few more weeks while we sort this out." Then it misses that deadline too.
Then somewhere around December, everything that did not get done gets bundled into one enormous bill, thousands of pages long, dropped a day or two before the vote, and passed under a deadline with a government shutdown as the alternative.
As I write this in September, not one of the twelve bills for the fiscal year that starts October 1 has been enacted, and Congress has already passed a continuing resolution that runs to December 11.
Last year none of the twelve was done on time.
The year opened with a 43-day shutdown, ran on stopgap resolutions through three funding lapses, and the last of the twelve bills was not signed until April 30.
Why this is not a process complaint I want to be careful here, because "Congress should follow regular order" sounds like the kind of thing people say when they do not have anything specific to offer.
Here is why it is specific.
When twelve bills become one bill, nobody can vote against any single thing.
A member who thinks one program is waste has to vote against military pay, food inspection, air traffic control, and veterans' care to register that objection.
So they do not register it.
The bad line item survives, not because anyone defended it, but because it was standing next to something nobody could vote against.
That is not an accident.
That is what the bundling is for.
And when the bill drops two days before the deadline, nobody has read it.
I mean that literally.
There is no person in that building who has read the whole thing before the vote.
If a controller told you he was signing off on a set of books he had not read, you would not accept the explanation that the deadline was tight.
What Congress actually controls All of it.
This is the one area where there is no one else to point at.
Congress writes the budget.
Congress sets its own calendar.
Congress decides whether to bundle.
Congress decides whether to allow amendments.
The rules that produce this outcome are rules Congress wrote for itself and can change tomorrow.
There is no court blocking it, no agency resisting it, no state government in the way.
When people say Washington is broken on spending, this is the specific mechanism, and it is entirely self-inflicted.
What needs to change Pass the twelve bills separately, and vote on them separately.
This is the whole reform.
If a program cannot survive its own vote in daylight, it should not survive.
No vote on a bill nobody has read.
A mandatory public availability window before any spending vote, measured in days, not hours.
This is ordinary practice in any organization that handles money seriously.
Deadlines with teeth.
If the appropriations bills are not done by October 1, the consequence should land on Congress, not on the country.
There are versions of this idea from both parties and they are worth taking seriously.
Then hold to it.
Passing a budget is not the same as sticking to one.
A budget you revise every time it becomes inconvenient is not a budget.
It is a press release.
Why this matters to you Because everything else in this campaign runs through it.
When people ask me about drug prices, or hospitals, or roads, or defense, the honest answer usually starts with how the money moves.
And the money moves through a process designed so that no single decision is ever anyone's fault.
I am not going to promise you I can fix the federal budget by myself.
One member of Congress cannot.
What I can tell you is that I know what a real set of books looks like, I know what it looks like when someone is hiding a number inside a bigger number, and I have spent thirty years being the person who says the uncomfortable thing about the arithmetic.
That is the job I am asking for.
