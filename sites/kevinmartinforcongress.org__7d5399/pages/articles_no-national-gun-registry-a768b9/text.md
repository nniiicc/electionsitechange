It Just Makes a List.
Kevin Martin on why he opposes a national firearms registry, stated as a practical argument about what it would and would not accomplish.
I oppose a national firearms registry.
I want to explain why in practical terms rather than starting with the slogan, because I think the practical argument is the stronger one.
Start with what it would do A registry is a federal database of who lawfully owns what.
The case for it is that if a firearm is recovered at a crime scene, you can trace it to an owner.
That is a real thing, and I am not going to pretend it is nonsense.
Here is the problem.
The firearms used in most violent crimes are not held by the person who lawfully bought them.
They were stolen or bought by somebody else on behalf of the person who could not pass a check or moved through a trafficking chain.
In those cases, the registry tells you who owned it before it was stolen.
You have identified a victim of a burglary.
So, the database that would be comprehensive about lawful owners would be close to blank on the population it was built to address.
That is not a philosophical objection.
It is a design problem.
The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms, and Explosives (ATF) maintains trace data, serial number linked records of firearms recovered in crimes, linked to purchase or transfer.
They publish this information state by state and international trace reports.
They use the data to identify three areas: Trafficking: guns moved from lawful commerce into illegal markets such as private sales without background checks; Straw Purchases: Individuals legally buying firearms for others who are prohibited from receiving and/or selling to black markets; and Stolen Firearms. (source - Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms, and Explosives - Firearms Trace Data Report) The cost side I look at things as a cost-benefit question, so here is the other column.
You would be building and maintaining a federal database of tens of millions of law-abiding people and what they own.
That database would need to be secured, and government databases have been breached before.
It would need to be kept accurate, and every registry of this kind drifts as things are sold, inherited, lost, or destroyed.
And there is the objection people in this district will raise first, which is that a list of who has what is the precondition for taking it.
I am not going to tell you that fear is irrational.
Whether or not anyone intends it, the list is what would make it possible, and people are entitled to notice that.
So, there is significant cost, permanent risk, and limited benefit against the actual problem.
In any other context that is an easy call.
What Congress actually controls Federal law currently prohibits a national registry, and dealer records are kept by the dealer rather than centrally.
Congress wrote that and Congress could change it.
Congress also funds ATF, sets what the background check system may retain and for how long, and decides the rules for trace requests.
What needs to change Keep the prohibition.
Plainly.
Put the effort where the firearms actually come from.
Trafficking and straw purchases are how prohibited people get armed.
Those are already federal crimes.
Prosecuting them reaches the actual pathway.
Make the background check system work on the records it already has.
When a disqualifying record never gets submitted, the check clears somebody it should have stopped.
That is not an argument for a new database.
It is an argument for the existing one being maintained properly.
This goes to my background involving AUDITS and asking the questions, and again, ask, WHY.
Make tracing work without central registration.
The current system lets investigators trace a specific firearm in a specific case.
Fund it and staff it.
That is targeted, and it does not require a list of everyone.
How I want to argue this I know this issue makes people angry on both sides, and I know that a lot of the argument is really about whether each side thinks the other is acting in good faith.
What I would ask is that any proposal — mine or anyone else's — has to answer the same question.
Which crimes would this have prevented, and how do we know?
I do not think a federal national registry passes that test.
If somebody can show me the evidence that it does, I will look at it, because that is how I would want anyone to treat a claim of mine.
