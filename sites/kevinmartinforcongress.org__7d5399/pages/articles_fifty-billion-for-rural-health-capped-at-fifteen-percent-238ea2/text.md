Only 15% of It Can Pay Providers for Care.
Congress put $50 billion into rural health.
CMS then capped payments to hospitals and other providers for care at 15% of each state's award.
Kevin Martin, a controller, on why most of the money cannot pay for the care it was supposed to save.
I want to walk through a piece of arithmetic, because this one genuinely bothers me as a numbers person, and because almost nobody has seen it laid out.
Start with where community hospitals are.
More than 40% of rural hospitals are operating at a loss.
Something like 417 are considered vulnerable to closure.
Since 2010, more than 200 have closed outright or converted to a model that no longer includes inpatient care.
More than 300 have stopped doing general surgery.
Nearly 450 have stopped offering chemotherapy.
That last one is worth sitting with.
Nearly four hundred and fifty rural hospitals where, if you get cancer, the treatment is now a drive.
The fix that does not reach the problem Congress responded with the Rural Health Transformation Program, which puts $50 billion into rural health care over five years, $10 billion a year from 2026 through 2030.
Half is split evenly among the states.
CMS divides the other half.
Every state got a first-year award in December 2025.
Georgia's was about $219 million.
That is a real number and I am not going to sneer at it.
Here is the part that does not work.
The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services set a rule that no more than 15 percent of a state's award in any year can go to payments to hospitals and other providers for care.
The law itself does not say that.
CMS wrote it into the program rules.
Read that again, because I had to.
The money is meant to address a crisis in which hospitals are closing, and at least 85 percent of it cannot go out as payments to providers for care.
It goes to other things, like technology, recruitment, planning, and coordination.
Some of that is useful, and some of it will reach hospitals.
None of it pays the electric bill at a facility running in the red.
Even the 15 percent cannot be used to raise what a hospital is paid for services it already bills.
Meanwhile, KFF projects, using Congressional Budget Office estimates, that the same law will cut federal Medicaid spending in rural areas by $137 billion over ten years.
So the arithmetic is a projected $137 billion hole against a $50 billion fund, of which at most $7.5 billion can go out as payments to providers for care.
I have closed a lot of books.
I know what it looks like when someone builds a number designed to be announced rather than to work.
The honest part about this district I should say plainly that GA-06 is not a rural district.
We are suburban and exurban Atlanta.
When you hear "rural hospital crisis," that is not literally us.
Two reasons it is still our problem.
First, the same federal payment rules that squeeze a low-volume hospital in south Georgia apply to community hospitals generally.
Second, when hospitals around the metro close or shed services, the patients do not disappear.
They arrive at the facilities that are still open.
We watched that happen here.
In May 2022, Wellstar closed the emergency room and hospital beds at its East Point campus.
That fall it closed Atlanta Medical Center downtown.
A year later, the state's own capacity dashboard was often listing Grady and Emory as severely or dangerously overcrowded.
A hospital closing across town shows up as your wait time.
What Congress actually controls Nearly all of it, because hospital finance is federal payment policy.
Congress sets Medicare payment rules and how they treat low-volume facilities.
Congress sets the Medicaid framework and the federal match.
Congress wrote the Rural Health Transformation Program.
The 15 percent cap came from CMS, not the law, and Congress can override it in law.
Congress decides what counts as a qualifying facility.
When a community hospital closes, the proximate cause is almost always that it could not cover its costs at the rates it was paid.
Those rates are a federal decision.
What needs to change Let the money reach the hospitals.
Lift or substantially raise the 15 percent cap.
A fund that can put only 15 percent toward paying for care is not much of a health care fund.
Pay what low-volume care actually costs.
A hospital serving a thin population cannot spread fixed costs the way a large urban system can.
That is arithmetic, not mismanagement.
Payment formulas built around volume will always strand these facilities, and the fix is a formula that reflects standby capacity as a real cost.
Count the ambulance ride.
Federal payment policy measures the cost of a service.
It does not measure the cost of that service not existing within fifty miles.
Until the distance to care is a number in the formula, closures will keep looking cheap on paper.
Then audit it.
Five years, $50 billion.
Somebody should have to report what it bought, by facility.
If the answer is consultants and dashboards, we should know that in year two, not year six.
Why it matters A hospital is not just a building where sick people go.
It is usually one of the largest employers in its area, it is why some doctors live nearby at all, and it is the reason an emergency is survivable.
Major Medicaid cuts in that same law take effect in 2027.
That is not a distant future.
That is next year.
I am not going to tell you I can reverse this by myself.
I will tell you that when the next version of this fund comes up for a vote, somebody in the room should be able to read the cap and say out loud what it means.
