An Arrest Does Not Mean Much If the Same Person Is Back Out That Afternoon
Kevin Martin on the federal side of pretrial release, where the data is missing, and why judges cannot make good decisions on incomplete records.
The complaint I hear most often about the justice system is not about police.
It is about what happens afterwards and sometimes even before a suspect sees a courtroom.
Somebody gets arrested.
The officers make the arrest, the paperwork gets filed, and the same person is back on the street quickly, sometimes before the officer has finished the shift.
Then it happens again.
I want to be precise about this one, because it is the easiest place in this whole campaign to say something that sounds strong and is not true.
Most of this is not federal
Bail and pretrial release for the crimes that affect daily life here are governed by Georgia law and decided by state judges.
If you are frustrated about a release decision in the counties of the 6th District (Cobb, Douglas, Fayette, and Fulton), the people who set those rules work in Atlanta, not Washington.
A member of Congress cannot change that.
I would rather tell you plainly than let you assume I can.
The federal part that is real
Federal pretrial release applies in federal cases, and Congress writes those rules.
Federal cases are a small share of all prosecutions, but they are concentrated in exactly the categories people worry about most — human trafficking, firearms offenses crossing state lines, organized distribution of illegal items, and mail fraud.
There is a second federal role that matters more than people realize, and it is about information.
A judge making a release decision is making a prediction with whatever record is in front of them.
If that record is incomplete — if an arrest in another state never made it into the system, if a disposition was never entered, if a protective order was never transmitted — then the judge is deciding on a partial picture and everyone downstream lives with the result.
The systems that share criminal history across state lines are federal.
So are the standards and the funding for keeping them current.
That is a genuinely federal job, and it is unglamorous enough that it rarely gets attention.
Multiple federal reviews such as GAO-15-162 document that many criminal history records lack final disposition data.
Progress is being made, but there is still a significant gap.
(source - US Government Accountability Office and Bureau of Justice Statistics).
What needs to change
Make the records complete.
Fund and require the reporting so that a judge in one state can see what happened in another.
This is the highest-return, lowest-controversy item in the whole justice conversation and almost nobody campaigns on it because it is boring.
This will eliminate a significant amount of "second guessing" after the fact of habitual criminals.
Fix the federal pretrial rules where the offense is violent or the person is already prohibited.
That is a narrow category, and it is where the federal system's own decisions matter most.
Measure what happens after release.
Not anecdotes.
Rearrest rates, failure to appear, by offense category, published.
Right now, most of this argument is conducted with stories because the numbers are not there.
Stop pretending the federal government can fix state bail.
It cannot.
Anyone promising it is either confused or counting on you being.
Why I am handling it this way
Because this is the issue where the gap between what a candidate can promise and what a member of Congress can deliver is widest.
A US Congressional member can only help with Federal related crimes.
It is up to state lawmakers to handle state issues of crime.
But still the same question to ask again, Why.
Why is US Congress not putting in place policies and procedures related to Federal Courts to protect US Citizens from habitual criminal activities?
I could write something angrier.
It would probably perform better.
It would also be the kind of thing that, if I got elected, I would have to quietly stop saying once somebody explained the actual jurisdiction to me.
I would rather start where I intend to finish.
The federal part is the records, the federal cases, and the measurement.
That part I will work on.
The rest of it belongs to people you elect in Georgia, and I will say so on a stage if I am asked.
