We Learned That Once Already.
Kevin Martin on why loosening lending standards to solve affordability repeats the 2000s, and what honest underwriting actually protects.
When housing gets unaffordable there is always a proposal to fix it by making the loan easier to get.
Lower the down payment.
Stretch the term.
Relax the income documentation.
Get people to the closing table.
I understand why it is appealing.
It works immediately, which is more than you can say for building supply.
We ran that experiment in the 2000s.
I watched it, and so did most people reading this.
What actually happened People were approved for loans they could not carry.
A lot of those loans had rates that started low and adjusted later, and the household budget worked at the starting rate and did not work at the adjusted one.
When rates moved, people lost their homes.
Federal underwriting rules are what permitted that.
Not a conspiracy — a set of decisions, made for understandable reasons, about what a lender was allowed to offer and what documentation was required.
Here is the part that gets missed.
Easier credit did not make housing cheaper.
It made it more expensive.
When more people can bid on the same number of houses, prices go up.
The buyer got a bigger loan and the same house at a higher price, and took on more risk to do it.
That is not a moral argument.
It is arithmetic.
Demand-side help with a fixed supply becomes a price increase.
This is economics 101.
Where that leaves the honest position I said in an earlier post that supply is the real constraint here and that federal infrastructure capacity is the part of it Washington actually touches.
This is the other half.
If you loosen lending without adding supply, then you're creating a higher demand for limited supply and you get higher prices and more people in loans they cannot carry.
You get the worst version of both.
So I am not going to campaign on making mortgages easier to get.
I know that is a less satisfying thing to say than promising to get people into homes.
What I do think is worth looking at There is a difference between loosening standards and looking at structure.
If a thirty-year note at current rates does not put a house within reach of a young family, the question of whether other structures could work deserves an actual hearing rather than a shrug.
That is not the same as approving people who cannot pay.
It is asking whether the standard product is the right product.
I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the territory where the 2000s went wrong.
The test is simple.
Does the borrower still have to demonstrate they can carry it, under realistic assumptions, including if rates move?
If yes, it is worth examining.
If the innovation is that the borrower no longer has to prove they can pay, we have seen that movie.
The rinse and repeat of failure.
From consumerfinance.gov , the primary federal underwriting rule for most consumer mortgages is the Ability-to-Repay/Qualified Mortgage Rule under Regulation Z, implemented by CFPB.
The general rule for QMR requires creditors make good-faith reasonable decision on consumers' ability to repay and prohibits certain loan features.
In 2020, the general QM Final Rule replaced the 43% debt-to-income cap with price-based thresholds.
What needs to change Hold the line on ability to repay.
Whatever else changes, the borrower demonstrating they can carry the loan under realistic assumptions is the thing that protects them.
This includes verifying items such as source of income to repay loan and other assets owned by purchaser for support of collateral against loan.
Be suspicious of demand-side fixes.
Anything that lets more people bid on the same houses raises prices.
If it comes without supply, it is a price increase with extra steps.
Again, ask the question, WHY.
Why are more people bidding higher for the same home?
Is it supply shortage or are there other factors involved such as local schools, safe communities, easy access to amenities and local businesses?
Look at structure, carefully, in daylight.
With the repayment test intact.
Teach people how credit works.
A lot of what keeps young buyers out is not income.
It is that nobody explained how to build a credit profile, and by the time they find out they are three years behind.
Why I am willing to be the one saying no here Because the person who gets hurt by a loan they cannot carry is not the lender or the policymaker.
It is the family that loses the house.
I would rather be the candidate who tells you the easy version does not work than the one who promises it and lets you find out in year four when the rate adjusts.
The biggest gains will be made by increasing the supply of homes and this can only happen when government rules and red tape are reduced to make it where builders can build quality homes without the delays and extra costs.
Where Kevin stands on this issue Housing Open unused federal land for people to build on, give mortgage terms a real hearing, and keep lending standards honest so we do not repeat the 2000s.
