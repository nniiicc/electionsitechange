Kevin Martin · Schools
Two Schools, Fifteen Minutes Apart, Different Outcomes.
Washington Cannot Fix That From Washington.
Kevin Martin on the achievement gap inside this district, why federal policy has not closed it, and what raising the bottom without lowering the top would actually take.
You can drive fifteen minutes in this district and go from a school that competes with anything in Georgia to one that is struggling badly.
Same county.
Same state funding formula.
Same federal programs.
Same decade.
That is the fact I keep coming back to, because it rules out most of the easy explanations.
If federal policy were the deciding factor, both schools would be in the same place.
They get the same federal policy.
Whatever is producing the gap is closer to home than Washington.
What I am not going to claim
I am not going to tell you a member of Congress can fix a specific school in this district.
That is not the job of a congressman or senator in Washington DC, and anyone who says otherwise is running for the wrong office.
Curriculum is a state and local decision.
Hiring is local.
Discipline policy is local.
School construction is local.
The superintendent does not work for Congress and should not.
So, what am I actually offering?
Two things.
Get the federal weight off the districts that are trying to solve this and be honest about what the gap is.
The thing nobody says out loud
When a district has one group of schools performing well and another struggling, there is a strong pull toward solutions that narrow the gap by holding back the top.
It is rarely stated that way.
It shows up as pressure to standardize, to move resources uniformly, to eliminate programs that look like advantages.
I think that is the wrong instinct and I would say so.
The goal is to raise the bottom.
A parent whose child is in a strong school should not have to feel that improving another school comes at their child's expense, because that is how you lose the political coalition for doing anything at all.
Raising the bottom is harder and costs more than leveling.
It is also the only version worth doing and the return on investment is greater than reducing standards rate of return.
Reducing standards or removing resources at well performing schools is a diminishing return on investment.
What Congress actually controls
The money and the conditions attached to it.
Roughly — and this is worth knowing — federal dollars are a modest share of what a district spends, while carrying a disproportionate share of the reporting burden.
The National Center for Education Statistics shows public schools in Georgia receive 12.2% of their TOTAL K-12 funding from federal sources during the 2023-2024 school year.
This amounts to $2,253 per student in federal funds.
The US Average is $2,460.
Georgia's total funding is estimated at about $3.94 billion.
The main areas covered are Title I programs ($594 Million), Child Nutrition Act ($683 Million) and Children with Disabilities ($336 Million) or roughly 45% of total.
(source: Georgia Budget and Policy Institute) Information related to the GA-06 achievement gap tables can be requested from GaDOE Data Request Form or GOEWS Data Request Form.
Congress also decides whether money arrives as a block grant the district can direct or as categorical funding it must spend a prescribed way.
That distinction matters enormously to a superintendent trying to fix one specific building.
What needs to change
Send it as a block grant and hold them to outcomes.
The people who know which building needs what are in the building.
Report results, not process.
Cut the reporting to match the dollars.
If federal money is a small share of the budget, it should not consume most of the compliance staff's year.
Fund what closes gaps, and check whether it did.
Tutoring, early literacy, teacher retention in hard assignments.
Some of this works and some does not, and we should be willing to find out which.
You will find in most of these cases, the one question that is least asked, WHY.
Why did it work and why did it not work.
Do not solve it by leveling down.
Say it plainly, in advance, so nobody has to guess.
Why this one is personal
I have lived in this district a long time and I have watched it change.
The decline I talk about is not abstract to me and neither is this part of it.
A kid's odds should not be set by which side of a county line they were born on.
Right now, to a real degree, they are, and we need to break through those lines so that ALL children have the same resources and odds of success.
I cannot fix that from Washington.
What I can do is stop Washington from making it harder, send the money home with fewer strings, and be honest about what the numbers say when we check.
