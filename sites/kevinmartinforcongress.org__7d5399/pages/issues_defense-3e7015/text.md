Where Kevin Stands
Know What We Have Before We Write the Check
Start with the inventory, not the check.
Set a real readiness benchmark, require replenishment when we fall below it, and audit defense like every other line.
What the issue is
Ask most candidates about defense and you get a number — spend more, or spend less.
Kevin thinks that's the wrong question, and it's the wrong question for a reason he's spent thirty years learning.
You don't start with the check.
You start with the inventory.
What do we actually have?
Missiles, drones, munitions, ships, the equipment that would matter if it were needed tomorrow.
What's the minimum we need to defend the country — the continental United States, Alaska, Hawaii, and our territories?
Where are we against that number right now?
Almost nobody in Washington can answer that, which is remarkable given what we spend.
What Congress actually controls
Every dollar.
Defense is discretionary spending appropriated by Congress each year, and it is one of the largest single things Congress does.
Congress also sets what the Pentagon must report and how the money can be moved once appropriated.
If Congress wanted a hard accounting of readiness and inventory before writing the check, Congress could require one.
It hasn't.
What needs to change
Set a real benchmark.
Establish the minimum level of supply and readiness required to defend the country, and write it down as a number rather than a feeling.
Put a replenishment requirement in the budget.
If we fall below that benchmark — through a conflict, through consumption, through age — funding to get back to it should be automatic, not a fight every year.
"If we drop below it, then there should be a provision in the budget that states we must elevate back up to that benchmark that's been established."
— Kevin Martin
Set the number with the people who know, then check their work.
Military leadership should establish what's actually needed, because they know the answer and civilians in Congress don't.
But Kevin has been on the other side of enough budget requests to know what happens next.
"You give people an open-ended number to bring forth, they're gonna shoot for the moon."
— Kevin Martin
That's where Congress comes in — not to second-guess the military on what it needs, but to hold the line between a requirement and a wish list.
No open checkbook.
Defense gets funded first, and it gets audited like everything else.
Those two things are not in conflict, and Kevin doesn't accept that supporting the military means never asking where the money went.
Why it matters
This is what a controller does.
Before you approve a purchase, you find out what's already on the shelf, what the minimum has to be, and what it costs to hold that line.
Then you fund it and you verify it.
The country currently does none of that with the largest discretionary line in the budget.
We fund defense by momentum and argument rather than by inventory, and nobody can say with confidence whether we're above the line or below it.
Supporting the military and auditing the spending are the same job.
Anyone telling you otherwise is protecting a contract, not a country.
