The Only Agency of Twenty-Four That Has Never Passed.
Kevin Martin on the Pentagon's eighth consecutive failed audit, what a failed audit does and does not mean, and why supporting the military means insisting it can account for the money.
The Department of Defense has now failed its financial audit eight times in a row.
Every single one since Congress started requiring them in 2018.
Of the government's twenty-four major agencies, it is the only one that has never passed.
The most recent audit found twenty-six material weaknesses.
I have spent my career around audits, so let me tell you what that phrase means and what it does not.
What a failed audit actually means It does not mean money was stolen.
You will hear people say trillions went missing.
That is not what a disclaimed opinion is.
It means the auditors could not obtain enough reliable evidence to form an opinion on the financial statements.
They could not verify the numbers well enough to say whether they are right.
That sounds more boring than theft.
In some ways it is worse.
Theft is a discrete event you can find, quantify, and prosecute.
What a disclaimed opinion says is that the organization does not have reliable enough records to know.
Not that the money went somewhere bad.
That we cannot say where it went.
One of the findings involved the F-35 program, where assets in the global spares pool were not properly reported.
That is not a rounding error on a small program.
It is the department's largest.
The part that should bother people who love the military I want to be careful here, because this argument gets used by people who simply want to spend less on defense, and that is not where I am coming from.
If you believe a strong military is essential — and I do — then you should be the angriest person in the room about this.
Here is why.
Every dollar that cannot be accounted for is a dollar that might not be buying readiness.
If we cannot verify what we have, we cannot know what we need.
The people arguing for a larger defense budget are undermined more by eight failed audits than by any critic, because the first question any serious person asks is: more money for an organization that cannot tell you where the last of it went?
Accountability is not the opposite of supporting the military.
It is a precondition for making the case.
The 2028 promise The department says it will pass by 2028.
I would like to believe that.
I would also point out that it has been failing for eight years while stating an intention to fix it, and that year eleven of a plan is not a plan.
It is a horizon that keeps receding.
In any organization I have worked in, a controller who said "we will have reliable books in three more years, for the fourth time" would be having a different conversation.
A sample of information outlining the detail of the 2024 audit can be found in the publication of GAO-25-107427 Q&A Report to Congressional Committees September 16, 2025.
This report outlines some of the steps that are in place to address the 28 areas of concern by 2028.
It is my opinion that something of this magnitude should not be sitting unaddressed by any member of Congress and resolution to the issues should be addressed immediately.
In my years of experience, no company would be allowed by any financial lending institution to push remediation extensions beyond one year.
This would violate 100% of all covenant agreements between companies and the fiduciary responsibilities of the lending institutions.
Why should we not hold our federal government and Department of Defense to the same accountability?
Because, by all means, this is funding from the United States of America Citizens for the purpose of funding the defense of our nation.
What Congress actually controls Congress required the audit.
That is the proof that congressional insistence produces measurement.
Congress can attach consequences to the result.
It can require the department to report against a remediation schedule and appear when it slips.
It can condition portions of funding on progress against specific material weaknesses.
It can fund the financial systems modernization that a lot of this actually depends on, because some of the problem is genuinely that the underlying systems cannot produce the data.
What needs to change Consequences attached to the schedule.
Not the whole budget — that would be reckless and would land on service members.
But a portion of administrative funding tied to closing specific weaknesses is ordinary management.
Fix the systems, not just the reporting.
You cannot audit your way out of a records problem.
Some of this requires actual investment in the accounting infrastructure, and that is a legitimate thing to fund.
No open checkbook.
Set the number with the people who know what readiness requires, then check their work.
Both halves.
The checking is not disrespect.
It is the job.
Report it publicly, every year, in plain language.
Not a document nobody reads.
A short public statement of whether it passed and what is still broken.
Why I keep coming back to this Because it is the clearest example of the thing I have been saying since I started my campaign and run for US Congress.
Ask the questions and start with, WHY.
Washington is not mostly a den of thieves.
It is an organization with no controls, no consequences, and nobody whose job it is to say the uncomfortable thing about the arithmetic.
I have been that person for thirty years.
It does not make you popular in the room.
It does make the numbers real.
And it makes people accountable.
Identifying failures in controls only helps our defense to be stronger and resolute in knowing what we have in order to defend the citizens of the United States from our adversaries.
