The people who still work and still own homes in Ballard, Magnolia, Queen Anne, Fremont, and Greenwood are paying more and living less.

They are the ones who used to take their family out to eat once a month and no longer can without thinking twice.
A neighborhood place isn’t a luxury.
It’s where ordinary life feels like sitting at a friend’s table — and too many of those rooms are going dark while Seattle gets colder and more impersonal.
They are the ones who drive to Bellevue or Redmond for work and watch half a paycheck disappear into gas taxes and carbon fees every week.
They are the ones who still mow their own lawns, still pay their own bills, and still expect government to live within its means the way they do.
Seattle and Olympia have treated these costs as the price of “progress.” Working people experience them as a slow reduction in the quality of ordinary life.
This is not that complicated.

When restaurants and small businesses feel the same squeeze, the neighborhood loses the places people used to go.
