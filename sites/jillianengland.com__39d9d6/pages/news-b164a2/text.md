Subsidies create a debt treadmill.
Most large wind projects only work because of tax credits, mandates, and forced power-purchase agreements.
Those lock in above-market prices for decades.
When turbines age or underperform, new subsidies are required just to keep servicing the old debt.
Each tranche partially pays for the last one.
Materials and disposal compound the cost.
Turbines demand massive steel, concrete, fiberglass, and rare-earth magnets.
Blades are largely non-recyclable and pile up in landfills.
The deferred cleanup is real and rarely counted in the rosy cost numbers.
The biological damage is permanent.
Direct kills of birds and bats are under-counted.
More serious is the loss of their offspring and the disruption of local food webs—insects, predators, and everything downstream.
That information is gone.
You cannot offset it with credits or trees elsewhere.
Entropy does not negotiate.
Dense sources (nuclear, gas, hydro) concentrate waste into small, manageable streams.
Diffuse sources spread disorder across land, sky, and supply chains, then require endless political transfers to stay solvent.
The subsidy treadmill and irreversible ecological losses scale with capacity.
They are features of the current build-out, not bugs.

They do not explain why it became unsustainable.
Seattle has been trying to run two different theories of public safety at the same time.
One theory treats public safety as the capacity of government to establish and maintain order: visible presence, focused deterrence, rapid response, thorough investigation, and the removal of violent offenders from the streets.
The other places primary emphasis on alternative response programs, diversion, behavioral-health interventions, and the deliberate reduction of traditional enforcement wherever possible.
Both approaches have legitimate uses.
The problem is not that they exist.

Ordinary days can conceal that conflict.
A mass-casualty event cannot.
When gunfire erupts at a major public festival, the public expects immediate command, accurate information, decisive action, and visible control.
If the city’s operating philosophy remains divided about the actual role of policing, those divisions surface as hesitation, mixed messaging, and eroded public confidence.
That is exactly what happened.

A city can integrate police, fire, paramedics, behavioral-health teams, and social services into a coherent system.
Integration, however, requires agreement on doctrine.
