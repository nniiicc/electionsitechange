What We're Hearing… Taxes, Rising Costs, and Kindness Taxes It's tax season, and fairness matters.
Property values have jumped fast, especially here in Lewis and Clark County.
When values rise faster than incomes, people get priced out of homes they already own.
That is not a "free market" outcome.
That is a policy failure.
Relief should match what people can actually afford.
If your income stays flat but your tax bill spikes, the system should automatically step in.
That's how you protect seniors on fixed incomes, working families, and people who've lived here for decades.
Renters deserve relief, too.
Renters pay property taxes indirectly through their rent.
Yet very few landlords applied for relief this cycle (see Helena IR / MT Free Press ).
If we want to keep Helena livable, we cannot pretend renters aren't carrying the burden. 💡 Did your rent go up because of property taxes?
You can check whether your landlord applied for relief at revenue.mt.gov using the property geocode from Montana Cadastral .

Income tax: simple isn’t always fair.
On income tax, I do not support a flat 5% rate.
It sounds simple, but it pushes Montana in the wrong direction.
A teacher and a millionaire are not the same.
Working families spend most of their income necessities such as groceries, car repairs, school supplies, and small businesses right here in Helena.
For the highest earners?
That money is the least likely to come back to our community.
No ripple, no trickle, just POOF!
And when the state cuts revenue without a plan?
The bill shows up somewhere else, usually in higher property taxes or reduced services.
Working with Senate Democrats in 2025 reinforced something I believe strongly: fair income tax policy isn't about slogans.
It's about choices, trade-offs, and clear priorities.
Here are the four questions I use when evaluating tax policy: Who does the relief really target?
Working families and full-time Montana residents should benefit most.
Where does the savings go next?
When working families keep more, it goes back into the local economy, such as groceries, car repairs, small businesses right here in Helena.
Is it fiscally responsible?
A tax cut that creates a deficit isn't relief.
It's a delayed bill.
Is it simple enough to actually use?
If it's too complicated, the benefit goes to people who can afford accountants.
That's the direction I'll keep pushing: a tax system that's fair, sustainable, and focused on keeping Helena home.
Housing A personal reality.
On April 2, Rob and I celebrated 14 years of owning our home in SD41.
I'm honestly not sure we could afford that same house today.
A new analysis puts Montana near the bottom nationally for how long it takes to save for a down payment .
The average age of first-time homeownership is nearing 40.
That is not inevitable.
It’s the result of policy choices.
We need to: Build more housing working people can afford Bring financing tools to projects that are stuck Fix property taxes so rising values don’t force people out If you have a housing story you're willing to share, reply and tell me what you're seeing.
I really want to know.

What We’re Cooking… Spring calls for something light and fresh, the kind of dish you can prep ahead for Easter brunch or bring to a picnic.
This recipe fits tax month in a funny way.
Wild rice has been called "pocket money." Early traders used that term because true wild rice swells to about four times its size.
A small handful could feed a lot of hungry people.
One of my favorite chefs, Sean Sherman, talks about this in The Sioux Chef cookbook.
I'm still trying to dial in the taste of my favorite version.
The version that showed up at gatherings in the Many Nations Longhouse during my law school days.
I remember living off those leftovers for weeks.
Just like those early traders: a little went a long way.
