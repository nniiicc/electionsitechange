This bill focuses on one simple idea: When someone calls 911, it should work — every time, everywhere, no matter what.
It sets national standards to make 911 more reliable, strengthens rural emergency services, requires real backup systems, and makes sure the public is notified when 911 isn’t working.
It also adds strong enforcement so these rules actually matter.
Across the country — especially in rural areas — people experience:
- 911 outages during storms
- Dead zones where mobile 911 doesn’t work
- Dispatch centers that close or go understaffed after 5 PM
- EMS units that aren’t available overnight
- Carriers failing to report outages
- Slow or inaccurate location information
- No backup power at cell towers
- No accountability when failures happen
This bill fixes those problems by creating national minimum standards for reliability, staffing, and transparency.
- Uptime: 911 systems must be working 99.999% of the time.
- Redundant routing: 911 calls must have two separate network paths so one fiber cut can’t take down a whole county.
- Backup power: Cell towers and routing centers must have 8–24 hours of backup power.
This prevents outages caused by storms, equipment failures, or power loss.
The bill creates 911 Priority Zones — areas with poor 911 access — and requires carriers to:
- Map dead zones
- File plans to fix them
- Meet deadlines
- Prove they’ve improved coverage
Carriers that don’t comply lose access to federal telecom funds.
The bill funds upgrades for:
- Next‑Generation 911 (NG911)
- Better location accuracy
- Text‑to‑911
- GIS mapping
- CAD systems
- Cybersecurity protections
This helps dispatchers find callers faster and respond more effectively.
The bill directly addresses the real‑world problem of: “I’m sorry, no one is available after 5 PM — no one is coming.”
- 24/7 on‑site staffing at every dispatch center
- At least one fully staffed EMS unit on duty at all times
- Response time standards for urban and rural areas
- Mutual‑aid agreements between counties
Rural counties can apply for federal staffing grants to help meet these requirements.
If 911 isn’t working, carriers must notify:
- The public
- Local emergency officials
- The state
- The federal oversight office
Notifications must include:
- What’s wrong
- Who is affected
- Alternative emergency numbers
- Updates every 15 minutes
This prevents the public from being left in the dark during outages.
This dashboard shows:
- All 911 outages
- Staffing failures
- EMS coverage gaps
- Carrier violations
- County reports
- Corrective actions
It gives the public and local officials full transparency.
The bill includes strong penalties:
- Up to $1,000,000 for failures that cause injury or death
- Up to $500,000 for routing failures
- Up to $250,000 for backup‑power failures
- Up to $50,000 per hour for unreported outages
- Up to $50,000 for false reports
- Up to 1 year in jail (misdemeanor)
- Up to 5 years in jail (felony if someone is hurt or killed)
- Carriers and counties can lose federal funding if they fail to comply.
The Federal Emergency Communications Oversight Office (FECOO) will:
- Audit carriers and PSAPs
- Issue subpoenas
- Conduct surprise inspections
- Enforce penalties
- Run the 911 dashboard
- Administer grants
This ensures the rules are actually followed.
Funding comes from:
- Universal Service Fund (USF)
- Rural Digital Opportunity Fund (RDOF)
- BEAD program
- Penalties collected under the bill
No new taxes or fees are created.
- Immediate: Penalties and outage reporting
- 1 year: Staffing and EMS requirements
- 2 years: Uptime, redundancy, backup power, and NG911 upgrades
Extensions are available for rural and tribal communities.
- Rural communities: fewer dead zones, better EMS coverage
- Suburban and urban areas: better location accuracy and faster response
- First responders: better tools and more reliable systems
- Families: peace of mind that 911 will work
- Local governments: access to federal support and clear standards
This bill is designed to make sure that when someone dials 911 — in a city, a small town, or a rural back road — the call goes through, help is available, and the system is strong enough to keep working when it matters most.
