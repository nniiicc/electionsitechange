Green Party Candidate Pete Karas Unveils the BADASS Bill to Rewrite Wisconsin's Election Rules
The Ballot Access, Democracy, and Suffrage Statute would widen ballot access, pull big money out of campaigns, open up voting machines, and give Wisconsin a proportional state Senate.
FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE
September 3, 2026

MUSKEGO, Wis., - Pete Karas, the Wisconsin Green Party candidate for Secretary of State, today released the Ballot Access, Democracy, and Suffrage Statute, or BADASS, a piece of model legislation he wrote to open up the state's elections.
The bill rewrites the rules candidates run under, and voters vote under.
It cuts the signatures needed to reach a statewide ballot to 1,200.
It bans PAC, corporate, and union money from candidate campaigns and matches small donations six-to-one through a public fund.
It makes the source code inside voting machines public and orders a hand-counted audit after every general election.
It registers eligible voters automatically at the DMV and keeps the vote for people with felony convictions, in or out of prison.
And it turns the 33 single-member state Senate districts into 11 three-member districts elected proportionally, with maps drawn by nonpartisan staff.
“Both parties like the rules exactly how they are,” Karas said.
“Hard to get on the ballot, easy to raise big money, and district maps that pick the winners before anybody shows up to vote.
I wrote a bill that takes all of that apart.”
Karas is quick to point out that the office he's running for can't pass any of it.
The Secretary of State has almost no policymaking power, widely considered the weakest such office in the country.
He treats that as a license to run on big ideas.
“Nobody has to worry about wasting a vote on me,” Karas said.
“The office can't spoil anything, and it can't wreck anything.
A vote for me is a vote to put these reforms on the table and show how many people in this state actually want them.”
The full text of the bill and a plain-language summary are posted at PeteKaras.com.
Karas said he plans to talk it through with voters, county clerks, and reform groups across the state through the fall.
