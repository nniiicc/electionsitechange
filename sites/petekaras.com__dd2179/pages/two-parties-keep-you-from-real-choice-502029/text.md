Ballot Access: A Gate Kept by the Duopoly
Want to run for office outside the two-party system?
Good luck.
In many states, including Wisconsin, the hurdles to simply get on the ballot are steep.
Independent and third-party candidates are often required to collect thousands more signatures than their major-party counterparts, meet tighter deadlines, and jump through more legal hoops.
These requirements aren’t about maintaining fairness—they’re about preserving power.
They keep alternative voices off the ballot and voters stuck with the same limited choices.
This isn’t democracy.
It’s gatekeeping.
Debate Exclusion: Silencing Independent Voices
Even when independent candidates overcome ballot access restrictions, they often face another obstacle: debate exclusion.
Major debates are often organized by partisan commissions or media outlets with vested interests.
The rules are written to favor establishment candidates.
If a candidate like Pete Karas meets the eligibility threshold to appear on the ballot, shouldn’t he also be allowed to debate?
Voters deserve to hear from all qualified candidates.
But the two-party system knows that when voters hear real alternatives, they just might stop settling.
Media Coverage: Manufactured Visibility
Mainstream media plays a significant role in sustaining this rigged system.
Coverage focuses overwhelmingly on Democratic and Republican candidates, while independents and third-party voices are sidelined or dismissed.
This lack of visibility translates into fewer donations, fewer volunteers, and fewer votes—not because the candidates are unqualified, but because they’re ignored.
Media narratives shape voter perception.
When outlets fail to report on independent campaigns like Pete Karas’s, they’re not just showing bias—they’re reinforcing a rigged political structure that thrives on exclusion.
Follow the Money: Corporate Influence Protects the Status Quo
The duopoly isn’t just protected by laws and media—it’s funded by corporations and lobbyists who benefit from the status quo.
Both parties rely on massive campaign contributions, which come with strings attached.
This money influences who gets attention, who gets resources, and ultimately, who gets power.
What This Means for Wisconsin Voters
In Wisconsin, voters are often left choosing between two candidates who represent different wings of the same system.
The barriers to entry for outsiders—like ballot access, debate inclusion, and media fairness—are so high that the idea of real competition is often an illusion.
This isn’t just bad for independents.
It’s bad for democracy.
Without competition, accountability erodes.
Without choice, voter turnout drops.
And without new voices, our government stagnates.
