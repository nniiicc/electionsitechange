That's Exactly Why You Can't Have Both.
Fusion voting and ranked-choice voting solve the same problem.
That's the whole issue.
Both exist because minor parties face a trap: run your own candidate and you risk splitting the vote, handing the election to whoever your voters like least.
Both systems try to spring that trap.
They just go in opposite directions.
Two Different Answers to the Spoiler Problem Fusion says: hand your ballot line to the Democrat or Republican your voters prefer.
The minor party gives up its own candidate but gets to claim partial credit for the win and, in theory, extract a few policy commitments in return.
Ranked-choice says: run your own candidate.
If your candidate gets knocked out, your voters' second choices count.
Nobody becomes a spoiler, and no vote is wasted.
Those are genuinely incompatible ideas.
Fusion asks minor parties to fold into the two-party race.
Ranked-choice asks them to compete independently of it.
Combine them on one ballot and you get overlapping candidate lines, unclear ranking rules for duplicate entries, and a system that would be difficult for clerks to administer and voters to understand at the booth.
Why I Filed an Amicus Brief That's why I filed an amicus brief this week in United Wisconsin v.
Wisconsin Elections Commission in Dane County Circuit Court, siding with the Wisconsin Elections Commission's motion for summary judgment.
The brief was joined by the Wisconsin Green Party and three fellow Green candidates.
I'm not against fusion voting as an idea.
Some states have used it, and it has produced real results in places like New York, where the Working Families Party has used it to extract genuine policy commitments from Democrats it might otherwise have no leverage over.
I understand the logic.
What I'm against is a court ordering it.
Because a court-ordered mandate would not just settle this lawsuit.
It could write fusion voting into the constitutional architecture of Wisconsin elections.
And when something gets written into the constitutional architecture of Wisconsin elections, it outranks any law the Legislature might pass later.
Ranked-choice voting bills are already in the hopper in Madison.
A court-ordered fusion mandate doesn't leave them alone.
It would put a ceiling on what they could do.
Future legislators can want ranked-choice all they like.
A constitutional mandate built around fusion could stand in their way.
That's too big a call for one judge to make.
It belongs to voters and their elected representatives.
Election Reform Should Belong to Voters My platform calls for ranked-choice voting, proportional representation for the Legislature, public financing through democracy vouchers, independent redistricting, and lower ballot-access thresholds for minor parties.
Those are structural reforms that change who can run and who can win.
You earn them through a legislature and through voters.
You do not get them handed to you by a lawsuit, and you do not get them taken away that way either.
I want Wisconsin to have real elections reform.
I do not want a Dane County courtroom to be where one of the biggest electoral questions of the generation gets settled for good, quietly, through a summary judgment motion most voters will never hear about.
The two major parties are already running the show.
They do not need a court to hand them one more structural advantage disguised as reform.
Pete Karas is the Green Party candidate for Wisconsin Secretary of State.
