While most people recognize the flag instantly, few know the remarkable history behind it or the important role Ohio has played in its story.
For residents of Ohio’s 34th House District (Akron, Hudson, Munroe Falls, Silver Lake, Stow, and Tallmadge), the history of the American flag is more than a national story.
It is part of our local story as well.
The Birth of an American Symbol On June 14, 1777, during the Revolutionary War, the Continental Congress adopted the first official American flag.
Their resolution was simple: thirteen stars on a blue field and thirteen red and white alternating stripes representing the original states united in a common cause.
The young nation was still fighting for its independence, and the flag quickly became a powerful symbol of liberty, self-government, and the promise of a new republic.
Ohio Becomes the Seventeenth Star As America expanded westward, the flag expanded with it.
In 1803, Ohio became the seventeenth state admitted to the Union.
The same era that saw Ohio join the Union also saw the founding of many of the communities we call home today.
Hudson was founded in 1799 by settlers from Connecticut who traveled west into the Connecticut Western Reserve.
Tallmadge followed in 1807, while Stow and neighboring communities grew during the early decades of the nineteenth century.
Those settlers arrived at a time when the nation itself was still taking shape.
The flag they knew looked very different from the one we fly today.
Instead of fifty stars, it contained just fifteen.
The United States was a young nation, and Northeast Ohio was a frontier.
It is remarkable to imagine those early settlers building homes, schools, churches, and businesses beneath a flag representing a country less than a generation removed from the Revolution.
The Flag That Inspired the National Anthem The Stars and Stripes gained even greater significance during the War of 1812.
In September 1814, after witnessing the bombardment of Fort McHenry in Baltimore Harbor, Francis Scott Key looked out at dawn and saw the American flag still flying over the fort.
Inspired by the sight, he penned the words that would eventually become "The Star-Spangled Banner." While that famous moment occurred hundreds of miles away, Ohioans were also experiencing the uncertainties of war with battles along the shores of Lake Erie.
Communities across the frontier watched events unfold closely, knowing the conflict would help determine the future of the young nation.
The flag that inspired our national anthem represented not only victory, but resilience.
A Growing Nation and a Growing Debate As the United States continued to expand, new states brought new stars to the flag.
Each addition reflected a nation growing in size, influence, and opportunity.
Yet expansion also brought difficult questions.
Throughout the nineteenth century, Americans debated the future of slavery, the balance of power between states, and the meaning of the freedoms promised at the nation's founding.
Those conversations were not confined to Washington.
They occurred in communities throughout Ohio, including Hudson, which became known for its support of abolition and the Underground Railroad.
The history of the flag is, in many ways, the history of America's ongoing effort to live up to its highest ideals.
Under One Flag When the Civil War erupted in 1861, Americans found themselves divided in ways previously unimaginable.
Yet throughout the conflict, the Stars and Stripes remained the symbol of the Union that generations had worked to build.
Men from communities across Summit County answered the call to serve.
Families in Hudson, Stow, Tallmadge, and neighboring towns experienced the sacrifices and hardships of war alongside millions of other Americans.
The preservation of the Union ensured that the flag would continue to represent one nation, united despite its differences.
Why We Celebrate Flag Day Although the American flag had existed since 1777, Flag Day did not become an official national observance until much later.
Throughout the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, educators, veterans, civic leaders, and patriotic organizations promoted greater recognition of June 14 as the anniversary of the flag's adoption.
In 1949, Congress officially established National Flag Day, and President Harry Truman signed the legislation into law.
Ohio even played a role in helping advance the effort, further strengthening our state's connection to this important tradition.
The Ohio Student Who Designed Today's Flag One of the most fascinating chapters in the flag's history also has an Ohio connection.
In 1958, a Lancaster, Ohio high school student named Robert Heft designed a new American flag as part of a school project.
Anticipating the addition of Alaska and Hawaii to the Union, he arranged the stars into a clean, symmetrical pattern.
His teacher reportedly awarded the project a B-minus.
Soon afterward, however, Heft's design was selected from among thousands of submissions and ultimately adopted as the official flag of the United States.
The design he created as a teenager remains the fifty-star flag that flies across America today.
A Symbol That Connects Generations From the Revolutionary War to the frontier settlements of the Western Reserve, from the Civil War to the Moon, the American flag has witnessed the story of our nation unfold.
It flew when Ohio became the seventeenth state.
It flew when Akron, Munroe Falls, and Silver Lake were young communities finding their place in a growing country.
It flew over generations who built businesses, raised families, served in uniform, and contributed to the strength of our state and nation.
This Flag Day, as we reflect on the history of the Stars and Stripes, we can also reflect on our own place in that story.
The flag remains a symbol not only of where America has been, but of the freedoms, responsibilities, and opportunities we share as citizens.
For more than two centuries, Ohio has helped shape the American story.
Every star on the flag tells part of that story—and one of those stars has proudly represented Ohio (and all of us) since 1818.
