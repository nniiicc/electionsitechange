They deal with weather, volatile commodity prices, disease, labor shortages, fuel costs and global markets.
What they should not also have to manage is a federal government that changes the rules with little warning and expects producers to absorb the consequences.
The administration’s latest beef-import decision is a good example.
President Trump recently announced plans to waive out-of-quota tariffs for 90 days on as much as 300,000 metric tons of imported lean beef trimmings, with foreign exporters expected to sell that product below current market prices.
For consumers frustrated by grocery prices, cheaper beef may sound appealing.
But agricultural policy cannot be judged by the announcement alone.
We have to ask what it does to the people producing our food and whether it actually addresses the problem it claims to solve.
For cattle producers, timing matters enormously.
Many cow-calf operations spend nearly an entire year paying for feed, fuel, fencing, veterinary care and equipment before selling their annual calf crop.
In Missouri, October and November are a critical part of the fall calf run for many spring-calving herds.
There is no do-over if Washington disrupts that market at exactly the wrong time.
And this is not an isolated policy change.
In February, the administration expanded tariff-free access for Argentine lean beef by 80,000 metric tons annually.
In May, the White House proposed suspending quantitative limits under the beef tariff-rate quota system for 200 days, which could have allowed eligible trading partners to ship unlimited volumes at lower tariff rates.
That action was later postponed.
Now comes another proposed intervention.
Meanwhile, U.S. beef imports were already running at record levels.
During the first quarter of 2026, the United States imported 562,000 metric tons of beef and beef products, up 18 percent from the previous year and 122 percent from five years earlier.
