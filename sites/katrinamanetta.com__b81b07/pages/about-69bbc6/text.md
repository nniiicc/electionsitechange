Leadership that is rooted here, answers to real people, and is willing to challenge the way things have always been done.
For me, this is not a sudden decision or a political career move.
It is the next step in work I have already been doing on the ground in Metro Detroit.
As a community organizer, I have spent years helping neighbors get informed, get involved, and speak up about the issues affecting their daily lives.
I have knocked doors, had kitchen-table conversations, helped people navigate broken systems, and brought everyday voices into spaces where they are usually ignored.

My Story I did not wait for permission to start showing up.
I was born and raised in Shelby Township, and I still live here today, in the same house I grew up in.
I went to Utica schools, graduated from Oakland University, and was raised by this community in every sense of the word.
This district taught me early that you show up, you do your part, and you look out for one another.
I am in my early thirties, part of a generation that worked hard, followed the rules, and still found stability harder to reach than it should be.
I have always pushed back on the idea that “this is just how it is.” Over time, that instinct became action.
I have never been comfortable accepting broken systems as normal.
Some people like to turn that awareness into an insult.
I am awake to what is happening here because this is my home, and looking away has never been an option.
I became a community organizer because I was tired of watching decisions get made about our lives without people like us in the room.
I have helped neighbors understand what was happening at the local and state level, encouraged people who had never been involved before to participate, and worked to turn frustration into collective action.
Organizing taught me how to listen, how to mediate between different perspectives, and how to keep people engaged even when change feels slow.
In recognition of that work, I was honored with the Bayard Rustin Strategist Award at Michigan United’s 2026 MLK Celebration, an award that celebrates organizers who build people power and advance justice in our communities.
That work also showed me the limits of organizing alone.
Too many of the problems families are facing here are shaped by decisions made in Lansing.
And too often, the people making those decisions have never lived the consequences.
That is why I am running for the Michigan State House.
Learn More About Why I'm Running I know What the Struggle Looks Like Because I Have Lived It, Not Studied It For the last eight years, I have worked as a server.
I know what it is like to depend on tips, to budget week to week, and to feel the stress of one unexpected expense throwing everything off.
When you live that way, there is no cushion.
You learn quickly how fragile stability can be.
That experience changes how you see people.
It builds grit, but it also builds empathy.
You stop assuming that anyone is lazy or irresponsible when you understand how close most families are to the edge, even when they are doing everything right.
My family knows that reality deeply.
My aunt has a mental disability, and my mom has been her full-time caregiver since I was five years old.
I grew up watching what responsibility looks like when it is constant, unpaid, and rarely supported.
It showed me how much our systems rely on people quietly carrying enormous weight without help.
I have also lived through deep loss.
I lost my sister, my dad, and my boyfriend within a few short years.
That kind of loss does not harden you.
It strips away what is unimportant and sharpens your sense of what is worth fighting for.
These experiences did not push me away from my community.
They pulled me closer to it.

It also taught me where the limits are when the people making decisions are disconnected from the consequences.
Too many of the challenges families face here are shaped by policies set in Lansing.
Rising costs.
Inadequate support for caregivers.
A government that feels distant, unresponsive, or captured by special interests.
I have seen firsthand how hard people work to be heard and how often those voices are ignored.
I am running for the Michigan State House because representation should mean more than a title.
It should mean understanding, accountability, and follow-through.
Lansing needs leaders who know what it feels like to live paycheck to paycheck, to navigate broken systems, and to fight for change without a safety net.
This campaign is about taking the fight we have been waging locally and bringing it to the state level, where decisions actually get made.

Follow the Campaign A People-Powered Campaign Accountable to the Community I do not take corporate PAC money.
That choice is intentional.
Right now, our political system is shaped by who can afford access.
Research shows that a small number of large corporations spend billions lobbying lawmakers and receive trillions in taxpayer-funded contracts, subsidies, and support in return.
That kind of imbalance makes it harder for everyday people to be heard, even when there is overwhelming public support for change.
In fact, multiple studies have shown that the preferences of average Americans have almost zero impact on whether legislation actually passes.
When money and access outweigh public opinion, representation stops working the way it should.
That is why this campaign is powered by everyday people.
Not corporations.
Not insiders.
People who live here, work here, and feel the consequences of broken systems every day.
I am running this campaign the same way I have lived my life.
Without special treatment.
Without a safety net.
With transparency, hard work, and accountability to the people around me.
If you believe our community deserves leadership that listens to working families and answers to them, I would be honored to have you be part of this campaign.
