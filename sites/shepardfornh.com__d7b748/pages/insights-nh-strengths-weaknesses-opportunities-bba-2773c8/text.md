At a glance A statewide SWOT assessment: the strengths New Hampshire can build on, weaknesses that constrain it, opportunities worth exploring, and outside threats that require preparation.
Read full text View PDF Print / Save as PDF Open / save PDF ↗ Executive Summary: New Hampshire’s Strengths, Risks, and the Choices Ahead New Hampshire is, by many measures, one of the strongest places in America to live.
People earn high incomes, crime is low, health outcomes are strong, and the state offers an exceptional quality of life.
At the same time, beneath these successes are structural challenges that—if left unaddressed—could weaken the very advantages residents value most.
This SWOT analysis looks honestly at what New Hampshire does well , where it is vulnerable , what opportunities exist , and what external threats could disrupt the state’s future .
The goal is not to advocate a particular policy, but to clarify the trade-offs facing the state so residents can better understand the decisions ahead.
What New Hampshire Does Well New Hampshire’s biggest strengths are economic and social: High incomes and low poverty mean most residents are doing well by national standards.
Low crime and good health outcomes make communities safe and attractive.
An educated workforce and near-universal broadband support high-skill jobs and remote work.
Strong fiscal management has earned the state an excellent credit rating and solid reserves.
No broad-based income or sales tax remains a defining feature that attracts residents and businesses.
Natural beauty and quality of life fuel tourism and make the state appealing to families and retirees.
These strengths reinforce one another.
A safe, healthy, well-educated population supports economic success, which in turn supports public services and community stability.
Where the State Is Struggling Despite these advantages, several internal weaknesses are putting pressure on residents and local governments: Housing is scarce and expensive.
Many workers—especially young families and service employees—cannot afford to live near their jobs.
Property taxes are very high.
Because the state raises relatively little revenue itself, costs are pushed down to towns, creating large tax differences between communities.
The population is aging rapidly.
Retirements are accelerating, births are declining, and employers struggle to find workers.
Education funding is unequal and legally challenged.
Courts have ruled that the state is not paying its constitutional share, leaving local taxpayers to fill the gap.
Mental health and rural healthcare access remain strained , even as demand rises.
State revenues are narrow and volatile , relying heavily on business taxes that fall quickly in downturns.
These are not short-term problems.
They are structural issues that compound over time.
Opportunities the State Can Still Seize New Hampshire also has real opportunities—if it can act on them: Remote work and in-migration can help replenish the workforce if housing is available.
Federal infrastructure and energy funding can modernize roads, bridges, broadband, and power systems.
Clean energy and efficiency investments could lower some of the region’s highest electricity costs.
Advanced manufacturing and tech hubs offer high-wage job growth outside Boston.
Tourism and outdoor recreation continue to expand, especially beyond peak seasons.
Regional collaboration can reduce costs by sharing infrastructure and services.
The “silver economy” —services for older adults—can become an economic strength if planned intentionally.
These opportunities align closely with the state’s existing strengths—but only if barriers such as housing shortages and infrastructure resistance are addressed.
External Threats That Cannot Be Ignored Several risks lie largely outside the state’s direct control: An economic recession would hit New Hampshire hard because its revenue system depends on business profits.
Worsening housing costs could push workers out faster than new residents arrive.
Federal funding cuts would strain state and local budgets.
Climate change threatens winter recreation, infrastructure, and flood-prone communities.
Competition from neighboring states for workers and businesses is increasing.
Deferred infrastructure maintenance raises the risk of costly failures.
Political stalemate has slowed responses to housing, education, and revenue challenges.
These threats do not arrive one at a time—they interact and reinforce one another.
The Central Tension: Advantage vs.
Sustainability At the heart of this analysis is a fundamental tension: New Hampshire’s low-tax model delivers real benefits—but it also limits the state’s ability to respond to modern costs, court mandates, demographic change, and economic shocks.
Avoiding broad-based taxes has shifted pressure onto: Property taxes, Local school budgets, Housing affordability, Infrastructure maintenance, And long-term workforce stability.
The question is not whether New Hampshire should abandon its identity—but whether it can adapt its systems to sustain that identity over the next 20 years.
What This Means for Residents For ordinary citizens, this SWOT points to a simple reality: The state is strong today.
Many current problems are the predictable result of long-standing policy choices.
Doing nothing is itself a choice—with consequences for taxes, schools, housing, and services.
Future decisions will involve real trade-offs, not easy wins.
Understanding those trade-offs is essential for informed civic debate.
This executive summary is intended to support that understanding.
Purpose and Value of a SWOT Analysis A SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) is a foundational strategic framework used to evaluate an organization’s competitive position and to develop strategic planning.
The Purpose Internal Audit: It forces an honest look at internal factors— Strengths (what you do well) and Weaknesses (where you struggle)—to understand your true capabilities.
External Scan: It requires analyzing the external environment— Opportunities (market trends you can exploit) and Threats (competitors or risks)—to understand the landscape you operate in.
Integration: Its primary goal is to connect these two views, helping you leverage internal strengths to capture external opportunities, or to shore up weaknesses to defend against threats.
The Value Clarity & Focus: It cuts through noise to provide a single, organized snapshot of the current reality, preventing “strategic drift.” Risk Mitigation: By explicitly listing threats (like the “checkmate” scenarios discussed in the NH analysis), organizations can prepare contingency plans rather than reacting in crisis.
Actionable Strategy: It moves planning from abstract goals to concrete actions.
For example, matching a Strength (e.g., strong fiscal reserves) with an Opportunity (investing in infrastructure) creates a clear strategic initiative.
Consensus Building: It serves as a common language for stakeholders, aligning diverse teams (e.g., finance, operations, policy) around a shared understanding of the challenges and goals.
Strengths (Internal, Positive) S1.
Affluent, High-Quality Economy – Top Income and Low Poverty New Hampshire’s population enjoys one of the highest median household incomes in the nation and a very low poverty rate.
In 2024, median household income reached $99,782 , remaining nearly 22% above the U.S. median (~$81,600).
The state’s 7.2% poverty rate is the lowest of all states (U.S. ~12.5%), and child poverty stands at approximately 8.0% , also among the lowest in the country.
High educational attainment and a mix of well-paying industries (tech, healthcare, advanced manufacturing) contribute to broadly shared prosperity.
Why it matters: Affluence translates to a strong tax base, consumer spending, and lower social service burdens.
New Hampshire’s low poverty also correlates with positive social outcomes (lower crime, better health) that reinforce its quality of life appeal.
(Inference: The state’s economic stability is a “moat” that protects it during downturns, though recent data suggests income growth has flattened in real terms, highlighting the need to sustain high-value job creation.) Confidence: High.
(Confirmed by U.S.
Census Bureau 2024 ACS 1-Year Estimates released in September 2025.) S2.
Very Low Unemployment and High Labor Participation New Hampshire is effectively at full employment, with one of the tightest labor markets in the country.
The state’s unemployment rate was a preliminary 3.0% in late 2025 (vs.
4.4% U.S. rate in December 2025), consistently remaining well below the national average.
Labor force participation hovers around 65.4% (significantly above the 62.4% national rate), indicating that a large share of residents are employed or actively seeking work.
New Hampshire also boasts low underemployment; its broader “U-6” measure was 6.3% in mid-2025, roughly two percentage points lower than the U.S. rate of 8.4% (Dec 2025).
Why it matters: A fully engaged workforce signals robust economic health – jobs are plentiful and residents are working.
High participation, including among women ( 60.9% vs ~57.4% U.S.) and seniors, expands the labor pool for employers.
(Inference: Employers benefit from a deep labor participation culture, and households benefit from earnings – though such tightness can constrain growth if not addressed).
Confidence: High.
(Consistent with Bureau of Labor Statistics and NH Employment Security data released in January 2026).
S3.
Educated and Skilled Workforce New Hampshire workers are among the most educated in the U.S., underpinning a productive economy.
41.5% of adults have a bachelor’s degree or higher (vs. ~35% U.S.), and over 94% have at least a high school diploma (vs. ~89% U.S.)—one of the highest rates in the nation.
State students continue to score above national averages in core K-12 metrics; for instance, 2024 NAEP results show NH 4th graders scored 7 points higher in reading than the national average (221 vs.
214).
The state also has robust career training: active apprenticeships have surged, reaching over 3,530 participants in late 2025, an ~85% increase over the last decade according to the Community College System of NH.
Why it matters: A well-educated workforce attracts employers in tech, R&D, and advanced manufacturing.
It also correlates with higher productivity and innovation—New Hampshire was a top-5 state for labor productivity growth in 2024 ( +4.3% ), significantly outpacing the national average.
(Inference: This strength positions NH to compete for knowledge-driven industries and adapt to technological changes, though maintaining this edge requires retaining graduates who often migrate to larger metros.) Confidence: High.
(Verified by 2024 Census American Community Survey data, 2024 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) scores, and 2025 Department of Labor/CCSNH reports.) S4.
Low Crime and Healthy Communities New Hampshire offers an exceptionally safe and healthy living environment.
It consistently has one of the lowest crime rates in the nation: in 2024, its violent crime rate was 110 per 100,000 residents , ranking second lowest in the U.S. (behind only Maine) and nearly 70% below the national average .
Property crime is also exceptionally low ( 918 per 100,000 vs. ~1,760 U.S.).
Health outcomes rank in the top tier: life expectancy is ~78.5 years , and the state held its uninsured rate steady at 4.5% in 2024 (significantly better than the national average of ~7.7%).
The state’s opioid overdose death rate has seen a promising turnaround; preliminary 2024 data shows a ~33% decrease in confirmed overdose deaths compared to the same period in 2023, signaling that public health interventions are gaining traction.
Why it matters: Safe communities and good health reduce costs (e.g., NH spends less on corrections per capita than 45 other states) and make the state attractive for families and retirees.
These factors also bolster workforce productivity by minimizing crime-related disruptions and maintaining a healthier labor pool.
(Confidence: High.
Confirmed by 2024 FBI Uniform Crime Reporting data and late 2025 CDC provisional mortality statistics.) S5.
Strong Fiscal Reserves and Credit Standing Prudent fiscal management has maintained New Hampshire’s solid financial cushion and high credit standing.
In March 2024 , S&P Global Ratings upgraded NH’s general obligation bond rating to AA+ (its first upgrade in decades), citing the state’s “strong, sustainable, and efficient position.” While the state faced a revenue dip in FY2025, the Rainy Day Fund (Revenue Stabilization Reserve) remains robust, projected to hold over $228 million through the FY2026–2027 biennium.
The state’s tax-supported debt per capita remains low—roughly half the U.S. state average —and it strictly adheres to statutory debt limits.
Why it matters: A higher credit rating reduces borrowing costs for infrastructure projects (saving millions in interest payments).
Healthy reserves mean the state can weather economic downturns (like the revenue volatility seen in 2025) without being forced into immediate, drastic service cuts or tax hikes.
(Inference: This fiscal strength provides the “dry powder” needed to respond to future crises or invest strategically, a critical asset given NH’s limited revenue levers.) Confidence: High.
(Validated by the S&P upgrade in March 2024 and official State Treasury reports from mid-2025.) S6.
Business-Friendly Tax Climate (No Income/Sales Tax) New Hampshire’s tax structure—no broad-based income or sales tax—remains its most distinctive draw for businesses and individuals.
As of January 1, 2025 , the state fully repealed its Interest & Dividends (I&D) Tax, making New Hampshire the only state in the nation with no tax on earned income, investment income, or retail sales.
Consequently, overall tax burdens on households are exceptionally low: NH ranks 3rd lowest for total tax burden (approx.
5.94% of personal income, per WalletHub 2025) and moved up to #3 overall in the Tax Foundation’s 2026 State Business Tax Climate Index .
While NH levies business taxes, rates have become more competitive: the Business Profits Tax (BPT) is now 7.5% and the Business Enterprise Tax (BET) is 0.55% (for 2024–2025 tax periods), with legislative proposals in 2025 aiming to lower the BET further to 0.50% by 2026.
Why it matters: The complete absence of income/sales taxes is a magnet for high-earning individuals (e.g., entrepreneurs, retirees) and drives significant cross-border retail activity from high-tax neighbors like Massachusetts.
It maximizes disposable income for residents, bolstering local purchasing power.
(Inference: This tax advantage is the cornerstone of NH’s “Live Free or Die” brand; the recent I&D repeal removes the final “asterisk” from its no-income-tax status, likely accelerating the migration of wealth into the state.) Confidence: High.
(Confirmed by the enacted Jan 1, 2025 I&D tax repeal and the Tax Foundation’s late-2025 release placing NH at #3 nationally.) S7.
High Broadband and Digital Connectivity · New Hampshire has achieved near-universal internet access, cementing its status as a leader in digital infrastructure.
As of late 2025, over 98% of homes and businesses have access to high-speed broadband, leaving only ~5,000 addresses in the entire state classified as “unserved.” This progress places NH #3 in the nation for Internet Access (U.S.
News & World Report 2025) and #1 for median download speeds (165.3 Mbps).
The state has aggressively targeted the “last mile” using public-private partnerships, recently securing $18.6 million in targeted BEAD funding (Nov 2025) to connect the final hard-to-reach locations in the North Country. · Why it matters: Strong broadband access enables remote work, e-commerce, and telehealth—crucial for competitiveness in a decentralized economy.
This connectivity is a primary driver behind NH’s ability to attract remote workers (who now make up a significant portion of in-migration).
It also supports a thriving tech ecosystem: New Hampshire has one of the highest concentrations of tech workers in the country ( ~10.7% of the workforce vs. ~7.3% nationally), proving that high-tech jobs can flourish outside of major urban hubs. · (Confidence: High.
Data from the 2025 NH Broadband Mapping Initiative, U.S.
News rankings, and confirmed federal funding allocations validate the state’s top-tier connectivity status.) S8.
Attractive Quality of Life and Environment The state offers natural beauty, recreation, and livability that consistently rank highly.
Over 82% of New Hampshire’s land is forest or protected, featuring the White Mountains, lakes, and seacoast – outdoor recreation now contributes $3.9 billion to the state’s economy (BEA 2024 release), a massive jump from previous years.
The state ranks #1 for Public Safety and #1 Healthiest State in the nation (U.S.
News & World Report 2025; United Health Foundation 2025).
Commute times remain reasonable (average 27.2 minutes ), and urban areas are relatively low-congestion.
New Hampshire also has among the lowest carbon emissions per capita in the nation (about 9.7 MTCO2 vs ~14 MT U.S. average) due to cleaner energy and less heavy industry.
Why it matters: These factors help attract skilled workers and retirees.
Families often choose NH for its good schools and access to nature—a key reason the state was named the #1 Best Place to Raise a Family in 2025.
The high quality of life supports workforce retention – e.g., surveys by NH Businesses for Social Responsibility find that employees cite “community environment” as a top reason to stay in-state.
(Inference: Quality of life is a strategic asset that complements the economic strengths, helping NH punch above its weight in talent attraction despite its small size.) Confidence: High.
(Supported by the latest BEA outdoor economy reports, 2025 national safety/health rankings, and confirmed Census commute data.) Weaknesses (Internal, Negative) W1.
Acute Housing Shortage and High Costs Housing has become a critical weak point – supply is not keeping up with demand, driving affordability problems.
New Hampshire is undersupplying housing by an estimated ~20–30% relative to need: only ~5,100 housing permits were issued in 2024, far below the ~9,000/year pace seen in the early 2000s.
Rental vacancy rates remain extraordinarily low ( 0.6% in 2024–2025), and home prices have continued their relentless climb.
By late 2025, the median single-family home price reached $535,000 (peaking at $565,000 in June), a record high.
The median home now costs ~5.5 times the median household income, significantly above the 4.0 “affordability” benchmark.
Rental costs have similarly spiked – the median 2-bedroom rent hit $1,833 (with utilities) in 2024, requiring an income of over $73,000 to be affordable, which prices out many essential service workers.
Evidence: NH Housing’s 2025 reports indicate a need for over 30,000 additional units to restore a balanced market.
The affordability gap is stark: recent data shows only ~15% of NH households can afford the median-priced home, and inventory stands at just 2.0 months of supply (vs. a healthy 5–7 months).
Every county now faces this pressure; even traditionally affordable rural areas have seen prices jump as inventory remains scarce.
Why it matters: Expensive, scarce housing deters young families and in-migrants – surveys consistently find housing costs are the top reason young professionals consider leaving.
Employers cite lack of workforce housing as a primary barrier to hiring (e.g., hospitals struggling to recruit nurses who cannot find apartments).
(Inference: If unaddressed, this weakness could stall economic growth and exacerbate labor shortages, as workers simply live elsewhere.
It also widens inequality, as lower-income residents spend >50% of income on rent or are pushed into longer commutes.) Confidence: High.
(Supported by multiple 2025 data points: NH Association of Realtors price data, NH Housing rental surveys, and permit issuance statistics.) W2.
Heavy Reliance on Property Taxes (Tax Inequity) New Hampshire’s tax structure leans extremely on local property taxes, creating high burdens in some communities and funding disparities.
Over 64% of all state-local revenue in NH comes from property taxes (highest in the U.S.), and local governments continue to shoulder roughly 70% of school funding costs.
As a result, NH homeowners face the 4th-highest property tax burden in the nation (averaging nearly $6,505 per year on a median home) and a high effective tax rate.
Property tax bills can be especially onerous for residents on fixed incomes (e.g., many seniors) and those in property-poor towns.
Communities with low property values must set very high tax rates to fund services, yet still often spend less per student than their wealthier counterparts.
Evidence: In 2025, the disparity remains stark: property-poor towns like Charlestown and Keene faced tax rates exceeding $30 per $1,000 of value, while wealthy resort towns like Moultonborough enjoyed rates as low as ~$5.33 .
The state’s funding inequities were confirmed in July 2025 , when the NH Supreme Court ruled in ConVal v.
State that the state’s base education funding ($4,100 per pupil) was unconstitutionally low, mandating an increase to at least $7,356 .
However, in the parallel Rand decision (June 2025), the Court ruled that wealthy towns can keep excess “Statewide Education Property Tax” revenue rather than redistributing it, cementing the advantage of property-rich municipalities.
Why it matters: High property taxes can discourage home ownership and business investment (since NH taxes business property and machinery heavily too).
It also leads to entrenched regional inequality—prosperous towns can thrive with low rates and high services, while struggling towns face a “death spiral” of rising rates and service cuts.
(Inference: The reliance on an essentially regressive tax (not tied to ability to pay) is a structural weakness that fuels political conflicts and hampers equal opportunity, creating a “two-tier” state based on zip code.) Confidence: High.
(Confirmed by 2025 legislative audits, the ConVal/Rand Supreme Court opinions, and 2025 national tax burden rankings.) W3.
Rapid Demographic Aging and Workforce Drain New Hampshire’s population is aging faster than the nation’s, raising acute concerns about labor force sustainability and caregiving burdens.
The state’s median age has risen to 43.6 years (2024 estimate), making it the second-oldest state in the U.S. (tied with Vermont, behind Maine).
By 2030 , the population of seniors (65+) is projected to permanently exceed the number of children (under 18)—a “demographic crossover” that has already occurred if counting residents over 60.
Meanwhile, the prime working-age share is shrinking relative to retirees; deaths now consistently outnumber births in the state, meaning 100% of workforce growth relies on migration.
Evidence: As of late 2024, nearly 22% of New Hampshire’s population was over age 65, while the under-18 population had shrunk to just ~18% .
The workforce pipeline faces a “silver tsunami”: state labor data from 2025 reveals that 31% of NH’s labor force is now age 55 or older .
While migration has slightly boosted the 25–44 cohort recently, it is insufficient to offset retirements in critical sectors.
Employers in healthcare and manufacturing report persistent vacancies not due to a lack of budget, but a lack of bodies; for instance, the ratio of potential caregivers (aged 45–64) to seniors (80+) is plummeting, creating a structural deficit in eldercare capacity.
Why it matters: Fewer working-age adults relative to retirees strains everything from the tax base (fewer taxpayers supporting more services) to the health system.
It creates a vicious cycle where a lack of workers (e.g., in child care or elder care) prevents other working-age adults from entering the labor force.
(Inference: Without aggressive measures to attract younger people—e.g., affordable housing and “lifestyle” infrastructure—NH risks a permanent “demographic cliff” where businesses structurally cannot fill jobs, forcing them to relocate.
The aging weakness is internal, structural, and currently the state’s biggest economic throttle.) Confidence: High.
(U.S.
Census Bureau 2024 estimates and NH Employment Security 2025 reports unanimously underscore the severity of the aging trend and the “deaths > births” reality.) W5.
Constrained State Revenue Flexibility The state government’s ability to raise revenue for new initiatives is limited by its narrow tax base and political aversion to new taxes.
With no broad sales or income tax—and the Interest & Dividends (I&D) tax fully repealed as of January 1, 2025 —New Hampshire relies on relatively volatile streams like the Business Profits Tax (BPT) and Business Enterprise Tax (BET).
When economic downturns hit or new spending needs arise, the state has fewer levers than most.
Evidence: This volatility was starkly realized in Fiscal Year 2025 , which ended with a $67.3 million deficit (requiring a withdrawal from the Rainy Day Fund), a sharp reversal from prior surpluses.
State revenues in late 2024 tracked ~5.8% below the 15-year trend as business tax receipts slowed.
Additionally, statutory constraints limit new revenue sources even as costs grow.
For instance, while the actual cost to educate a student reached $21,545 in 2024, the state’s “base adequacy” grant remained near $4,100–$4,800 , shifting the burden to local property taxpayers.
This creates a structural gap: the state has the lowest share of K–12 funding in the nation (~30% vs. ~47% national avg), yet attempts to boost state aid are blocked by the lack of available revenue buckets.
Why it matters: This weakness means the state often under-invests in areas that fuel long-term growth (e.g., higher education, public health, transit) because raising funds is politically and structurally difficult.
Local governments backfill the gap, but this drives up property taxes, effectively transferring the “revenue constraint” problem to homeowners.
(Inference: The revenue rigidity is no longer just a theoretical risk—it is a current fiscal reality.
As the state faces deficits again in 2025–2026, it may be forced to cut services or delay modernization, putting it at a disadvantage compared to peers with more resilient, diversified tax structures.) Confidence: High.
(Confirmed by the January 2026 state audit reporting the FY2025 deficit and official legislative records on the I&D tax repeal.) W6.
Education Funding Inequity and Strain on Local Schools The heavy local funding model for education has led to unequal resources and rising strain on property-poor districts.
Because ~70% of K–12 funding is still raised via local property taxes (among the highest rates in the nation), towns with low property values struggle to fund competitive teacher salaries, facilities, or programs.
Evidence: In the 2023–2024 school year, the disparity widened: the highest-spending NH school district (Pittsburg) spent $44,484 per pupil , while large urban districts like Manchester spent only ~$17,734 – a massive gap largely tied to local property wealth (state average was $21,545 ).
The state’s funding formula remains under intense legal scrutiny.
In a landmark July 2025 decision , the New Hampshire Supreme Court upheld a lower court ruling ( ConVal School District v.
State ) declaring the current base adequacy funding of ~$4,100 unconstitutional, affirming that the true minimum cost for an adequate education is at least $7,356 per student .
Meanwhile, declining enrollments exacerbate the issue: public school enrollment fell to 160,323 in late 2025 (down ~20% since 2002), meaning rural districts lose “economies of scale,” driving per-student costs higher even as they cut offerings.
Why it matters: Educational weakness in under-resourced communities translates to skill disparities in the workforce.
It can also perpetuate poverty cycles in poorer regions (e.g., parts of Coos County) where special education costs— 83% of which are funded locally —devour budgets.
Additionally, property tax strain for school funding fuels political division; many towns regularly vote down school budget increases, causing deferred maintenance and program cuts.
(Inference: Without the structural reform now mandated by the courts, this weakness could undermine the state’s overall strong educational outcomes and create pockets of under-education that drag on economic potential.) Confidence: High.
(Supported by the definite July 2025 Supreme Court ruling and official NH Department of Education fiscal reports released in 2025.) W7.
Health Care Access Gaps, Especially Mental Health Despite generally good health outcomes, New Hampshire’s healthcare system has notable capacity issues, particularly in mental health and rural care.
The state has among the fewest psychiatric inpatient beds per capita in New England—leading to a chronic backlog of patients waiting in ERs.
While the state launched “Mission Zero” in 2023 to eliminate this practice, daily boarding persists, exacerbated by the May 2025 construction halt (due to foundation issues) of the new 24-bed forensic psychiatric hospital in Concord, which pushes relief out to late 2026 or 2027.
Primary care provider shortages remain acute north of Concord; most rural counties retained their designation as Health Professional Shortage Areas (HPSAs) in the October 2025 federal update.
Evidence: As of late 2024, the statewide ratio of population to primary care physicians was ~1,247:1 , but this masks deep regional disparities; rural counties like Coos continue to see ratios nearly double that of the Seacoast.
2025 state reports confirm that 9 of NH’s 16 rural hospitals have closed labor and delivery units since 2000, forcing patients to travel farther for essential care.
In mental health, New Hampshire Hospital in Concord is operating at full capacity (~185 beds), yet demand continues to outpace supply.
Meanwhile, the “silver tsunami” is pressuring budgets: preliminary State Fiscal Year 2025 data shows Medicaid nursing facility reimbursements rose to ~$287 million (up from ~$256 million the prior year), with per-person costs averaging over $56,000 .
Why it matters: Gaps in healthcare infrastructure can lead to worse health outcomes in parts of the state and higher long-term costs (e.g., untreated mental illness contributing to homelessness or incarceration).
Businesses also consider healthcare access a key factor for location—employees need assurance of nearby services.
(Inference: If unaddressed, these weaknesses could degrade NH’s quality of life advantage; mental health capacity remains the system’s “choke point,” affecting community well-being and productivity despite high-level state interventions.) Confidence: High.
(Supported by the DHHS “Mission Zero” mid-year 2025 report, confirmed construction delays on the forensic hospital reported in Nov 2025, and FY2025 Medicaid fiscal data.) W8.
Small, Homogeneous Market (Innovation & Diversity Drawbacks) New Hampshire’s small population and relative lack of diversity can be a limiting factor for economic dynamism.
With 1.41 million people (2024 Census est.), the state’s market size is modest – there are fewer large urban centers to drive innovation clusters or support specialized industries compared to larger states.
Additionally, while the state is diversifying, NH’s workforce remains ~88% White (non-Hispanic) and only ~7% foreign-born , trailing the U.S. average (which hit ~15.6% foreign-born in 2024).
This homogeneity might hamper the state’s ability to attract a diverse talent pool or foster the breadth of perspectives that spur innovation.
Evidence: New Hampshire continues to attract significantly less venture capital than its southern neighbor; while Massachusetts led the nation in 2024 with over $21,000 in VC investment per $1 million of GDP , New Hampshire’s volume remains a fraction of that intensity.
Part of this is scale and part the absence of a global-tier research hub: while UNH successfully retained its top-tier “R1” research status in 2025 and grew external funding to $252 million , it remains smaller and less R&D-intensive than the multi-billion dollar ecosystems of MIT or Harvard.
Companies in tech and STEM fields increasingly emphasize diverse and inclusive environments – NH’s demographics could be seen as a drawback for recruitment.
Why it matters: A smaller, more insular market can mean fewer entrepreneurial startups (less “creative churn”) and can make it harder for global companies to convince talent to relocate.
(Inference: New Hampshire’s cultural and consumer offerings can seem limited to some young professionals compared to Boston or more diverse states – which may be a weakness in retaining graduates or attracting newcomers in certain sectors.) Confidence: High.
(Demographic data is based on confirmed 2024 U.S.
Census Bureau releases; venture capital and university research figures are drawn from 2025 fiscal reports and Carnegie Classification updates.) W9.
Energy Transmission Gridlock & Infrastructure NIMBYism (Weakness) High energy costs remain a competitive disadvantage, and the state’s “local control” culture threatens to block the infrastructure needed to solve it.
The Weakness: New Hampshire faces electricity rates ~40-50% above the U.S. average, a major burden for manufacturers and residents.
While the state has a massive opportunity to lower costs through offshore wind and Canadian hydro imports (O3), realizing these benefits requires building new high-voltage transmission lines through local communities.
Evidence: The state’s history of rejecting major transmission projects (e.g., Northern Pass) sets a precedent where local opposition can derail regional energy solutions.
The same “local control” friction that has diluted housing reform bills in 2024 and 2025 creates a high risk for energy infrastructure, as individual towns can use zoning to block or delay transmission lines needed to bring cheap power to the grid.
Why it matters: Without new transmission capacity, the recent federal offshore wind leases in the Gulf of Maine will benefit neighboring states while NH remains stuck with high rates.
This gridlock effectively traps the state in a high-cost energy environment, negating some of the savings from its favorable tax climate.
Confidence: Medium-High.
(Inferred from the established pattern of “local control” blocking zoning reforms and the logistical reality of the 2024 Gulf of Maine wind leases requiring onshore connection points.) Opportunities (External, Positive) O1.
In-Migration of Remote Workers and Families from High-Cost Areas New Hampshire can capitalize on the permanent shift to remote/hybrid work and the region’s cost differentials to attract new residents.
With Boston metro housing costs remaining prohibitive, many remote-capable workers continue to relocate for better affordability and quality of life.
In 2024, New Hampshire’s population reached a record 1.41 million , with growth driven entirely by migration (+9,200 net migrants from 2023–2024) which offset a natural decrease (more deaths than births).
This trend remains robust: 2025 real estate reports indicate that out-of-state buyers—primarily from Massachusetts—continue to dominate the market, often outcompeting locals with cash offers in the $600,000+ single-family range.
Why it’s an opportunity: Each new high-earning family brings income, skills, and tax base.
NH’s lack of income tax is a massive selling point for a MA worker who can work from home in NH (effectively giving themselves a ~5% raise by paying no MA income tax on days worked remotely, adhering to current physical presence tax rules).
The state can market itself as the “affordable Boston exurb” with recreation at your doorstep and nearly universal broadband.
Expanded commuter rail (Nashua-Manchester) or coworking hubs could amplify this appeal.

The “Cliff” (Fiscal Sustainability) Description: The “New Hampshire Advantage” (no income/sales tax) is the state’s economic engine, but it has left the government with a “brittle” revenue structure that is now cracking under modern costs.
Data: Revenue Volatility: In FY2025, business tax revenues dropped ~12.4%, causing a $67.3 million deficit .
The Squeeze: The state fully repealed the I&D tax (Jan 2025) just as federal pandemic aid ended and the Supreme Court mandated higher education spending.
Underinvestment: To keep the budget balanced, the state under-invests in infrastructure (192 red-list bridges) and relies on federal funds (like IIJA) to plug holes.
Why it’s Hard: The state has removed its shock absorbers .
By eliminating the I&D tax, it narrowed its revenue base right before a potential economic slowdown.
The tension is that preserving the political victory of “zero income tax” risks the operational failure of the state government to pay its bills or maintain its AA+ credit rating in the face of the new education funding mandates.
