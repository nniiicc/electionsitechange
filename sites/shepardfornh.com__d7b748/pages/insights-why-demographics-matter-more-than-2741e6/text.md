At a glance An analysis of how population trends, worker availability, housing and other conditions can affect New Hampshire’s economy.
It asks why tax rates alone offer an incomplete picture of long-term competitiveness.
Read full article Print / Save as PDF It’s time to admit that our state’s tax policy is only as good as the people it attracts.
To preserve our tax base and quality of life, our fiscal strategy must shift its focus toward actively recruiting and retaining younger workers to replace our aging population.
To do that effectively, we must address the foundational barriers to entry: affordable housing, accessible childcare, and a robust education system from K-12 through university.
The Hidden Fiscal Loop The most common argument against attracting young families is a fear of rising costs.
Many residents believe that blocking new development or limiting school funding saves money by keeping local costs down.
However, a "hidden fiscal loop" suggests the opposite is true: · Starving the Engine : As our retiring population grows and the working-age population shrinks, the labor pool contracts, triggering a downturn that harms the economy.
Without a reliable labor force, local businesses cannot expand or even maintain current operations. · Business Taxes Dwindle : Because New Hampshire’s state revenue depends heavily on business activity, labor shortages and business stagnation cause state tax revenues to flatline. · The Tax Base Shrinks : As state business tax revenues decline, funding for state aid to cities and towns declines, even as demand for services such as public safety and road maintenance continues to rise as the population ages. · The Only Levers Left: Raising Property Taxes Or Introducing Broad-based Taxes : To fill the resulting budget hole, towns are forced to shift costs down to the community level.
This forces local officials to pull the only levers they have left: raising property taxes on existing homeowners or introducing broad-based taxes.
By failing to invest in the younger generation, you are inadvertently forcing your own tax bill to explode to support a system that requires more services as it ages.
The "Arithmetic of Daily Life" Demographics is the "arithmetic of daily life".
Roughly one-third of New Hampshire residents are now over 55, and many will retire over the next decade.
This shift is a logistical challenge.
A functioning town requires a workforce in their #s, #s, and #s to "turn the gears": Public Safety : Younger residents are needed to serve as firefighters, EMTs, and police officers.
Infrastructure : Snowplow drivers, utility crews, and electricians are the backbone of our physical stability.
Essential Services : Grocery clerks, pharmacists, and home health aides keep daily life running smoothly.
The Foundational Pillars of Attraction To compete for a shrinking pool of young talent, New Hampshire must treat social infrastructure as a core economic driver.
1.
Housing: The Entry Point High housing costs are a primary deterrent for young talent.
Without affordable options, young professionals and graduates find it financially unfeasible to settle in the Granite State, opting instead for regions where their wages can cover the cost of living.
2.
Childcare: The Workforce Catalyst Access to childcare is as essential to a family's quality of life as housing.
The current crisis is reducing the labor force because young parents cannot return to work when there is no safe, affordable place for their children to go.
Childcare isn't just a "family issue"; it is an economic infrastructure issue.
3.
Education: The Long-Term Pipeline K-12 Excellence : School quality is often the first metric young families research when deciding where to settle.
Disinvesting in schools signals to young families that a town is not invested in its future.
University Partnerships : Retaining recent graduates from institutions like the University of New Hampshire is critical.
Programs that connect students with local internships and support student loan repayment are vital tools for anchoring young workers to the state.
Investment as Infrastructure We must stop viewing workforce housing, childcare, and education as "charity" and start seeing them as infrastructure for retirees.
Labor Supply : If a plumber or an EMT cannot afford to live in your town, they won't be there to answer the call when you need them.
Independence : You can only "age in place" if the community systems around you—like home health aides and pharmacies—are fully staffed by a younger workforce.
An aging state does not thrive by standing still.
It thrives when we build an environment where the next generation wants to live—because their success is your security.
