At a glance This article examines the state’s revenue structure, its Rainy Day Fund, and what past budget crises suggest about the risks for towns, schools, and property taxpayers during a future recession.
Read full article Print / Save as PDF Last week, I asked a simple question: If New Hampshire’s tax system is such a great model, why hasn’t another state copied it?
Being different does not automatically make a system worse, but understanding its trade-offs is essential.
New Hampshire’s choice is unusual: no broad-based personal income tax and no general sales tax.
Alaska also lacks those two statewide taxes, but relies heavily on natural-resource revenue.
New Hampshire’s distinction is its reliance on business taxes.
Fiscal year 2025 data from The Pew Charitable Trusts (Pew) highlights that distinction.
Looking at each state’s largest source of state tax revenue: 25 states relied on personal income taxes.
20 states relied on general sales taxes.
3 states —Alaska, New Mexico, and North Dakota—relied on severance taxes from oil, gas, and mineral extraction.
1 state —Vermont—relied on property taxes.
1 state—New Hampshire—relied on corporate income taxes.
This creates a fundamental trade-off: exposure to swings in business tax revenue .
Corporate income taxes are generally more volatile than broad-based sales or personal income taxes.
While this does not make New Hampshire’s tax system the most volatile overall, our heavy dependence on business taxes creates a structural risk that demands careful planning.
A financial cushion should reflect the risks it is built to absorb—and I am not convinced ours does.
Reserve Size Must Match Revenue Risk It is easy to look at New Hampshire’s $225 million Rainy Day Fund and assume it is sufficient.
But the real question is: How large is the risk it must cover?
According to the National Association of State Budget Officers (NASBO) Spring 2026 report, New Hampshire’s Rainy Day Fund equaled 11.2% of General Fund spending in FY2025, below the national median of 13.5% .
The median provides a comparison, not a recommended minimum.
However, New Hampshire’s General Fund also backs state education adequacy payments whenever the Education Trust Fund (ETF) falls short.
Using the state’s statutory revenue schedules, our $225.2 million reserve equaled 8.2% of combined FY2025 General Fund and ETF revenues , excluding the Statewide Education Property Tax, or SWEPT, which is retained locally to support schools.
These metrics serve distinct purposes: The 11.2% figure benchmarks the Rainy Day Fund against General Fund spending.
The 8.2% figure compares the reserve with the broader revenue base supporting both funds, excluding SWEPT.
Further, Pew recommends tailoring reserve targets to each state’s revenue volatility, spending pressures, and financial flexibility.
The question is whether New Hampshire’s cushion matches New Hampshire’s specific risks.
That cushion has already grown smaller.
State audited accounts show a $67.3 million General Fund deficit at the close of the FY2024–2025 biennium.
A transfer from the Rainy Day Fund covered that shortfall, reducing its balance from $292.5 million to $225.2 million as of June 30, 2025—a 23% decline in a single year , while the economy was relatively strong.
Although that single drawdown does not establish an exact reserve threshold, it clearly demonstrates that our existing buffers are insufficient for a severe economic downturn.
Stress-Testing the System To evaluate this exposure, I modeled recession scenarios affecting New Hampshire’s principal revenue sources, including business taxes, real estate transfer taxes, and Meals and Rooms taxes.
The analysis also considered increased demand for public services and underlying budget pressures.
In the central deep-recession scenario , which assumes a recession begins January 1, 2027: FY2028: Revenues fall approximately $436 million below the no-recession baseline .
Recession-related costs and an existing modeled baseline imbalance bring the total funding gap to about $576 million .
FY2029: Revenues remain approximately $350 million below baseline , with a total modeled funding gap of approximately $462 million .
These are scenario estimates, not predictions or official state forecasts.
The funding gaps include revenue losses, additional recession-related spending, and an illustrative baseline imbalance.
They are measured before reserve use, corrective policy decisions, or additional federal relief.
The timing matters.
The hypothetical recession begins halfway through FY2027.
Its largest modeled fiscal impact arrives in FY2028, as weaker economic activity works its way through tax collections.
For scale, a $225.2 million Rainy Day Fund would be less than two-fifths of the modeled FY2028 funding gap, even if the entire reserve were still available then.
Another substantial gap follows in FY2029.
A Rainy Day Fund does not have to replace every dollar lost during a multiyear recession to be useful.
It buys time to adjust.
But the Rainy Day Fund alone is not nearly large enough to preserve the modeled service levels through a downturn of this scale.
The connection to public education is direct.
Under current law, 39% of revenue from both the Business Profits Tax and the Business Enterprise Tax is allocated to the Education Trust Fund .
A drop in business-tax collections does not automatically reduce state adequacy grants.
State law requires those payments to continue and requires the General Fund to cover any resulting ETF deficit.
That legal requirement protects adequacy payments, but it creates no new revenue.
When business-tax collections fall, both funds lose revenue while the General Fund remains responsible for backing those payments.
The pressure spreads across the state budget.
How State Revenue Losses Reach Local Communities Government should always pursue savings from waste, fraud, and abuse.
But a credible recession plan cannot assume hundreds of millions of dollars in savings without identifying where they would come from and demonstrating that they can be achieved .
Without sufficient reserves, other available balances, additional revenue, or federal assistance, closing a gap this large would likely require spending changes that significantly affect core state services—including Health and Human Services and education.
Those choices could also put pressure on state support for local communities.
During and after the Great Recession, Concord suspended traditional municipal revenue sharing and repeatedly froze growth in Meals and Rooms tax distributions by suspending the distribution formula.
Cities and towns received less support than they otherwise would have for services they still needed to provide.
Some of the funding streams Concord cut in earlier downturns are already gone: Retirement contributions: The state eliminated its recurring contribution toward local teacher, police, and firefighter employer pension costs in 2011.
Subsequent assistance has included a one-time reimbursement enacted in 2022 and funding for certain first-responder pension benefit changes, but the former recurring cost-sharing arrangement has not been restored.
Revenue sharing: The state formally repealed traditional municipal revenue sharing, suspended beginning in 2010, in the 2025 state budget.
That funding is already gone; lawmakers cannot cut it again to close a new shortfall.
Other aid remains, including the municipal share of Meals and Rooms tax revenue.
If lawmakers reduce state support or shift additional costs to communities, local leaders face familiar choices: raise property taxes, cut services, defer infrastructure maintenance, or draw down local reserves.
Fiscal pressure does not disappear when it leaves Concord.
It reaches local taxpayers and the services they depend on.
The True Test of the “New Hampshire Advantage” The success of New Hampshire’s tax model should be judged across the full economic cycle—including whether it can weather bad years without placing unreasonable burdens on local communities.
Pew recommends using stress tests to inform reserve targets and adopting rules to save a portion of unusually strong revenues.
New Hampshire needs an explicit strategy for protecting essential services and local communities when revenues weaken.
That strategy should explain how much protection reserves are intended to provide, what other resources would be available, and what actions would follow if the downturn outlasted the cushion.
That task becomes more urgent with HB 1300 .
The law requires local votes on school property-tax levy caps and related school administrative unit (SAU) central-office spending limits.
Adoption requires three-fifths approval; caps approved in November 2026 take effect in FY2028 budgets.
Caps are not automatically imposed everywhere, and override procedures remain available.
But districts that adopt a levy cap would face an additional hurdle if replacing lost state aid required local taxes to exceed that cap.
Current law protects adequacy payments.
A recession would not automatically cancel that protection.
But lawmakers’ choices about other school aid, future funding formulas, and state-to-local cost sharing would still matter.
If Concord responds to a major shortfall by reducing school support or shifting costs—and a district cannot secure the override needed to replace that funding—what happens to its students and services?
That’s where we go next.
