First, What’s the big deal about CASH?
Cash preserves our freedom.
Cash ensures no one can track or control our purchases.
It keeps our location anonymous.
It helps us budget because we only spend what we have.
Furthermore, consider this: If I give a $20 tip to a waiter at a local restaurant, and she hands that same $20 to the hairdresser for a hair cut, and the hairdresser gives it to a babysitter, and the babysitter uses it to buy a pizza, that same $20 bill keeps moving through the community at full value.
No one skims anything off the top [1].
But in the digital system, that cut happens again and again, and the effect compounds.
At a 3.5 percent fee, after one transaction, that $20 becomes $19.30.
After two, $18.62.
After three, $17.97.
After four, $17.34.
After five digital transactions, only $16.74 remains in circulation.
More than $3 of the original $20 has quietly disappeared in just a handful of everyday exchanges.
That money didn’t go to the waiter, the hairdresser, the babysitter, or the pizza shop.
It left our community entirely to big, out-of-state corporations.
It’s a quiet drain on small communities, a squeeze we barely see because it’s spread out, invisible, and normalized [1].
Digital Money = Digital Control
But the real reason that I am so passionate about cash is because of the digital control grid – the control grid that Manuel and I helped build unknowingly during our time as high tech engineers in Silicon Valley.
If all of our transactions are digital, our accounts can be centrally frozen if we do not behave in the way the controllers want.
Remember the Canadian truckers and their supporters who had their bank accounts frozen?
Remember the de-banked Medical Doctors who expressed their opinions in the fallout of COVID?
The elimination of cash is a great way to control our actions without any government involved – this is taxation without representation.
Cash at School Events
The elimination of cash payments at school events in the fall of 2024 prompted our family to stand up.
We did not move to South Dakota to be digitally controlled by out-of-state elites.
We went to school board meetings, wrote letters to the editor, and stood outside our kids’ events with other families.
Although Brandon Valley accepted cash, Sioux Falls and other districts didn’t.
Brandon Valley families could not pay with cash at some away games.
Finally, we found one of our good legislators willing to bring accept cash legislation.
We supported the legislation with emails, phone calls, statistics, and testimony in Pierre.
Long story short and about 20 newspaper articles later, the accept cash at school events legislation passed by 1 vote and the grace of God.
