The Garden City Telegram
Several USD 457 staff members spoke against a proposed Electronic Media Policy at the USD 457 Board of Education meeting on Monday night.
The policy speaks to employees conduct on electronic/social media platforms.
Levi Burnfin, Garden City High School Speech teacher and President of the Garden City Education Association, said the vast majority of the policy makes sense, things should be professional on professional accounts, making sure that communication is professional for colleagues and students, but there is a piece that needs to be changed to avoid possible issues if unaltered.
Specifically, it's the piece speaking to employees being held to the same professional standards in the use of their personal electronic media accounts as they are for any other public conduct, Burnfin said.
If the employee's use of it interferes with their ability to perform their job, they could be subject to disciplinary action or termination of employment.
The policy and guidelines aim to protect students and maintain professionalism and to promote responsible behavior; those are important goals, Burnfin said.
But as it's currently written, a few areas can unintentionally create confusion, uneven enforcement or perception of bias.
"The policy says employees must meet professional standards and avoid anything that interferes with their job performance," he said.
"The problem is, those terms aren't defined."
Without examples of clear criteria, one administrator could see a post as harmless, while another could view it as unprofessional, Burnfin said.
This could be seen in situations involving politics and values, which can influence how social media posts are interpreted.
"An employee who shares views that don't match the community's majority opinion could face more scrutiny than someone expressing the opposite view," he said.
"Even if decisions are made in good faith, the outcome can still appear politically biased."
If personal interpretations shape how the policy is enforced, it could lead to unfair or uneven discipline based on opinion rather than actual behavior, Burnfin said.
"The policy tells employees to assume their social media use is public; staff may choose to avoid posting anything remotely controversial, even outside of work," he said.
"That kind of self-censorship can discourage healthy participation in community or civic discussions, which isn't good for our profession built on critical thinking and open dialogue."
Becca Burnfin, GCHS Social Studies teacher, said the policy addition regarding personal accounts was previously raised at a GCHS staff meeting.
At the meeting she heard Interim Superintendent Josh Guymon say, when asked about cases in neighboring districts where staff was let go for making political comment on their personal pages, that "there is no place for that here."
"While I respect the intent to uphold professionalism, I believe the creation of this policy excludes the voices of those it directly impacts, which in my opinion, violates the Board Policy GAC, where it states the Board "shall, to the fullest extent practicable, involve the employees of the district,"" she said.
The proposed policy contradicts the standards they set for students when it comes to teaching them to become informed, thoughtful and engaged and to recognize bias of a point of view, recognize their own values and to be respectful of others, Becca Burnfin said.
"If we discourage responsible expression of civic engagement, we fail to model the skills and values we ask of our students to develop," she said.
"I must speak up because I cannot in good conscious ask students to exercise their voice and civic responsibility if educators are prohibited to do the same."
Russ Tidwell, GCHS Speech teacher and Debate and Forensics coach, said he doesn't feel comfortable being in a world where because he chose to be an educator, he must give up a "good chunk" of his civic right to express himself.
Specifically, he believes that he should still have the right to advocate for a political candidate and to be opposed publicly to a political candidate – not in the classroom, but in his private life with social media and advocacy in public that's not on the district's time.
"I have a right as an American to Freedom of Expression," he said.
"Yes, that right carries consequences, yes, I'm respectful of that, yes, I believe it comes with responsibilities, but I don't think I should give it all up."
Additionally, Tidwell said he's passionate about this because he's seen three of his colleagues and two or three other teachers in the last four weeks either be put on leave and then put back in their jobs or outright forced to resign or been fired because of things that happened in their private social media.
"I don't defend all of the things any of those people said, I would not have said some of the things some of those people said, but the First Amendment means that I'm going to defend their right to say those things in private or to their sphere of influence outside of their job," he said.
Tidwell also expressed that the policy as written is incredibly vague, and it's vague on purpose to the district's benefit and the employees' detriment.
A policy is needed, Tidwell said, but there needs to be clear cut lines.
The Board did not approve the policy; they gave direction for staff to investigate changes.
Board member Robin Bergkamp, said she agrees that the personal use aspect of the policy is a little vague.
"I don't know how you tweak it to make it better, but I think it's going to be really difficult to impose," she said.
Board member Andy Fahrmeir said to him it doesn't read as vague, but it does seem to refer to the district policy around public conduct.
"I read this as it is conduct done online is being interpreted as being public comment," he said.
Drew Thon, Interim Deputy Superintendent, said they don't have a policy specifically labeled as public conduct, but they have a policy on ethics and behavior.
"But I'm with you … It would tie back to that," he said.
Bergkamp agrees that it sounds like it should tie back to other policies already in place.
"If you don't support the same candidate that I support, you have every right to, on your personal account, say whatever you want to say in support of that candidate," she said.
"Obviously, there are things that we don't want teachers saying in public … (but) I don't love how that part reads.
Board member Randy Ralston said he would like the policy to be more detailed, because he agrees that employees should be able to utilize their First Amendment Right to free speech.
"To me, when it gets into the gray areas is when we start condoning violence and things like that," he said.
"I would like more specific (language), I know you can't say everything, but I would like more specifics."
