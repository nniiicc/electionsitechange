Term Limits & Real Accountability
Why I believe this — and how I’ll fight for it.
Congress was never supposed to be a career.
The founders pictured something different: you’d make your life as a farmer, a nurse, a builder, a small-business owner, serve your neighbors for a time, then come home to live under the laws you passed.
George Washington set the example — he had the power to stay, and chose to go home.
Service, then back to your life.
Somewhere along the way, we lost it.
Today people go to Washington and never leave — and the longer they stay, the more they change.
Your world stops being your hometown and becomes Washington, and Washington changes people.
You get comfortable, you drift from the people you left behind, and you grow dependent on the industry lobbyists, special interests, and foreign money that fund your campaigns and keep you in your seat.
The longer you’re there, the more you’ll do to stay.
That’s why I believe in term limits.
Go in knowing you have a set amount of time, and you spend it on results instead of protecting your seat.
I’ve already taken a 12-year pledge.
If you can’t be effective in 12 years, it’s time to go anyway.
I’m not here to build a career in Congress.
I’m here because this moment called for it: to set this district up to win, and then hand it back to the people — not to a successor I pick.
Here’s what I’ll do — and why
Put a 12-year limit on Congress — and I’ve already limited myself.
Public service shouldn’t be a 28-year career. 12 years is enough to learn the job and get real things done, without becoming a fixture who answers to the system instead of the people.
The honest part most won’t tell you: universal term limits take a constitutional amendment.
The Supreme Court ruled in 1995 that nothing less works, so I’ll co-sponsor and fight for one.
But I won’t wait for Washington to limit itself: I’ve pledged to hold myself to 12 years, and when my time’s up I’ll hand this seat back to the people, not to a successor I pick.
Call on both parties to clean their own house — no amendment required.
Here’s what Washington won’t admit: the parties don’t have to wait for an amendment.
Each one can set its own rules and make a term limit a condition of its endorsement and its money — and stop propping up members who’ve overstayed their moment.
Term limits are one of the most popular ideas in the country, with every kind of voter.
The party that leads on this, that trusts the people instead of protecting its incumbents, will win back trust, energy, and elections.
I hold myself to 12 years.
Both parties should too.
Put conflicts of interest in the daylight — in real time.
Right now a member files a financial snapshot once a year and reports trades weeks later, so by the time you learn where your representative’s money is, the vote’s over.
By then it’s just paperwork.
I’d require live, public disclosure: a searchable record of where a member’s money, income, and interests sit, updated as it changes.
Paired with a ban on trading individual stocks in office, it comes down to one thing — you can see, the moment it happens, whether the person writing the laws stands to get richer from them.
Corporate welfare gets an expiration date.
Every corporate subsidy, every tax carve-out, and every major federal contract gets a fixed expiration date and has to prove it still works before it is renewed — real goals, real numbers, an independent audit of where the money went.
Pass, and it continues.
Fail, or finish the job it was created to do, and it expires.
That reaches what Washington never touches: the defense contracts that overrun by billions, the farm payments targeted at corporate farms instead of family farms, the tax carve-outs that cost the Treasury more than most agencies spend.
None of it has ever faced a review in its life.
A hatchet is not a review.
Cutting across the board tells you nothing about whether something works.
It moves the pain onto the people with the least room to absorb it and leaves the waste exactly where it sat.

Give the ethics rules real teeth.
Today’s rules are guidelines with no enforcement.
Congress polices itself, and the office meant to investigate wrongdoing can only suggest, never act.
So members steer deals to companies they own or that fund their campaigns, get rich off the public payroll, then leave to lobby for the same interests — and nothing happens.
I’d give the rules teeth: independent enforcement that can actually investigate and penalize, hard bans on self-dealing, and real consequences when someone crosses the line.
We should hold the people who write the nation’s laws to the highest standard, not the lowest.
The bottom line
Term limits, real disclosure, programs that earn their keep, ethics with teeth — it all comes down to one idea: public service is a duty, not a career.
Picture a Congress full of people who came to solve something and then go home to live under the laws they passed.
A government that feels like ours again.
That’s not a fantasy; it’s how this country was designed to work, and we can take it back.
Show up, tell the truth, deliver, and go home.
Do that, and we don’t just fix Washington — we leave our kids a democracy stronger than the one we were handed.
Our own congressman has been in Washington for nearly 30 years.
It’s fair to ask what three decades bought this district.
This is bigger than any one man: a system that rewards holding on over getting things done.
Term limits end that game.
