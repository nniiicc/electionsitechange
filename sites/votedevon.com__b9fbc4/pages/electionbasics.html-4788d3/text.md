Remember, if you vote straight ticket, you will miss important races that impact us locally here in District 29.
The general order is: Public questions 1–3 Federal, state, and some local positions School board and township board Judicial retention questions What you’ll see first: Public questions Questions 1–3 Public questions include a state constitutional amendment and any local questions placed on your ballot.
Open each question for a plain-English explanation.
Public question 1: Bail Under our current constitution, everyone has a right to bail except those accused of murder or treason.
This question asks whether to expand a judge’s authority to deny bail in other types of cases.
Judges can currently set bail very high, effectively limiting a person’s ability to meet bail requirements.
This question asks whether to expand a judge’s authority to hold someone without bail.
Advocates of voting yes say it will make the community safer by ensuring dangerous people can’t come back into the community.
Advocates of voting no warn that judges could abuse this provision for a number of reasons.
Public question 2: City and town court judges and where they may reside Our constitution is currently written so that town and city officials must live in the town or city where they preside.
This question asks whether to pass an amendment allowing city or town court judges to live in the closest bordering county.
Advocates of voting yes say this will help fill needs in cities and towns, especially in more rural parts of Indiana where lawyers are scarce.
Advocates of voting no worry judges won’t know the community well enough to judge people accordingly.
Public question 3: Local school referendum This question asks whether to renew the current operating referendum for local schools.
The question language is set by the legislature and requires schools to include the highest possible rate and calculate the increase as if the community were starting at $0.
For Noblesville Schools, taxpayers will see a question that says their property taxes would increase by $955.
For the average homeowner who currently pays about $650 annually for local schools, the increase is closer to $12 annually.
Learn more from Noblesville Schools , Hamilton Southeastern Schools , and Devon’s statement .

4 Step four Avoid these common mix-ups 1 School board races are partisan for the first time A 2025 state law made school board races partisan starting this November.
Candidates may list a party, run as independent, or choose not to disclose an affiliation.
There are no primaries—they qualify by petition and go straight to November.
A straight-party vote does not count for school board, so mark it on its own.
2 A straight-party vote doesn’t cover the whole ballot If you vote straight ticket, any race where your party has no candidate gets no vote.
You can cast a vote in those races yourself.
You can also change your vote in any single race after choosing the straight-ticket option.
