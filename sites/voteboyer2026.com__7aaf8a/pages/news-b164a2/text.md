Dr.
Arthur Boyer launches District 82 bid, calls Immokalee “second home”
Candidate highlights affordability, families’ rights, and Everglades restoration in community-focused message
In a campaign message aimed directly at Immokalee residents, longtime educator and community volunteer Dr.
Arthur Boyer, EdD announced he is running for the Florida House of Representatives in District 82, framing his candidacy around what he called the community’s “assets, difficulties, and the bridge to a great future.”
Dr.
Boyer described Immokalee as his “second home” for 34 years, saying he has worked as an educator in the area for about 20 years and has spent roughly 30 years in community service.
That service, he said, has included adult education programs and radio broadcasting designed to “inform, connect, and uplift our neighbors.” “Immokalee is the heart of the agricultural workforce and a vibrant, multicultural community,” Boyer said, describing the town as a place rooted in “faith, family, and resilience.”
A bid centered on “effective and compassionate” leadership
Boyer said his decision followed “reflection, prayer, and conversations across our community.” He told residents District 82 needs leadership that “listens, builds bridges, and delivers practical results for everyday people.” Rather than focusing only on campaign rhetoric, Boyer organized his message into three themes: what Immokalee contributes, what families are facing, and what he presented as a plan to move forward.
“Immokalee helps feed Florida and America”
Boyer began by emphasizing the community’s role in agriculture and the state’s broader economy.
“Immokalee helps feed Florida and America,” he said, pointing to farmworkers as the backbone of an economy that “depends on their strength.” He also highlighted local small businesses, churches, and community organizations as stabilizing forces for families, and described the area’s diversity as “a strength and a source of pride.”
Affordability pressures and a climate of fear
Boyer then turned to what he called the pressures squeezing working families.
He cited rising housing costs, spiraling property insurance, and higher prices for groceries, gas, and basic necessities, saying those increases force “hard choices about what to postpone—or go without.”
But Boyer also singled out another burden he said many Immokalee families carry: fear—fear of being targeted, fear of family separation, fear of reporting exploitation, and fear of speaking up at work.
From there, he delivered one of the central lines of his message: “Immigration and workers’ rights protections are essential.”
Boyer argued that immigrant families are “the backbone of District 82’s agricultural economy and vibrant communities,” noting their roles in farm labor, small business, schools, and churches.
He called for policies he described as “fair” and “humane,” emphasizing dignity over fear and urging protections for due process.
He also stressed workplace fairness, adding: “If you work hard, you deserve to be paid fairly, treated with respect, and protected from exploitation and wage theft.”
A three-part agenda for District 82
Boyer said the “bridge to a great future” is a plan built around three priorities, which he described as interconnected.
- Priority One: Housing, cost of living, and affordability — Expand workforce and affordable housing, protect renters and homeowners, confront the cost drivers behind rising property insurance, and lower everyday expenses so families can keep more of what they earn.
- Priority Two: Protect families and rights — Strengthen public schools by investing in educators and ensuring every child has access to quality education.
Expand healthcare and mental health access, especially in rural communities.
Support community-based public safety that builds trust, and defend fundamental rights including due process, equal protection, and civil liberties.
- Priority Three: Protect water, land, and future — Advance Everglades restoration, protect water quality, and strengthen resilience against hurricanes and flooding.
Clean water and healthy ecosystems support agriculture and the region’s way of life.
A “people-powered” campaign message.
Boyer closed by positioning his campaign as rooted in local organizing rather than big donors.
“This campaign is powered by neighbors—not special interests,” he said, asking residents to volunteer, host conversations, spread the word, and contribute financially if they are able.
He also framed the election as a broader test of civic participation: “Florida’s future should not be decided by who has the loudest voice or the biggest donors, but by neighbors standing up for one another,” he said.
Boyer believes in a vision of District 82 where “hard work is rewarded,” schools are strong, healthcare is accessible, immigrant neighbors are valued, water is clean, and government “works for the people it serves.”

Community Gathering Set for 4–5 p.m. on U.S. 41 Outside “Alligator Alcatraz,” With Local Advocate Scheduled to Speak
District 82, Fla.
(Sunday, Jan. 4, 2026) — Community members and advocates will gather today from 4 to 5 p.m. along U.S. 41 (Tamiami Trail) in front of the Everglades-area immigration detention facility widely known as “Alligator Alcatraz” to draw attention to the impact immigration enforcement and parental deportations can have on children and the stability of fragile communities.
I am scheduled to deliver remarks at the event.
In my speech, I plan to focus on the ripple effects that family separation can create—children experiencing fear and uncertainty, disruptions in school routines, sudden housing and financial instability, and increased need for trauma-informed support.
The facility is located at the Dade-Collier Training and Transition Airport in the Big Cypress/Everglades region near Ochopee, directly along U.S. 41, and has remained in the public spotlight since opening in July 2025.
Emphasis on children and community responsibility
Organizers say the purpose of the gathering is to encourage the public to look beyond headlines and consider what happens to children when a parent is detained or deported.
In prepared remarks, I call on faith leaders, school officials, nonprofit partners, and civic leaders to strengthen a coordinated safety net for affected children—support that includes mentoring and tutoring, counseling referrals, emergency food and housing help, and trusted points of contact for families seeking assistance.
While immigration policy debates often center on adults, speakers at the gathering are expected to emphasize that children frequently bear the most immediate consequences: missed school days, emotional distress, and instability that can affect academic progress and well-being.
A high-profile and controversial site
“Alligator Alcatraz” has drawn statewide and national attention, including scrutiny and legal disputes related to environmental review and broader concerns raised by advocates.
Supporters have described the facility as a deterrent and a tool to expand detention capacity.
From concern to action
Event participants say the gathering is intended not only to raise awareness but also to mobilize community support for children and caregivers who may face sudden decisions about housing, childcare, transportation, and school continuity after a family disruption.
In my remarks, I am expected to call for practical action: building local “care circles,” expanding trusted referral networks for reputable services, and ensuring children are protected from bullying and stigma.
I also plan to encourage community leaders to coordinate efforts so families are not left to navigate crises alone.

January 1st, Haytian Independence Day
On this Haytian Independence Day, we honor a people who transformed suffering into sovereignty and proved that freedom is not a gift—it is a right worth defending.
We remember the women and men, named and unnamed, whose courage carried a nation from bondage to self-determination.
Their sacrifice calls us to unity, dignity, and shared responsibility.
On January 1, 1804, in Gonaïves, Jean-Jacques Dessalines proclaimed Hayti’s independence.
That declaration crowned the Haytian Revolution, a struggle that began in 1791 in Saint-Domingue and ended in 1804.
Enslaved Africans and their allies rose against a brutal plantation system, endured immense loss, and organized a movement that changed the Atlantic world.
Hayti became the first modern Black republic and the only nation born from a successful slave revolution, reshaping the global conversation about equality, citizenship, and human rights.
The road to independence was marked by disciplined leadership and collective determination.
Toussaint Louverture helped shape the revolutionary movement and its international impact, while countless community leaders, soldiers, and families carried the daily burden of resistance.
In the final phase, Haytians secured a decisive victory at the Battle of Vertières on November 18, 1803—an enduring symbol of resolve and a turning point that cleared the path to the declaration that followed.
Independence, however, did not end the test.
The young nation faced isolation and economic punishment for daring to be free, and later endured heavy financial obligations that constrained development.
Yet through every obstacle, Haytians kept building—through farming, learning, worship, entrepreneurship, and community care—proving again and again that resilience is part of the national character.
Across the diaspora, Haytian communities carry that legacy forward by working, serving, raising families, and keeping culture alive across generations.
Today, we celebrate more than a date.
We celebrate a living legacy: the Haytian flag, the Creole language, the faith and music, the art and stories, and the strength that shines across Hayti and throughout the world.
Independence is also a daily practice—choosing integrity over corruption, cooperation over division, and service over selfishness.
May we teach our children the full story of 1804 and inspire them to build a future worthy of that victory.
May this day renew our commitment to honor freedom in practical ways—by protecting families, uplifting young people, investing in education, respecting honest work, and standing for justice wherever Haytians live.
Let us carry forward the best of our history with humility, courage, and love for one another.

Educator and community servant launches a people-powered campaign focused on affordability, strong schools, healthcare access, and protecting our water and future.

Arthur Boyer, EdD today announced his candidacy for the Florida House of Representatives in District 82 as a Democrat, following reflection, prayer, and conversations with neighbors across the community.
District 82 includes Immokalee, Harlem, LaBelle, Clewiston, Ave Maria, Big Cypress Seminole Indian Reservation, Everglades City, Chokoloskee — communities Dr.
Boyer describes as hardworking, resilient, and deserving of leadership that listens and delivers practical results.
“Too many families in District 82 are being squeezed and feel unheard.
Housing costs keep rising.
Property insurance is spiraling.
Groceries, gas, and basic necessities cost more each month, and inflation has made it harder for working families and seniors to keep up.
Teachers, first responders, farmworkers, and young families are being priced out of the communities they serve.
It’s time for a change.
We can do better — and we can do it together,” Boyer said.
Dr.
Boyer has served the region for decades and says District 82 has shaped his life’s work.
Immokalee has been his second home for 34 years.
He has worked as an educator in the area for more than 20 years, and his community service spans about 30 years, including adult education programs, radio broadcasting, and other local initiatives.
Before becoming an educator, Boyer worked as a farmworker while attending adult education classes as an English as a Second Language (ESL) student in Immokalee — experiences he says keep him grounded in the realities facing working families and newcomers seeking opportunity.
He is running to bring that same service-driven leadership to Tallahassee.
“Our district deserves leadership that is effective and compassionate — leadership that listens, builds bridges, and delivers practical results for everyday people,” Boyer said.
