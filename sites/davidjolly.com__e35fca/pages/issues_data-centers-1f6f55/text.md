Frequently asked questions
Q.
What is a hyperscale data center?
Hyperscale generally refers to the largest class of data centers, and in Florida the working line is drawn at electrical demand.
State law defines a large-scale data center and a large load customer as a single location with an anticipated monthly peak load of 50 megawatts or more, calculated as the highest average load over a 15 minute interval, including colocated tenants at the same site.
WPLG Local 10 reported in July 2026 that the many data centers already operating in Miami-Dade County are not hyperscale because none requires 50 megawatts or more.
Project Tango was described by its project manager as 600 megawatts across five buildings.
The Florida sales tax exemption in section 212.08(5)(r) uses a different and higher threshold, requiring a critical IT load of 100 megawatts or higher plus a cumulative capital investment of at least $150 million.
Q.
Are data centers coming to Florida?
Some already exist, and more are proposed.
WPLG Local 10 reported in July 2026 that dozens of data centers already operate in Miami-Dade County, but none are hyperscale facilities requiring 50 megawatts or more.
Project Tango in Palm Beach County would have been the first hyperscale data center in the state if approved, and it was denied without prejudice on July 15, 2026.
Other proposals have moved and stalled across the state.
WPTV reported that projects in Indiantown, Okeechobee County and Fort Pierce were withdrawn after public opposition, while others remain under consideration on the Treasure Coast.
Lakeland published a proposed 12 month moratorium ordinance in June 2026 and noted the city currently has no large load customers and does not permit data centers under its land use rules.
Duke Energy Florida told regulators in September 2025 that it had no large load data center customers.
Q.
Did Palm Beach County approve or reject Project Tango?
Palm Beach County commissioners rejected it.
On July 15, 2026, after a hearing that ran more than 12 hours and drew more than 80 signed up public speakers, the commission voted 5 to 1 to deny the application without prejudice.
Commissioner Maria G.
Marino cast the only vote against denial.
Mayor Sara Baxter recused herself because the county attorney advised that her earlier public statements opposing the project created a conflict in a quasi judicial proceeding.
Denial without prejudice means the developer may revise the proposal and submit it again.
Commissioners said the applicant did not adequately answer questions about noise, water use, traffic and compatibility with the surrounding area.
The property still holds its previously approved entitlements for more than 2 million square feet of warehouse and data center development, which the vote did not change.
Q.
Can Florida cities and counties ban or pause data centers?
Local governments retain land use authority.
Section 163.326, Florida Statutes, created by CS/CS/SB 484 in 2026, states that local governments maintain the powers and responsibilities for comprehensive planning and land development regulation with respect to large load customers, and that a large load customer may not be treated as an electric substation, which would have triggered a streamlined approval process.
Local governments have used that authority.
Palm Beach County adopted a moratorium on AI data centers in early July 2026, though it did not apply to the pending Project Tango application.
Lakeland published a proposed ordinance in June 2026 for a temporary 12 month moratorium on applications for data centers and large load customers using more than 50 megawatts a month, with a public hearing set for July 6, 2026, while the city studies land use classifications, development standards and utility impacts.
Q.
Will data centers raise my power bill in Florida?
Florida law passed in 2026 is designed to prevent that.
CS/CS/SB 484 creates section 366.043, Florida Statutes, which requires public utility tariffs to reasonably ensure that each large load customer bears its own full cost of service, including connection, incremental transmission, incremental generation and other infrastructure costs, and that those costs are not shifted to the general body of ratepayers.
It also says the risk of nonpayment may not be borne by other ratepayers.
Every public utility had to file a compliant tariff with the Florida Public Service Commission by October 1, 2026.
The commission may approve tools such as minimum demand charges, financial guarantees, minimum load factors and take or pay provisions.
Whether those protections work in practice will depend on the tariffs the commission approves and on how utilities apply them, which is why the same bill ordered an independent study of rate impacts due July 1, 2027.
