Frequently asked questions
Q.
Why isn't Medicaid expansion enough on its own?
Because expansion provides coverage, but people still need somewhere to actually get care.
Jolly proposes a 5-year pilot to dramatically expand community health centers in every Florida county so new enrollees can connect with primary care providers, not just have coverage on paper.
Q.
How long would this plan take to actually help me?
Medicaid expansion can start within months once the Legislature approves it, with coverage beginning in the first year.
Scaling community health centers takes longer, which is why Jolly proposes a 5-year build to create durable access alongside the immediate coverage.
Q.
Does this plan raise my taxes as a Florida resident?
No.
The plan uses Florida tax dollars already being collected, paired with a 9-to-1 federal match.
The state share is offset by reduced emergency care costs, lower uncompensated care payments, and increased tax revenue from a stronger healthcare workforce.
Q.
How would this plan affect rural Floridians?
Rural counties have been hit hardest by healthcare gaps.
Jolly's plan scales community health centers in every county, including rural areas where private practices are unviable, and stabilizes rural hospital revenue by reducing uncompensated care through Medicaid expansion.
Q.
What if I am a small business owner in Florida?
Small businesses struggle to offer coverage because they cannot get the group rates large employers negotiate.
Medicaid expansion would let your lowest-paid workers have a coverage option instead of being stuck in the coverage gap, and it stabilizes the market where you and your employees buy insurance.
Q.
How does Florida rank for healthcare nationally?
Poorly across most measures.
Florida has one of the highest uninsured rates in the country at 15.5% for working-age adults, ranks among the worst states for maternal mortality, and has about 266,000 adults shut out of both Medicaid and marketplace subsidies.
Q.
What if the federal government cuts the 90% match?
The 90% match is set in statute, not annual appropriation, which means it cannot be changed without an act of Congress.
Any change would also disrupt 41 states' Medicaid programs, including red states like Indiana and Ohio, which makes a partisan cut politically difficult.
Florida could adopt trigger provisions that would automatically roll back expansion if the federal match drops below a certain level.
Q.
How would this plan affect Florida hospitals?
Florida hospitals would see two direct benefits.
First, Medicaid expansion would shift much of the uncompensated care burden to the federal government at the 90% match rate.
Second, scaling community health centers reduces preventable ER visits by triaging patients in primary care.
