Frequently asked questions
Q.
Is my insurance crisis related to climate?
Yes, structurally.
Florida's property insurance crisis is driven by hurricane risk, litigation costs, and reinsurance prices, and the hurricane risk component has grown with warming ocean temperatures and rising sea levels.
Insurers price policies to absorb rising expected losses, so climate is one of the underlying drivers of the insurance crisis.
Q.
Is climate change real in Florida?
Yes, and Florida faces some of the most direct climate impacts in the country.
Florida's Gulf Coast is projected for 22 to 26 inches of sea level rise by 2050 (about double the U.S. average), and hurricane intensity is rising.
The 2024 hurricane season caused over $100 billion in combined damage across the Southeast.
Q.
What does David Jolly want to do about clean energy?
Jolly's position is to unleash clean and renewable energies across Florida, requiring greater integration of clean energy technologies into public utilities.
This means stronger net metering rules to support rooftop solar and accelerated utility-scale renewable buildout.
Florida is projected to receive $62.7 billion in IRA-funded clean power investment by 2030.
Q.
Aren't climate policies just going to hurt the Florida economy?
The economic case runs the other way.
Florida's tourism economy ($134 billion in visitor spending, 1.8 million jobs), ocean economy, and saltwater fishing industry ($9.2 billion in annual impact) all depend on a healthy environment.
Climate policy in Florida protects the state's largest industries rather than imposing costs against them.
Q.
What about Floridians who live in coastal communities?
Coastal Floridians face the most direct climate exposure: rising sea levels, more intense hurricanes, and accelerating beach erosion.
David Jolly's plan calls for wiser coastal development practices and sustained investment in resiliency, which means living shorelines and hybrid systems, seawalls where necessary and appropriate, raised infrastructure, beach renourishment where cost-effective and sustainable, stormwater drainage upgrades, and building code modernization in coastal counties.
Q.
How would David Jolly handle Florida's water quality crises?
Jolly's plan ties water quality to broader environmental investment.
Blue-green algae crises are addressed by reducing discharges of nutrient pollution to our coastal waters, and springs degradation is addressed through septic to sewer conversions, agricultural runoff rules, and groundwater protection.
Protecting Florida's coast requires changing what flows into it.
Q.
What about FPL and renewable energy under David Jolly?
Jolly's clean energy position would require greater integration of renewables into Florida's utility planning.
That likely means stronger net metering rules to support rooftop solar and accelerated utility-scale renewable buildout, pushing FPL and other utilities to expand renewable generation.
Q.
Why oppose offshore drilling if it could create Florida jobs?
Offshore drilling creates very few Florida jobs because rigs are based out of Louisiana and Texas ports.
What Florida loses in a spill (tourism revenue, fishing industry, real estate value, ecological damage) runs to tens of billions of dollars.
Florida's tourism economy ($130 billion) and ocean economy ($30 billion) depend on clean beaches and healthy water.
