He sponsored HB 101 to raise Alaska’s age of consent from 16 to 18, close dangerous loopholes, and better protect 16- and 17-year-olds from exploitation and abuse.
The legislation preserved close-in-age protections so peer relationships are not criminalized, while giving prosecutors stronger tools to hold predators accountable.
The age-of-consent reform later moved forward as part of a broader public safety package.
HB 101 was designed to address a serious gap in Alaska law.
When 16 and 17-year-olds can be treated as legally able to consent to sexual activity with adults, predators can exploit that loophole, and survivors can face additional burdens in court.
Andrew’s bill raised the age of consent while preserving close-in-age protections, so consensual peer relationships would not be criminalized.
The goal was clear: protect minors from adults who groom, manipulate, or exploit them.
The bill drew support from people and organizations on the front lines of public safety, youth services, and survivor advocacy.
Charisse Millett, Executive Director of Alaska’s Public Safety Employees Association wrote in strong support of HB 101 .
PSEA warned that Alaska’s age-of-consent law created a dangerous loophole that put teenagers at risk and complicated prosecution of those who prey on vulnerable youth.
Millett said HB 101 would help close that gap, strengthen protections for young people, and ensure survivors are not forced to carry the difficult burden of proving lack of consent in court.
MY House, a Wasilla youth center serving at-risk young peopl from across Alaska, also supported the bill.
MY House explained that many of the youth it serves are survivors of abuse, including underage clients who are especially vulnerable to predators.
The organization wrote that HB 101 would help provide justice for minors who are survivors of sexual assault, prevent future victimization, and stop perpetrators from claiming “consensual relations” with 16- and 17-year-olds.
Research submitted with the bill reinforced why this mattered.
A study published in Child Abuse & Neglect found that wider age gaps in adolescent relationships were associated with higher sexual risk, lower use of protection against pregnancy and STIs, more emotional and physical victimization, and higher odds of unwanted sexual behavior.
The research also supported legal approaches that consider partner age gaps rather than relying only on a single age-of-consent number Andrew moved HB 101 forward with broad support.
The stand-alone bill passed the Alaska House with 39 yes votes and no votes against it before being sent to the Senate.
When the final path forward came through a broader public safety package, Andrew kept pushing.
The age-of-consent reform he championed was included in House Bill 239 , a wide-ranging crime bill that raised Alaska’s age of sexual consent to 18.
Alaska Public Media reported that HB 239 passed nearly unanimously, 20–0 in the Senate and 39–1 in the House.
Andrew Gray fought to protect Alaska’s youth, support survivors, and hold predators accountable.
Andrew helped move one of Alaska’s most important child-protection reforms in decades across the finish line.
