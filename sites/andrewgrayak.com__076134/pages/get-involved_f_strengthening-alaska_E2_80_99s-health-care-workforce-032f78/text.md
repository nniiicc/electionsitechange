As a physician associate, Andrew has seen firsthand where the system is failing patients and providers.
That is why he sponsored House Bill 110, a practical workforce bill to help bring more qualified health care and behavioral health professionals to Alaska.
HB 110 started with a clear goal: increase the number of social workers in Alaska by allowing the state to join the Social Work Licensure Compact.
Alaska faces an urgent and growing need for qualified social workers, with demand projected to increase by #% over the next three years.
Social workers are essential to Alaska’s health care system.
They serve in mental health care, schools, hospitals, public assistance, child protection, emergency response, substance use treatment, family services, veterans’ care, and community-based support.
When Alaska does not have enough social workers, patients wait longer, families go without help, providers burn out, and rural communities are hit especially hard.
HB 110 addresses that problem by reducing unnecessary licensing barriers while maintaining professional standards.
The Social Work Licensure Compact is designed to increase public access to social work services, reduce duplicative requirements for providers who would otherwise need multiple state licenses, promote workforce mobility, support military families, and allow telehealth to expand access to care.
The bill earned support from health care, behavioral health, workforce, and business leaders who understand that Alaska’s provider shortage is already affecting patients and communities.
The Greater Fairbanks Chamber of Commerce supported the bill, calling access to a stable and qualified workforce one of the most pressing challenges facing Alaska’s economy.
The Chamber wrote that health care workforce shortages are especially acute in Interior Alaska and directly affect business recruitment, family stability, and community growth.
It supported compacts because they reduce administrative barriers and allow qualified professionals to provide care more quickly, including through telehealth.
The Alaska Psychological Association supported the broader compact approach as well.
In its testimony on health care licensing compacts, AK-PA explained that joining PSYPACT would improve continuity of care for Alaskans who travel, expand access to telepsychology services in rural communities, and help Alaska attract psychologists from other states.
AK-PA also pointed to research showing that therapy delivered through telehealth can produce outcomes comparable to in-person services.
The bill moved forward with strong support and HB 110 passed the Alaska House unanimously, 40–0.
The Senate passed the final version 13–7, and the House concurred with 39 yes votes and one absent.
HB 110 is another example of Andrew’s approach to leadership: identify the problem, listen to the people doing the work, remove unnecessary barriers, and deliver solutions that help Alaskans get the care they need.
