Page one The analysis Draft bill text Sources Page one The short version What's broken, what the bill does, and what it costs, in plain English.
If you read one thing, read this.
Every county in our district has sheriff's deputies and road crews on the county payroll.
State law treats them differently.
If at least 30 percent of the workers in a bargaining unit are police, firefighters, deputies or other public safety workers, their union can still bargain over health insurance, overtime, safety, seniority and grievances.
Everyone else, like teachers, road crews and public hospital nurses, can bargain over base wages and not much else.
If talks go to an arbitrator, the raise can't top 3 percent or inflation, whichever is lower.
Before every contract ends, the union has to win a new election where not voting counts as a no.
The Iowa Supreme Court upheld that law 4 to 3, though the majority wrote that it left many public employees with fewer rights than others "with arguably similar jobs." In 2017, lawmakers also told cities, counties and school boards they can't require a project labor agreement on a public construction project.
A PLA is a deal signed before work starts that sets pay, safety rules and a no-strike promise for the whole job.
Here's what I'd rather see.
The LABOR Act (Labor Agreements, Bargaining, and Organizing Rights) does four things.
The same rules as police and fire.
Every public employee union can bargain over insurance, overtime, safety, evaluations and grievances again, the way public safety unions do today.
Fair votes.
It ends the raise cap and the elections that count staying home as a no.
Workers can still vote a union out, the way they could until 2017.
Dues by choice.
Members can have dues taken out of their paycheck again, only if they ask in writing, and can stop it with thirty days’ notice.
Local choice on PLAs.
A city, county or school board can require a PLA on a public construction project if it decides in public, union and non-union contractors can both bid, and the job hires apprentices.
What doesn't change: strikes stay illegal.
Nobody has to join a union or pay dues.
Bargaining doesn't force anyone to agree to anything, and a contract the employer can't legally pay for isn't valid.
The bill spends no state money directly, though bargaining could cost some later.
The state figured House File 291 saved it about $24.5 million across all its funds, most of it from health insurance.
How much comes back gets settled at the table, so no one can price it today.
If it's right for the deputy, it's right for the plow driver.
When workers have a seat at the table, an apprentice in southern Iowa can count on fair wages, job safety, and a reliable path to a pension.

It stays off the Pay As You Go Act's scorecard, because the scorecard can only trim spending or a tax credit a bill creates, and this one creates neither.
Any added payroll cost would be covered in the budget like any other.

The math What it costs and who pays The bill appropriates nothing.
It changes the rules public employers and their workers bargain under, and what that costs depends on contracts nobody has negotiated yet.
What House File 291 saved.
In 2019 the Legislative Services Agency reported the Department of Management's estimate that the changes in House File 291 saved the state about $24.5 million across all state funds ( Issue Review ).
Most of that, $21.3 million, was the yearly effect of health insurance changes, and that figure also counts new plan designs, shifts in enrollment and the Regents moving off the state's plans, so not all of it came from bargaining.
The rest was $2.6 million from smaller step raises for workers under the AFSCME contract and $684,000 from job classes that lost overtime pay.
That is state government only.
We found no count of what cities, counties, schools and community colleges saved.
What this bill does with that.
It doesn't hand any of it back automatically.
Insurance, overtime and the rest go back on the bargaining table, where the employer can still say no: under section 20.9 as the bill writes it, the duty to bargain in good faith doesn't make either side agree.
An arbitrator still has to weigh the employer's ability to pay and the effect on services, and a contract or award that breaks a legal limit on the employer's budget isn't valid (section 20.17).
The bill also bars every arbitrator from counting the employer's ability to raise taxes, fees or charges, or find other revenue, to pay an award.
Today that bar covers only units where fewer than 30 percent of the workers are in public safety, so the bill extends it to police and fire.
So the cost falls somewhere between nothing and a share of what House File 291 saved, and we can't narrow it further.
When.
The bill takes effect July 1, 2027.
State contracts run two years and start in July of odd-numbered years (section 20.15).
The next one starts July 1, 2027, the day the bill takes effect, and it is bargained under today's law in the months before, on the timetable in section 20.17.
So the first state contract bargained under this bill would start July 1, 2029.
Local employers bargain on their own calendars, so some local contracts could change sooner.
The board's costs.
When House File 291 was filed, the Legislative Services Agency said there wasn't enough information to estimate its total fiscal impact ( fiscal note ).
The Public Employment Relations Board told the agency it needed $384,000 in the first year to carry the law out, including $179,000 for running the elections it required, paid back by fees charged to the unions on the ballot.
Ending retention elections should take most of that election work off the board's desk.
The board's duties under chapter 20 now belong to the employment appeal board (section 20.3).
We haven't estimated the savings, and the Legislative Services Agency would if the bill were filed.
Who pays.
Public employers pay whatever they agree to or an arbitrator awards, which means state and local taxpayers.
Union members pay their own dues, and only the ones who ask have them taken out of a paycheck.
On a public project with a PLA, any difference in cost lands on that project's budget, and only where a city, county or school board chose a PLA in public.
The state can't require a PLA under the bill.
Where a local project with a PLA also uses state grant money, part of any added cost could reach the state.
The scorecard.
The bill stays off the Pay As You Go Act's scorecard.
The scorecard can only trim spending or a tax credit a bill creates, and this bill creates neither, so there would be nothing on it to trim.
Any added payroll cost would be covered in the regular budget like any other.
When House File 291 was filed, the Legislative Services Agency couldn't estimate its total cost, and we'd expect the same trouble running it the other way.
The limits of our case What the data does not support We can't say House File 291 drove teachers or other public workers out of Iowa.
No study we found separates its effect from everything else going on in those years.
We don't know how many local bargaining units have lost their union in retention elections since 2017, or how many workers they covered.
The board publishes election results, but we haven't counted them, so this page doesn't claim a number.
At the state level, the elections didn't strip unions.
In the first round, in October 2018, every bargaining unit of executive branch and court employees that the Legislative Services Agency counted kept its union.
The case against the elections is the rule itself: a worker who doesn't vote counts as a no, and the union pays for every election.
We can't claim the old law raised pay, or that the new one held it down.
The agency's table shows the across-the-board raise in the state's AFSCME contract was 0 percent in fiscal years 2014 and 2015, under the old law.
In fiscal years 2018 through 2021, under the new one, it came in at or above the inflation figure the agency lists beside it.
The cap only binds an arbitrator, and the agency lists the raises for fiscal years 2020 and 2021 as ratified agreements.
We can't say whether PLAs make public projects cost more or less.
We found no Iowa study, and the national research we've seen is mostly paid for by one side or the other.
That is why the bill leaves the choice to each board instead of requiring PLAs.
"A reliable path to a pension" is Brett's view of where steady, skilled work leads.
The bill doesn't touch IPERS or any other pension, and retirement systems stay outside bargaining.
House File 291 did more than this bill undoes.
It also changed how teachers are let go, how city civil service workers are disciplined and public access to personnel records.
This bill restores only what those parts took off the bargaining table: teacher evaluation and grievance procedures, and the procedures in a contract for city civil service layoffs and discipline.
The rest would take bills of their own.
This spring the Legislature also added to the retention election rules with Senate File 472, which lets any Iowan go to court against an employer that doesn't send the board a list of its workers.
This bill ends those elections, so those rules go too.
The other side's strongest points Where the evidence cuts against us The Iowa Supreme Court upheld House File 291 4 to 3 in May 2019.
The majority wrote that the unions that sued "must look to the ballot box and the elected branches to change this lawfully enacted statute." What we'd do instead is what this bill does: change it in the Legislature, where it was made.
House File 291 saved the state money, and some of that could come back as higher payroll costs.
We won't pretend otherwise.
What we'd do instead of giving up on bargaining is keep the guardrails and add one: the employer's ability to pay is weighed, no arbitrator may count new taxes, fees, charges or other revenue as a way to pay, an award that breaks a budget limit is void, contracts top out at five years, and no one has to agree to anything.
Public safety unions have bargained under most of those rules since 2017, and the bill puts every public worker under the same ones.
Retention elections give workers a vote before every contract, and some people will see ending them as taking a vote away.
Workers keep the right to vote a union out.
Under the bill they can petition for that vote at set times before a contract runs out, the way they could until 2017.
And when a union stands for election, it should win or lose on the votes cast, the same way a county supervisor does.
The old law didn't guarantee raises, and this bill doesn't either.
What we'd do instead of promising raises is put the rest of the job back on the table: health insurance, overtime, safety, transfers, layoffs and a fair way to settle a grievance.
Those are what police and fire kept, and what the plow driver lost.
A PLA adds terms every contractor on the job has to accept, and some contractors may decide not to bid.
On a small job, that could mean fewer bids.
So the bill doesn't require a PLA anywhere.
It lets a city, county or school board choose one, in public, before bids go out, only if union and non-union contractors can both bid and no worker has to join a union or pay dues.
State agencies still can't require one.
It also keeps today's ban on tying a grant or tax break for a private developer to a union agreement.
Iowa's state mandates law (chapter 25B) says a city, county, school district, community college or area education agency doesn't have to carry out a new state requirement that adds local costs unless the state pays for it or the bill says what share it will pay.
The law covers only duties beyond what Iowa required before July 1994.
Bargaining over insurance was already required in 1994, and stayed required until 2017, so we think that argument fails, but a city could still make it.
What we'd do instead of leaving that to a lawsuit is settle it in the bill before it passes, either by saying the mandates law doesn't apply to bargaining or by having the state share the cost.
Some people think government shouldn't collect union dues at all.
Under the bill, an employer takes dues out only for a worker who asks in writing, and stops on thirty days’ notice.
Iowa stays a right-to-work state (chapter 731), so no one has to pay a union to keep a job.

EXPLANATION The inclusion of this explanation does not constitute agreement with the explanation’s substance by the members of the general assembly.
This bill relates to labor relations involving public employers, including collective bargaining by public employees under Code chapter 20 (public employment relations), payroll deduction of employee organization dues, and project labor agreements for public improvements.
It reverses changes made by House File 291 and Senate File 438 of the 87th General Assembly.
SCOPE OF NEGOTIATIONS.
Under current law, a bargaining unit in which at least 30 percent of the members are public safety employees bargains over a full list of mandatory subjects, and any other bargaining unit bargains only over base wages and other matters mutually agreed upon.
The bill strikes and rewrites Code section 20.9 (scope of negotiations) so that every bargaining unit bargains over the full list that public safety units bargain over today, including insurance, overtime, seniority, transfer procedures, evaluation procedures, procedures for staff reduction, health and safety matters, and grievance procedures.
Negotiations also include terms authorizing dues checkoff on a member’s written request.
The bill removes the requirement that mandatory subjects be interpreted narrowly and restrictively.
Retirement systems and payroll deductions for political purposes remain excluded from the scope of negotiations, the existing limit on the length of a contract remains, and the obligation to negotiate in good faith still does not compel either party to agree to a proposal or make a concession.
TEACHER EVALUATIONS.
The bill restores, nearly in the words used before House File 291, the duty of a school board to negotiate evaluation procedures with a certified bargaining representative in Code section 279.14 (evaluation criteria and procedures), and the provisions of Code section 284.3 (Iowa teaching standards) under which a school board and its certified bargaining representative may negotiate evaluation and grievance procedures for beginning teachers, shall negotiate evaluation and grievance procedures for other teachers, and may negotiate additional teaching standards and criteria for other teachers’ performance reviews.
CITY CIVIL SERVICE.
Under current law, the procedures in a collective bargaining agreement govern a reduction in the number of city civil service employees, and supplement the rules for removal, discharge, demotion, or suspension, only for a bargaining unit in which at least 30 percent of the members are public safety employees.
The bill applies both rules to every bargaining unit, in Code section 400.18 (removal, discharge, demotion, or suspension) and Code section 400.28 (employees number diminished).
ELECTIONS.
The bill strikes and rewrites Code section 20.15 (elections).
It ends retention and recertification elections, including the procedures added to them by Senate File 472 of the 91st General Assembly, and removes references to those elections from Code section 22.7 (confidential records) and Code section 602.1401 (personnel system).
A certification election is again decided by a majority of the votes cast rather than a majority of all employees in the bargaining unit, with a runoff between the two leading choices if no choice receives a majority.
Another employee organization may again be listed on the ballot with the lower showing of support that applied before House File 291, and a petition for certification still requires the showing of support in Code section 20.14 (bargaining representative determination).
Under current law, the board may not consider a new petition for certification for two years after an employee organization loses a certification, retention and recertification, or decertification election.
Under the bill, the wait is one year after a failed certification election, and an employee organization that is decertified may not be a party to a new petition or election for one year.
Employees may still remove an employee organization by petitioning for a decertification election, within the time limits that applied before House File 291.
The provisions governing collective bargaining agreements with the state are unchanged.
The bill also restores the rule in Code section 20.12 (strikes prohibited —— penalties) that an employee organization decertified for a strike stops receiving dues by checkoff.
ARBITRATION.
The bill strikes and rewrites Code section 20.22 (binding arbitration).
It removes the limit on the base wage increase an arbitrator may award to a bargaining unit that is not a public safety unit, which is the lesser of 3 percent or the change in the consumer price index for the midwest region, and removes the separate list of factors the arbitrator considers for those units.
For every bargaining unit, the arbitrator considers the factors used for public safety units today: past collective bargaining contracts, comparison with other public employees doing comparable work, and the interests and welfare of the public, the ability of the public employer to finance economic adjustments, and the effect of such adjustments on the normal standard of services.
For every bargaining unit, the arbitrator may not consider the public employer’s ability to fund an award through the increase or imposition of new taxes, fees, or charges, or to develop other sources of revenues.
Under current law that bar applies only to bargaining units that are not public safety units.
A collective bargaining agreement or award that is inconsistent with a statutory limitation on the public employer’s funds, spending, or budget remains invalid under Code section 20.17.
DUES.
The bill strikes and rewrites Code section 70A.19 (payroll deduction for employee organization dues prohibited).
A public employer may deduct employee organization dues from an employee’s pay, and must do so if a collective bargaining agreement provides for it, but only upon the employee’s written request.
The employee may end the deduction at any time with thirty days’ written notice.
The bill restores the provision of Code section 70A.17A (payroll deduction for dues) that it does not affect a dues deduction a state employee elects under Code section 70A.19.
PROJECT LABOR AGREEMENTS.
The bill amends Code section 73A.28 (public improvement contracts).
A political subdivision or public school corporation, or a construction manager acting on its behalf, may require a project labor agreement on a public improvement if the governing body adopts the requirement at a meeting open to the public before notice to bidders is given, and the agreement is published with the notice to bidders.
The agreement must bind all contractors and subcontractors on the project, allow all of them to compete whether or not they are parties to other collective bargaining agreements, contain guarantees against strikes and lockouts, set procedures for resolving labor disputes, provide for labor-management cooperation on safety, health, and quality of work, provide for the employment of registered apprentices, and not require any employee to join or pay a labor organization.
An agreement with any particular labor organization may not be required.
The authority applies notwithstanding Code section 26.16 (prequalification requirements prohibited) and the provision of Code section 26A.3 (construction manager-at-risk) that releases a construction manager-at-risk from the terms of a labor agreement for trade contracts it does not perform itself, so a project labor agreement may also be required on a construction manager-at-risk project.
The state and its agencies remain barred from requiring an agreement with a labor organization.
The bill keeps the prohibition on conditioning a grant, tax abatement, or tax credit on a requirement that an awardee use an agreement with a labor organization, and keeps the prohibition on barring or penalizing a contractor for signing or refusing to sign such an agreement, except for a project labor agreement the bill allows.
The bill makes no appropriation and allows no tax credit.
The bill may include a state mandate as defined in Code section 25B.3 (definitions).
The bill takes effect July 1, 2027.
It applies to collective bargaining procedures occurring on or after that date and to notices to bidders given on or after that date.
It does not apply to a collective bargaining agreement ratified, awarded by an arbitrator, or effective before that date.
