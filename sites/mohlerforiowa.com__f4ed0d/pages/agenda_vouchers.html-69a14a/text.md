Iowa already had school choice.
Open enrollment has let families pick a public district since 1989, and private schools and homeschooling were always an option.
The voucher program mostly changed who pays tuition.
Last school year it cost an estimated $329.6 million.
In year one, state numbers showed two-thirds of voucher students were already in private school.
Only about 1 in 8 came from a public school.
The state auditor estimated this June that 78.5 percent of last school year's users would have been in private school anyway, which works out to about $37,300 for each student the program moved.
Princeton researchers found private kindergarten tuition rose 21 to 25 percent in the program's first year, the one grade where every family qualified.
Pre-K, which vouchers don't cover, didn't move.
More than a third of Iowa's counties don't have an accredited private school.
In our district, Albia and Bloomfield each got one this year.
If you can cover the rest of the tuition and there's a school nearby, you get $8,148 this year.
If there isn't, the voucher does nothing for your kids.
Each voucher student sends about $1,256 of state money back to the home district.
That's real money, but nowhere near what's leaving.
Every Iowa public school district gets audited yearly, holds public budget hearings and has to answer records requests.
A private school cashing voucher checks doesn't have to.

The OPEN Act (Oversight of Public Expenditures at Nonpublic Schools) does three things.
An income limit.
From school year 2028-29, only families earning up to three hundred percent of the federal poverty line qualify.
That's about $99,000 for a family of four at the line set in 2026, updated yearly.
A phase-out.
After that year no new students join, and the last vouchers pay for school year 2030-31, so families have time to adjust.
From next school year, any private school taking vouchers gets a yearly audit of that money, a public hearing on how it's spent and open records on it.
The savings are set aside for public schools, through the HELP Act, the PACE Act and Tools for Schools: an estimated $151 million to $218 million in the limit's first year and $350 million to $459 million a year after it ends.
The Pay As You Go Act keeps those bills from spending more.
