Who gets a raise Very few Iowans are paid exactly $7.25.
The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted about 6,000 Iowa workers paid by the hour at or below the federal minimum in 2024, out of 858,000 paid by the hour, or 0.7 percent ( BLS ).
BLS warns that sampling error "may be quite large for some state estimates," so read that as a few thousand people, not an exact count.
Its count leaves tips out.
The people this bill is really about earn a little more than that.
In the federal wage survey for May 2025, one Iowa job in ten paid $13.91 an hour or less, and in southeast Iowa outside the metro areas, which takes in Appanoose, Davis, Monroe and Wapello counties, one in ten paid $13.49 or less.
The job right in the middle, the median, paid $23.34 statewide and $22.69 in southeast Iowa ( BLS wage survey ).
So the bill's two steps, $10.00 and $11.50, reach fewer than one job in ten here.
Tipped workers are the exception.
The survey counts tips as pay, so a server whose pay with tips is above $13.49 sits above that line, but the cash wage the employer owes still rises, from $4.35 to $6.00, then $6.90.
We don't have a count of Iowa workers under either step.
No federal table publishes one, and building it from the Census Bureau's survey files is work we haven't done.
A year of full-time work at today's minimum pays $15,080.
At the first step it's $20,800, and at the second, $23,920.
How the yearly number is set Each year by September 1, the director of the Department of Inspections, Appeals, and Licensing works out the next year's rate.
The first one is due September 1, 2029, for the rate that starts January 1, 2030.
That department already runs and enforces Iowa's minimum wage law (section 10A.202).
The director uses two numbers the federal government already publishes: Prices.
Last year's rate, raised by how much the consumer price index for wage earners (CPI-W) went up over the year ending in July.
We use July instead of August because the August index comes out in mid-September, after the deadline.
Iowa wages.
55 percent of Iowa's median hourly wage in the newest May wage survey, but never more than 8 percent above last year's rate.
Each is rounded up to the next five cents, except that the wage figure rounds down instead when rounding up would carry it past the 8 percent cap.
Then the higher of the two wins.
The rate never goes down, even in a year when prices fall.
If the government misses a month, the director uses the latest month published for both years.
That has happened: there is no price index for October 2025, because of the federal shutdown ( BLS ).
The director publishes every rate along with the figures behind it, so anyone can check the math.
A worked example, on today's numbers.
These are the latest figures published now, not the ones the director will have in 2029.
Say the rate is $11.50 and the director runs the formula today.
Prices: the index for July 2026, compared with a year earlier, was up 3.4 percent. $11.50 plus 3.4 percent, rounded up to the next five cents, is $11.90.
Wages: 55 percent of Iowa's median of $23.34 is $12.84.
The cap holds that to 8 percent above $11.50, which is $12.42.
Rounding up would give $12.45, past the cap, so it rounds down to $12.40.
The higher amount is $12.40, a raise of 7.8 percent.
On today's numbers, then, the first computed rate would be $12.40, not $12.85.
The cap spreads the climb to the target over more than one year.
The real rate for that first year depends on the wage survey and prices published in 2029, and Iowa's median will have moved by then.
Why 55 percent.
Today Iowa's minimum is 31 percent of the state median.
Half the median would be $11.67, about what Minnesota ($11.41) and South Dakota ($11.85) pay now.
Nebraska and Missouri are at $15.00 and Illinois at $15.
Britain's government treats two-thirds of median hourly earnings as its benchmark ( UK government ), though it measures earnings a different way.
55 percent sits above our northern neighbors and well short of Britain.
We found no U.S. state that ties its minimum to a share of its own median wage.
That was a search that came up empty, not proof there isn't one.
The closest model is federal: the federal Raise the Wage Act, filed in 2021, would have raised the federal minimum in steps and then grown it "at the same rate as median hourly wages" ( CBO ).
Why a cap.
Each fixed step is a bigger jump than 8 percent.
After that, the cap keeps a jump in the survey, or a change in how BLS runs it, from moving the floor by double digits in one January.
Prices get no cap.
If inflation runs hot, the minimum keeps pace.
Small businesses, new hires and tipped workers Small employers.
An employer with fewer than 20 employees, averaged over the year before, pays $7.25 through December 31, 2028, $10.00 from January 1, 2029, and $11.50 from January 1, 2030.
From January 1, 2031, it catches up under the same limit.
Each year its rate rises by inflation or by 8 percent, whichever is more, but never past everyone else's.
While the 8 percent limit is what sets everyone else's rate, the small rate takes the same steps a year late, so small employers stay one year behind.
They close the gap only after the limit stops setting everyone else's rate.
One path, to show how that plays out: say prices keep rising 3.4 percent a year and Iowa's median wage grows 3 percent a year from today's $23.34.
That's an assumption, not a forecast.
Everyone else would pay $12.40 in 2030, $13.35 in 2031, $14.40 in 2032, and $15.35 in 2033, and each year a small employer would pay what everyone else paid the year before.
In 2034 the small rate would be $15.55, against $15.90, and in 2035, both would be $16.45.
If Iowa's wages grow faster, the limit holds longer: at 3.5 percent a year, they meet in 2036.
Inflation above 8 percent for more than a year would keep the gap open too, because both rates would then rise with prices.
Once they meet, small employers pay the same as everyone.
Part-time and seasonal workers count, workers in other states count, and so do the workers of every business under the same ownership, so a chain can't split itself into small pieces.
Iowa law already says a franchise company isn't the employer of a franchisee's workers (section 91D.1).
So a franchised store counts its own workers and those of other stores its owner controls, not the whole brand.
A small city or school district with fewer than 20 employees gets the extra year too.
A brand-new business counts by its average so far that year.
A business that shrinks under 20 doesn't get a lower minimum than it paid the year before.
An employer that claims the extra year has to prove it qualifies.
New hires.
Iowa law lets an employer pay $6.35 to a new hire for the first 90 days, but federal law requires $7.25 for most workers.
Under the bill, it's 85 percent of the minimum, rounded up to the next five cents: $8.50 at the first step and $9.80 at the second, and never less than today's $6.35.
No one can be rehired by the same employer, or a business under the same ownership, within a year of their last job there to start that clock over.
The bill keeps today's rule that an employer pays the federal minimum when it's higher.
Tipped workers.
Today an employer can count tips for up to 40 percent of the minimum, so the cash wage is $4.35 an hour ( Iowa Workforce Development poster ).
The bill keeps that share, so the cash wage is 60 percent of the minimum: $6.00 at the first step and $6.90 at the second.
If a worker's tips for the week come up short of what the employer counted, the employer pays the difference.
Today's law lets a worker appeal.
The bill also says it outright.

What it doesn't do It isn't a living wage.
MIT's living wage calculator estimates a single adult with no children in Iowa needs $21.29 an hour ( MIT ).
That's a model of what it costs to get by, not a government statistic, and this bill doesn't get anywhere near it.
What we'd do instead: if voters want the floor closer, a later session can raise the target from 55 percent of the median to 60 percent.

It keeps the small-business exemption.
An enterprise with gross sales under $300,000 a year is outside Iowa's minimum wage law today (section 91D.1), and stays outside it.
Its workers are covered only by federal law, where federal law reaches them.
We left it alone so this bill is about one thing.
It keeps the ban on local minimum wages.
In 2017, House File 295 barred counties and cities from setting their own minimum wage and voided the ones already passed (sections 331.304 and 364.3).

The Ottumwa Courier reported at the time that Ottumwa, Agency, Eldon and Eddyville passed resolutions against it ( Ottumwa Courier ).
At that meeting, the minutes record a small-business owner telling the supervisors "we're not in Cedar Rapids or Iowa City," and, in the same remarks, "I'm all for a raise in the minimum wage, it's time." The minutes paraphrase speakers, so those are the clerk's words for what he said.
His point about the cities is fair, and it's part of why this bill sets one statewide floor tied to Iowa's own wages instead of a patchwork.
Brett would vote to let counties and cities set their own again, in a separate bill.
The limits of our case What the data does not support We can't say how many Iowans get a raise from this bill.
No published table counts Iowa workers under $10.00 or $11.50, and we haven't built one.
We can't say lots of Iowans are paid $7.25 today.
About 0.7 percent of hourly workers are, and the count is rough.
We can't promise indexing lasts.
Missouri voters passed a yearly cost-of-living raise in 2024, and in 2025, the Missouri legislature took it out, so the raises stop at $15.00 ( Missouri law today and before the change ).
Nebraska voters tied their minimum to prices in 2022, and in 2026, the Nebraska legislature swapped that for a flat 1.75 percent a year ( Nebraska law ).
South Dakota's index, which voters passed in 2014, with 55 percent of the vote, still stands ( South Dakota Secretary of State , South Dakota law ).
Iowa has no ballot initiative, so this can only pass through the Legislature, and a later Legislature could undo it the same way.
"Iowa's own wages" means the federal survey's median for jobs in Iowa.
That survey leaves out the self-employed and farms, and counts tips as pay ( BLS technical notes ), so it isn't every paycheck in the state.
We can't put a number on how many Iowa families this lifts out of poverty.
We found no Iowa estimate.
The other side's strongest points Where the evidence cuts against us Some economists find job losses.
When the Congressional Budget Office studied raising the federal minimum in steps through 2025, it found that a $10 minimum would have almost no effect on jobs ( CBO ).
A $12 minimum would leave about 300,000 more people jobless in an average week, with a likely range from about zero to 800,000, and a $15 minimum about 1.3 million.
CBO also said employment responds more to raises that keep adjusting with wage growth, which is what this bill's wage route does.
The research is split.
A study of 138 state increases found the number of low-wage jobs "remained essentially unchanged over five years," with "some evidence of reduced employment in tradable sectors" ( Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer ).
A review of the published research found "a clear preponderance of negative estimates," strongest for teens, young adults and workers with less education ( Neumark and Shirley ).
What we'd do instead of a faster jump: steps first, a target well under Britain's, a cap on how fast the wage route moves, and an extra year for small employers, with every figure published so lawmakers can see what's happening and change course.
Southern Iowa pays less than the state as a whole.
The target uses the statewide median, $23.34, and southeast Iowa's is $22.69, so the same floor takes a bigger bite here.
A Wapello County business owner said as much in 2016.
What we'd do instead of a separate rural rate: one floor for the whole state, set well under the local median, with the extra year for small businesses.
A regional rate would draw a line through somebody's town, and one employer would pay less than the one across the road.
The cap makes it slower.
People who want 55 percent of the median right away won't always get it the first year, if the median has moved by then.
What we'd do instead of dropping the cap: keep the fixed steps on a known schedule, so workers see real money on January 1, 2028 and January 1, 2029 while the formula catches up.
Indexing can be undone.
Missouri and Nebraska show it.
What we'd do instead of hoping: keep the formula simple, let it never go down, and publish the math every year, so taking it away is a vote people can see.
The small-business exemption leaves some workers out.
What we'd do instead of reopening it in this bill: hold it for a separate debate after this passes, so a fight over the smallest shops doesn't sink the raise for everybody else.
The new-hire wage could be gamed by cycling through new workers every 90 days.
The bill stops an employer rehiring the same person within a year to restart the clock.
What we'd do instead of trusting that: if wage complaints show employers churning new hires, limit the share of hours paid at the new-hire wage.
Nebraska already does this: no more than one-fourth of the hours an employer pays can be at its training wage ( Nebraska law ).
The public cost is unknown.
The state, cities, counties, schools and Medicaid providers will pay something, and no one has priced it.
What we'd do instead of guessing: get the Legislative Services Agency's fiscal note before a floor vote, put the state's own share, for its own workers and the Medicaid rates it sets, in the budget, and fund a fix if the note shows a real cost to small towns.

Section 91D.1, subsection 1, Code 2026, is amended by striking the subsection and inserting in lieu thereof the following: 1. a.
For purposes of this section, unless the context otherwise requires: (1) “Consumer price index” means the consumer price index for urban wage earners and clerical workers, United States city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted, as published by the bureau of labor statistics of the United States department of labor, or its successor index.

(3) “Iowa median hourly wage” means the median hourly wage for all occupations in this state, all industries, as published by the bureau of labor statistics of the United States department of labor in its occupational employment and wage statistics estimates for a May reference period, or in any successor estimates, using the most recent such estimates published before September 1 of the year in which the director makes a computation under paragraph “e”.

For purposes of this subparagraph: (a) The average is the sum of the number of individuals employed by the employer on the twelfth day of each month of the calendar year, divided by twelve.
Each individual counts as one employee, whether employed full-time, part-time, or seasonally, and whether employed in this state or elsewhere. (b) The employees of every person that controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with the employer are counted as employees of the employer.
“Control” means ownership, directly or indirectly, of more than fifty percent of the voting or ownership interests of a person, or the power to direct the management and policies of a person. (c) For an employer that was in operation during only part of the preceding calendar year, the average is computed over the months in which it was in operation.
An employer that was not in operation at any time during the preceding calendar year is a small employer for a workweek during its first calendar year of operation if it employed fewer than twenty employees, on average, on the twelfth day of each month of that year that occurred after it began operation and before the workweek began or, if no such day has occurred, if it employs fewer than twenty employees on the first day of the workweek. Every employer, as defined in the federal Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, as amended to January 1, 2007, shall pay to each of the employer’s employees, as defined in the federal Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, as amended to January 1, 2007, the applicable state hourly wage stated in paragraph “c” or “d”, or the current federal minimum wage, pursuant to 29 U.S.C. §206, as amended, whichever is greater. c.

As of January 1, 2031, and as of January 1 of each year thereafter, the state hourly wage for an employee of a small employer shall be the lesser of the following amounts: (1) The state hourly wage under paragraph “c” that takes effect on the same January 1.
(2) The greater of the following amounts, each computed from the state hourly wage for an employee of a small employer in effect on the preceding December 31: (a) That wage increased by the same percentage, if any, used under paragraph “e”, subparagraph (1), subparagraph division (a), for the rate that takes effect under paragraph “c” on the same January 1, rounded up to the nearest multiple of five cents. EXPLANATION The inclusion of this explanation does not constitute agreement with the explanation’s substance by the members of the general assembly.
This bill relates to the state minimum wage in Code section 91D.1 (minimum wage requirements —— exceptions).
STATE HOURLY WAGE.
Under current law, the state hourly wage is $7.25, the rate set as of January 1, 2008, with no later increase, and an employer pays the greater of that rate or the federal minimum wage.
The bill strikes and rewrites the subsection of Code section 91D.1 that sets the state hourly wage, the tip credit, and the wage for new employees.
The state hourly wage remains $7.25 through December 31, 2027, rises to $10.00 on January 1, 2028, and to $11.50 on January 1, 2029.
Beginning January 1, 2030, the director of the department of inspections, appeals, and licensing (director) sets the rate each year, computing it by September 1 of the preceding year, the first time by September 1, 2029.
The new rate is the greater of the prior rate increased by the change in the consumer price index for urban wage earners and clerical workers from July to July, or fifty-five percent of the median hourly wage for all occupations in Iowa in the most recent federal occupational employment and wage statistics estimates, except that the second amount may not exceed the prior rate by more than 8 percent.
Each amount is rounded up to the nearest multiple of five cents, except that the second amount is rounded down when rounding up would carry it past that limit.
The rate may never decrease.
An employer still pays the federal minimum wage if it is higher.
SMALL EMPLOYERS.
An employer with fewer than twenty employees on average during the preceding calendar year, counting the employees of affiliated businesses under common control, reaches each step one year later: $10.00 on January 1, 2029, and $11.50 on January 1, 2030.
Beginning January 1, 2031, a small employer pays the rate for other employers, but not more than the greater of the statewide rate for employees of small employers in effect at the end of the preceding year, increased by the change in the consumer price index, rounded up to the nearest multiple of five cents, or that statewide rate increased by 8 percent, rounded down to the nearest multiple of five cents, so that it catches up to the rate for other employers over time.
An employer that was not in operation during the preceding calendar year is a small employer in a workweek of its first year if its average number of employees in the months of that year before that workweek, or its number of employees on the first day of the workweek if no month has yet been counted, is fewer than twenty.
No employer's applicable rate may be less than the rate that applied to it at the end of the preceding year.
NEW EMPLOYEES.
Under current law, an employer may pay a new employee $6.35 an hour for the first ninety calendar days of employment, but must still pay the federal minimum wage, $7.25 an hour for most employees, when it applies and is higher.
The bill sets that wage at eighty-five percent of the applicable state hourly wage, rounded up to the nearest multiple of five cents, and not less than $6.35.
The new-employee wage does not apply to a person the same employer, or an affiliated business, employed during the twelve months before the person’s most recent date of hire.
TIPPED EMPLOYEES.
The bill keeps the existing tip credit, which counts tips toward the minimum wage for up to 40 percent of the applicable minimum wage, and requires an employer to pay the difference when an employee’s tips in a workweek fall short of the credit the employer took.
PUBLICATION AND ENFORCEMENT.
By September 1 of each year, beginning in 2027, the director publishes each rate for the next calendar year, with the figures used to compute it, and every covered employer must post a notice of the rates where employees can see it.
The bill does not change the exemption for an enterprise with annual gross sales of less than $300,000, the other exemptions in Code section 91D.1, or enforcement under Code chapter 91A (wage payment collection).
The bill does not change the prohibition on county and city minimum wage ordinances in Code section 331.304 (procedural limitations on general county powers) and Code section 364.3 (limitation of powers).
The bill makes no appropriation and allows no tax credit.
The bill may include a state mandate as defined in Code section 25B.3 (definitions).
The bill makes inapplicable the part of Code section 25B.2 (findings and purpose —— effect of unfunded state mandate) that would relieve a political subdivision from complying with a state mandate if adequate funding is not provided.
The bill takes effect July 1, 2027.
