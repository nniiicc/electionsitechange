The rule.
The Pay As You Go Act has the nonpartisan Legislative Services Agency keep a scorecard every year.
Every bill in this agenda that adds new spending or a tax credit signs up.
New spending goes on one side, the savings that pay for it on the other.

The biggest swing: inflation The PACE Act sets school aid growth no lower than the rise in prices the year before.
When prices rise faster than lawmakers would have raised school aid anyway, that costs money, and each year's difference stays in the base.
Here we let prices rise anywhere from 2 percent to 3.7 percent a year, and we let the raise lawmakers would have given anyway vary the same way the OPEN Act's model does.
The PACE Act then costs $52 million to $194 million in 2028-29, its first year under this schedule, and $159 million to $468 million by school year 2031-32, its fixed programs included.
That range is most of the uncertainty in the table.
If prices run hot for years while lawmakers would have held school aid flat, the PACE floor alone could use up most of the voucher savings, and the scorecard would trim it.
How the scorecard works A bill joins the scorecard by saying so.
Every bill in this agenda that adds new spending or a tax credit says it's subject to the scorecard: the HELP Act, the PACE Act, Tools for Schools, and the STREAM, FAIR, CARE and DEED Acts.
Three bills in the agenda don't.
The LABOR Act, the labor bill, appropriates nothing and creates no tax credit, so the scorecard has nothing to trim.
Any payroll cost would come through contracts in the regular budget.
The RAISE Act, the minimum wage bill, is off too: it sets a wage for employers to pay and appropriates nothing.
Cities, counties and schools pay any raise it means for their own workers from their own budgets, and the bill doesn't let them opt out.
As for the FUEL Act, it moves an appropriation state law already makes each year, for fuel-pump grants, to a new use, so it adds no new general fund cost.
The OPEN Act and this bill say they're offsetting Acts, the ones that bring the savings.
The regular budget and the yearly school aid bill aren't on it unless lawmakers put them there.
After each session, the Legislative Services Agency lists each covered bill's cost to the general fund, including the cost of a tax credit like the STREAM Act's, and each offsetting bill's savings.
It uses its own fiscal notes, updated to the latest revenue estimate.
Three rules decide what can pay for what: Money that shows up once, like the sports betting balance, can only pay for a cost that happens once, like the STREAM loan fund.
Savings a bill sets aside go first to the costs they're set aside for.
The OPEN Act sets its voucher savings aside for the costs of appropriations for school districts, AEAs and the Department of Education's elementary and secondary programs.
This bill sets the data center money aside for water quality, soil conservation, health care and the nutrient management credit: the budget's share of each payment, the Iowa Future Fund's yearly draw, and sales tax from any center that doesn't pay.
In this agenda that means STREAM, CARE and the FAIR Act's grazing fence and water cost-share.
The FAIR Act's money is paid from the first year's one-time money anyway, so after the first year the data center money goes to STREAM and CARE.
A transfer out of the state's reserve funds or the Taxpayer Relief Fund doesn't count as a saving, and neither does borrowed money.
If a covered bill's costs for a year aren't met, the Department of Management trims it, by the same percentage as every other covered bill in that spot, until they are.
A tax credit is trimmed the same way.
It can only trim what a covered bill added.
A program that existed before doesn't get cut to pay for a new one.

The data center break Iowa exempts from sales tax the computers, cooling systems, power equipment, racks and cabling a data center or web search portal buys, if it invests at least $200 million within six years and meets the other terms in section 423.3.
It also exempts their electricity and backup generator fuel: with no end date for centers running before June 6, 2025, and for ten or fifteen years for newer buildings.
The law sets no job count and no wage floor.
The state's schedule for reviewing tax breaks (section 2.48) doesn't list it.
This bill doesn't repeal any of that.
Starting in 2028, a center keeps all of those breaks at a location for a year only if it pays $10 for each megawatt-hour of electricity bought for that location that year.
It pays the next January, with the annual report these centers already file with the Department of Revenue, Form 78-014, which asks for the kilowatt-hours bought at each location.
The bill writes that report into the law for each location, adds the megawatt-hours to it, and has the department issue each location its own exemption certificate, good for a year from the start of February.
The next certificate waits until the report is filed and the payment is in, so a center that pays late has no breaks until it pays.
A new location gets its first certificate when it registers.
Each location stands on its own, and a related company buying for a web search portal's site uses that site's certificate.
The power counted is what the electric meter shows.
A center that leases space and gets its power through its landlord counts the power on the landlord's meter for its space.
If the landlord doesn't meter that space separately, the tenant counts its share of the building's metered power, split by the computer power capacity each tenant contracted for, as the landlord certifies.
Either way, a tenant can't keep the breaks for free because someone else pays the power bill.
New sites.
A center pays on the power it buys in the same year, so a brand-new center's first payment comes the January after its first year running, on that year's power.
A site still being built that buys no power owes nothing while it's built.
But if it then skips the payment for either of its first two years running, it owes the sales tax on everything it bought tax-free while it was being built, from 2028 on, plus interest.
Skipping a later year's payment costs that year's breaks, and the next year's certificate waits until the tax owed is paid.
The equipment break during construction is part of the bargain, and reaching back over two years keeps a center from paying for a tiny first year and then walking away with it.
A center that pays two years and then stops still keeps its construction break.
That's a gap we'd accept: by then it has paid in, and it loses its breaks every year it doesn't pay.
When the power break has run out.
A newer center's power break ends after ten or fifteen years.
After that it pays sales tax on its power, and that tax goes to the energy loan fund, as described below.
It still owes the payment on the same power if it wants to keep the equipment break.
That's on purpose: the payment is the price of the equipment break too, and that break is large.
If centers whose power break has ended start dropping the payment, what we'd do instead is charge them a lower rate.
The rate rises each year with prices, by the federal consumer price index the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes, and the bill spells that out in its own words instead of borrowing a definition from the income tax law.
The rate never goes down.
It's a choice, not a tax.
A center can skip the payment, give up the breaks for that location that year, and pay sales tax like any other business.
The bill says so in plain words, and there's no penalty or interest on a payment nobody has to make.
The department can audit the power a center reports.
A center that claims the breaks during a year and then doesn't pay gets sixty days after notice, and then owes the sales tax it skipped that year, with interest, and for its first two years running the tax from the years it was being built too.
The department has three years from the payment's due date to bill that tax.
A center that underpays gets sixty days after notice to pay the rest with interest, or it's treated the same as a center that didn't pay.
Reports and payments are as confidential as tax returns, and the department publishes yearly totals without naming anyone.
Where the money goes.
For power bought in 2028, 75 percent of each payment goes to the general fund, set aside through the scorecard for water quality, soil conservation, health care and the nutrient management credit, and 25 percent goes to the new Iowa Future Fund.
The budget's share drops by five percentage points a year until it's half, for power bought in 2033 and after.
The Iowa Future Fund.
It sits with the state treasurer.
It never spends its principal, meaning everything paid into it.
Starting July 1, 2029, it sends the general fund 4 percent a year of its average value over the last five years, or fewer years while the fund is new, set aside for the same uses, but never so much that it drops below what was put in.
It can pay the cost of investing and auditing it, only out of what it has earned above that, and the state auditor checks it every year.
Nothing else can come out of it under this bill.

The fund never spends what was paid into it.
For now it has to invest the way the treasurer invests other state money, under section 12B.10: mostly federal bonds, insured deposits and short-term notes, and never common stocks.
The Iowa Constitution says the state can't become a stockholder in any corporation, and we didn't find a court ruling or Attorney General opinion on whether a state fund that buys stocks crosses that line.
So we wrote a short companion joint resolution to amend the constitution.
It would protect everything paid into the fund from being spent except by the yearly draw and the fund's own investing and audit costs, paid from earnings, and let the fund buy stocks.
Under the Iowa Constitution, an amendment has to pass two general assemblies in a row, with an election in between, and then the voters.
If lawmakers pass it in 2027, and the next legislature passes it again in 2029, voters would most likely decide it at the general election in 2030.
The fund doesn't wait for it: the law starts it with the first payments.
The tax already earmarked.
Under a law passed in 2025, when a newer center's ten- or fifteen-year electricity break runs out, the sales tax it starts paying goes to the energy infrastructure revolving loan fund (sections 423.2A and 423.43, and section 476.46A), not the general fund.
This bill leaves that alone.
Sales tax a center pays only because it skipped the payment goes to the general fund and is set aside like the payments.
Tax it would have owed anyway, because its time ran out, still goes to the energy loan fund.
That keeps the energy fund whole and keeps a center's choice from shifting tax from one fund to another.
Who backs the idea.
Zach Lahn, the Republican nominee for governor, said data center companies should "pay your fair share," and Radio Iowa reported he might support a state law forbidding tax breaks for data centers.
His own example was property tax abatements; this bill puts a price on a sales tax break.
Rob Sand, the Democratic nominee, has promised to end these breaks.
This bill keeps them for centers that pay, for the reasons below.
Sales tax collected from a center that doesn't pay also sends a share to school infrastructure and to local option sales taxes, the way all sales tax does.
The smaller trims The fuel-pump grant deposit.
Iowa law puts $5 million a year from the general fund into the fund that pays for higher-ethanol and biodiesel pumps at gas stations.
Lawmakers already set that deposit at zero for this budget year, in Senate File 2484, which the governor signed.
This bill does the same thing for one more year, using the same words.
After that, the FUEL Act, another bill in this agenda, sends most of that yearly money to a new Iowa ag diversification fund for new crop markets and pasture, and the rest to motor fuel inspection.
So under the full agenda, the pump fund gets no new general fund deposit.
It keeps whatever balance it has.
We didn't find a published figure for that balance.
The governor's office.
The general office appropriation went from $2,315,344 to $2,810,502 in the budget lawmakers passed in 2023, and to $2,864,932 the year after.
This bill sets it back to $2,315,344 for the first budget year.
The law setting the governor's own salary was last changed in 2005.
It's $130,000, and this bill doesn't touch it.
The sports betting balance.
The law says the fund's money is used "as directed by the general assembly." This bill directs the balance left on June 30, 2027, to the general fund.
The money that comes in after that keeps paying the standing amount for public safety equipment, and the top-up to the fund for volunteer firefighter and EMS service awards.
What we left out, and why Ending the ethanol and biodiesel credits.
They already end on January 1, 2028, under current law.
"Sunsetting" them saves nothing the law doesn't already save.
A bill to extend the biodiesel credit passed the House this year.
What we'd do instead is let the credits end on schedule and oppose any extension.
Cutting the governor's salary.
The law setting it was last changed in 2005.
The increase was to the office's budget, which this bill rolls back.
Capping cabinet directors' pay.
Much of that pay comes from federal money and fees, not the general fund, so it wouldn't help balance the budget.
It's a fair question about who sets that pay, but it's a different bill.

EXPLANATION The inclusion of this explanation does not constitute agreement with the explanation’s substance by the members of the general assembly.
This bill relates to state fiscal matters.
PAY-AS-YOU-GO SCORECARD.
The bill creates a new Code section in chapter 8 requiring the legislative services agency to keep a scorecard for each fiscal year beginning on or after July 1, 2027.
It applies only to Acts enacted on or after January 1, 2027, that say they are subject to it (covered Acts) or that they offset its costs (offsetting Acts).
The scorecard lists each covered Act’s estimated cost to the general fund, including the cost of a tax credit, and each offsetting Act’s estimated saving.
A transfer from the state’s reserve funds or the taxpayer relief fund, or borrowed money, is not a saving.
A saving that occurs in only one fiscal year may be counted only against a cost that occurs in only one fiscal year.
An offsetting Act may dedicate its savings to a class of costs of covered Acts, and a dedicated saving is counted first against those costs.
The agency publishes the scorecard after each regular session and updates it after each December revenue estimate.
If covered Acts’ costs for a fiscal year are not met, the department of management reduces them until they are.
It does so separately for one-time costs and other costs, and separately for each class of costs of covered Acts that savings are dedicated to.
Savings that are not dedicated, and dedicated savings left over after the costs they're dedicated to, are shared among unmet costs in proportion to those costs, and within each group every unmet cost is reduced by the same percentage.
A tax credit is reduced the same way, for the tax year whose credits were counted.
A reduction reaches only the spending or credit a covered Act created or increased.
DATA CENTER AND WEB SEARCH PORTAL EXEMPTIONS.
Under current law, a web search portal business or a data center business that meets the requirements of Code section 423.3 (exemptions) pays no sales or use tax on its computers and equipment, its backup power generation fuel, or its electricity, registers with the department of revenue, files an annual report on the electricity it bought the year before, and presents an exemption certificate the department issues each year after that report.
The bill adds a new subsection to Code section 423.3.
Beginning with calendar year 2028, a business may claim those exemptions for a location only under a certificate for that location, and only on the condition that it makes an Iowa future fund payment for that location for each calendar year in which it claims them.
The payment is $10 per megawatt-hour of the electricity bought for use at that location that year, including electricity an affiliate or a landlord buys for it.
A landlord’s electricity is measured by the landlord’s meter for the leased space or, if the space has no meter of its own, by the tenant’s share of the building’s metered electricity, based on the computer power capacity each tenant contracted for, as the landlord certifies.
Electricity the business supplies to its own tenant that files its own report is not counted.
The rate rises each later year by the prior year’s increase in the federal consumer price index for all urban consumers, and never goes down.
The payment is due at the end of the following January, with the annual report.
Each certificate runs for a year from the start of February, and the department issues the next one for a location after the business files that location’s report and makes its payment.
A new location gets its first certificate when the business registers it.
Certificates issued before 2028 stay valid through the end of January 2028.
If a business does not pay within sixty days after notice, it loses the exemptions for that location for that year and pays the sales and use tax it did not pay, with interest.
If the year it skipped is either of the first two years the location used electricity in operation, it also loses the exemptions for the years from 2028 on in which the location was being built.
The department may determine that tax within three years after the payment was due, notwithstanding Code section 423.37 (limitation period).
The department may audit the electricity reported, and a business must keep its landlord’s meter records and certifications.
A business that underpays may pay the shortfall with interest within sixty days after notice, or it loses the exemptions for that year in the same way.
The payment is not a tax and is not required, and no penalty or interest applies to a payment that is not made.
Reports and payment information are confidential like tax returns, and the department publishes yearly totals in the aggregate.
For payments for calendar year 2028, 75 percent goes to the general fund and the rest to the Iowa future fund.
The general fund’s share falls by five percentage points for each later calendar year, to half for calendar year 2033 and after.
Sales and use tax paid because a business did not make a payment stays in the general fund rather than going to the energy infrastructure revolving loan fund, except for tax the business would have owed anyway because an exemption period ended.
The bill also strikes and rewrites the registration, annual report and certificate requirements in the three subsections of Code section 423.3 that grant these exemptions, so that a business registers each location, files a report for each location stating its megawatt-hours, and presents the certificate for the location where it buys, and so that an affiliate of a web search portal business uses that location’s certificate.
IOWA FUTURE FUND.
The bill creates an Iowa future fund in chapter 12, under the treasurer of state, invested under Code section 12B.10.
Beginning July 1, 2029, each year the treasurer transfers to the general fund 4 percent of the fund’s average market value over the prior five fiscal years, or fewer while the fund is new, but never so much that the fund falls below the total paid into it, not counting earnings.
The fund may pay the costs of investing and auditing it, only out of value above that total.
Nothing else may be taken from the fund.
The auditor of state audits it each year, and the treasurer publishes the fund’s value, deposits, earnings, costs and transfer each year.
RENEWABLE FUEL INFRASTRUCTURE FUND.
The bill sets the standing appropriation to the renewable fuel infrastructure fund under Code section 159A.17 at zero for the fiscal year beginning July 1, 2027, as Senate File 2484, enacted in 2026, did for the fiscal year before.
GOVERNOR AND LIEUTENANT GOVERNOR.
The bill appropriates $2,315,344 for the general office of the governor and lieutenant governor for the fiscal year beginning July 1, 2027, in place of any other general fund appropriation for that office that year, the amount appropriated for that office for each fiscal year from July 1, 2020, through June 30, 2023.
SPORTS WAGERING RECEIPTS FUND.
The bill transfers the balance left in the sports wagering receipts fund on June 30, 2027, to the general fund.
Appropriations made from the fund’s receipts for later fiscal years, including the standing appropriation to the public safety equipment fund, are not affected.
The bill is an offsetting Act under the scorecard.
The general fund’s share of the data center payments, the yearly transfer from the Iowa future fund, and sales and use tax paid by a business that does not make a payment are dedicated to the costs, under covered Acts, of appropriations for water quality, soil conservation or health care, and of a nutrient management tax credit.
The bill takes effect upon enactment.
The scorecard applies to Acts enacted on or after January 1, 2027, and the data center payment applies to purchases made on or after January 1, 2028.
