Assessment of the Project W.O.R.T.H.
Lesson
First, I’d like to note that the students at New Oxford Middle School, whom I observed in Mr.
DeFelippo’s 8th grade, 4th period health class on Friday, March 1, 2019 ( a shortened period, due to a weather delay), were a delight to watch as they engaged, participated, and volunteered throughout the lesson.
I have no issue with the students of CVSD.
Also I’d like to note that this assessment is not to disparage the character of Mrs.
Janczyk, but to highlight the egregiousness of the use of Tender Care’s materials and agents during instructional time.
Not having been given a lesson plan to follow along the lesson with, I was promptly seated at the back of the classroom and began to take notes with pen and paper.
Mr.
DeFelippo opened the lesson by going around and checking homework.
He also commended students for their respect toward Mrs.
Janczyk and then he stepped aside.
This raised the first and basic question that was never satisfactorily answered throughout the lesson – why is the certified secondary education instructor relegated to simply checking homework, passing out papers, ending the lesson with a reminder about the next homework and deferring to an outsourced agent whose agency’s mission and values are focused less on science and much more on their Statement of Faith?
Presumably Mr.
DeFelippo and the other health teachers were hired due to their credentials including, but not limited to: college degrees of bachelor’s or higher in specific content areas including methods of teaching courses in his area of concentration, scores from teacher assessments like the Praxis series, experience in teaching, copies of previous observations, and any other life experiences that positively contribute to their teaching, as well as having passed rounds of interviews with CVSD personnel.
Furthermore, it is assumed that Mr.
DeFelippo takes continuing education to ensure his continued credentials.
This raised other questions that were also never satisfactorily answered – what special expertise does Mrs.
Janczyk possess beyond that of any certified secondary education health teacher?
With no identifiable written objective, Mrs.
Janczyk opened her part of the lesson with the statement “We’re going to talk today about important topics.
We’re going to talk about consent.” The importance of consent was announced repeatedly throughout the lesson, despite the fact Day 3 of Eighth Grade especially, was not a lesson about consent, nor did the activities and stated importance of the lesson match the materials about Project W.O.R.T.H. that I had been previously given to review.
The constant reminders of how important a topic this is combined with the specific activities and transparencies provided, inconsistent with the previous Project W.O.R.T.H. materials I had reviewed raised the next question – was this lesson newly created since I, along with other parents raised the issue about a substantial lack of teaching consent?
Mrs.
Janczyk then directed students to anonymously write down what they thought of the topic, if they thought consent was important and why, and directed students to put them in the container, in order to establish a baseline of student understanding before beginning the lesson, but this raised yet another question – would Mrs.
Janczyk expand upon and ensure individual mastery of concepts beyond this basic level of understanding?
Mrs.
Janczyk then verbally defined consent, but despite the importance of this topic, she did not direct students to write down the definition she gave, she did not have it written on the board for them to copy, and she did not direct them to one in the packet, if it existed.
After asking general questions about sexual consent and fielding student’s verbal answers, she posted the No/Yes transparency on the board and verbally went over it, not directing students to copy it, not directing students to any possible corresponding worksheet in the packet if it existed, and not validating any other examples that students may have provided by also writing them on the transparency.
In asking about the #Metoo movement, Mrs.
Janczyk verbally received a base level of student understanding and offered one local example of sexual assault.
By focusing on college students in a dorm situation, Mrs.
Janczyk missed multiple opportunities to cite several examples which are more appropriate considering the ages of the students.
According to an AP analysis of federal crime data, requoted by the NEA in their article http://neatoday.org/2017/12/04/sexual-assault-in-schools/ “While occasions of adult-on-child sexual assaults on school property claim headlines—and rightfully so—the problem of student-on-student sexual attacks is much more common.
For every adult-on-child sexual assault, there were seven such assaults by students”.
Mrs.
Janczyk also failed to mention local examples concerning adults in public secondary schools assaulting students.
https://fox43.com/2017/05/16/littlestown-high-school-band-director-charged-with-sexual-assault/
Reading student answers from the papers and verbally validating their responses, Mrs.
Janczyk used the word “coerced” and defined it verbally, but did not define it in writing and did not define other vocabulary related to non-consent such as bribery and intimidation.
While she mentioned sexual assault, she did not provide applicable law about statutory rape, statutory exclusion, or “Romeo and Juliet” laws of Pennsylvania.
When teaching about such an important topic, especially involving behaviors that could potentially carry legal jeopardy, it is important to provide relevant specifics.
After requesting two student volunteers to walk a straight line, Mrs.
Janczyk then provided Fatal Vision alcohol impairment goggles for them to wear, explaining that each pair of goggles simulated “the vision of being drunk”.
A quick glance at the website for the makers of the drunk goggles reveals that they are intended for the wearer, in order to simulate the experience of navigating while drunk.
Therefore, only two students used the products as intended.
While it is important for students to identify when an impaired person is incapable of giving informed consent, this demonstration in navigation was a weak correlation.
Demonstrating physical impairment in terms of balance and equilibrium does not correlate to simulating impaired judgement.
Additionally, Mrs.
Janczyk noted the simulated alcohol blood content levels at .07 and higher.
Pennsylvania law allows people under the age of 21 to be charged with a D.U.I. when BAC levels are as low as .02 which would have been timely to acknowledge.
Not acknowledging these things was yet another indication of Mrs.
Janczyk not considering the age of the students whom she was trying to teach.
Time would have been better spent if she, the adult with presumably more experience regarding sex and alcohol, had provided scenarios based in a reality that she or other adults had experienced, for students to act out and for other students to determine if they were witnessing examples of consent and if not, what types of persuasion like coercion, intimidation, bullying, extortion, etc.
Throughout the lesson Mrs.
Janczyk used awkward phrasing and weak transitions such as “Very interesting when you add the element of alcohol”, “There’s a lot to say when someone is drinking.
How do you put consent together in a party scene?” ,“How do you know about dating violence?”, and “When you match the pornography with alcohol, you don’t have self-control” indicating an unfamiliarity with the subject.
In my opinion, Mrs.
Janczyk also conveyed a lack of professionalism by referring to one student as “Sweetheart” and stating to another “You’re smart.
What do you think?”
Outside of mentioning that she had known people who were gang-raped, Mrs.
Janczyk did not convey expertise beyond that of the established secondary education teacher.
Additionally, Mrs.
Janczyk failed to make a strong correlation, if any, that consent honors the simple “no” of abstinence.
Mrs.
Janczyk also stated “People don’t want to go through a court scene or have people found guilty.” This statement undermines the point of the previous conversation about the #Metoo movement and Mrs.
Janczyk missed an exceptional opportunity to highlight the bravery of a local student who recently approached her high school counselor about an illicit sexual relationship that a man from Conewago Township had with her beginning when she was twelve years old.
Instead, Mrs.
Janczyk turned the lesson back to the Tender Care packet for a few minutes to read over some signs of unhealthy relationships.
Time would have been better spent by Mrs.
Janczyk showing a video about consent, such as https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fGoWLWS4-kU or https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=laMtr-rUEmY
While the students engaged in a group activity of answering questions about identifying boundaries, etc.
(1) When should you have this conversation? 2) What should you ask?3) What, if anything, do you want to be clear about?), it was questionable for a couple of reasons.
First, consent is a very abstract topic.
By not establishing concrete scenarios, students were asked to imagine sexual scenarios and then the types of questions they might ask.
Second, considering the relatively little sexual experience these students have presumably had, students would likely have little of their own concrete information to draw upon.
While the students did participate in the activity, answers vacillated between generalized and speculative.
In directing the class to the written homework assignment she reminded students and their parents to write down what they would do in certain scenarios and requested a parent signature.
Mrs.
Janczyk said about the homework “Work with Mom and Dad – I need their input”.
Why did Mrs.
Janczyk state that she would need parental input?
What happens if a student or parent shares views that are contrary to the express, explicit, and published views of Tender Care?
Student work was either collected in a container or it was displayed on the board, not to be taken home or even copied, but presumably, thrown away, calling in to question how much she truly valued student input.
A better homework assignment would have been for students to log two situations in which they were asked to give permission to someone else for something, and at least three situations in which they asked someone else for permission to do something, then to record how each situation went.
This would have allowed students to explore consent situations in their own lives, to consciously practice it, or remind themselves of times that they already use it, and think critically about how they are persuaded, as well as how they persuade others.
In summary, the lack of follow-through, the missed opportunities to cite actual laws or examples more relevant to the age of her student audience, the failure to clearly define terms, especially in any written form, the inability to establish having moved students to a level beyond that of their original understanding, and the inability to establish individual student mastery of concepts, all converge to one conclusion – that Mrs.
Janczyk, effectively, didn’t teach.
Sure, she said some of the right things and the students were clearly engaged, but with four adults in the room, how much control of the class did she truly manage?
Having observed this lesson, I am more affirmed of my position – that the Tender Care program, materials, representatives, and messages must be removed from instructional time in our public schools and replaced with the qualified instructors already in the classrooms presenting a truly comprehensive evidence-based sex ed program.
