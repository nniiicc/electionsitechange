Flock Flak “Mom, Mom, it’s a Flock camera!” As I entered the parking lot of a popular big box store, I looked over and noticed a thin pole whiz by.
After parking, much to the embarrassment of my children, I approached the camera, trying to understand a little more about it.
Clearly, it was positioned on the store property.
Moreover, it was aimed at the parking lot of the store.
Was this the government surveillance my children were trying to warn me about?
I took a picture and approached the store where plenty of other store cameras were aimed at me from the store perspective.
Then, I entered the store, and asked for the manager.
After a little bit of time, and what seemed like some low-toned conversations over the phone, a manager approached and kindly answered my questions.
No, neither the police, nor the government owned it or any of the others on the property.
These Flock cameras belonged to the store.
After listening to their explanation, I understood that the cameras were really used to guard against theft and had been in place for a few years already.
I thanked the manager, bought what we needed, then headed home to do more research.
While I accept that store owners will film me on their property, the problem with Flock cameras in several areas is that the police departments and municipalities include budget lines, and therefore access, to them.
I don’t believe this was the case at the big box store or the municipality where I was, but I balk at the idea that the government would pay for them, even for the public areas, as they currently are.
For the uninitiated, Flock cameras, or scanners known as “automated license plate reader” or ALPRs, use exceptional technology to capture, retain, and filter/sort license plate numbers which can be shared across their vast network of 120,000 cameras and their servers.
If you’re a criminal, then ALPRs are some of your worst enemies, but if you’re an innocent person, ALPRs aren’t your friends either because that retention of information can be used by unscrupulous people to track down: ex-lovers, or people trying to obtain an abortion in a different state, or just cataloguing people’s patterns in everyday life.
For Americans who value privacy, this is an egregious overreach by The State, especially as no third party is involved in the oversight of ALPRs to prevent abuse of the information.
To resolve the first issue, Flock agreed to retain the information of innocent people for only 7 days, but they have various exceptions they can deploy to override that for longer, and since people have abused the data, I believe the ACLU’s recommendations of 48 hours and assigning a third party enforcer of standards of privacy, are far better.
In addition, I agree with Representative Thomas Massie (R-KY)’s proposal to block grants from the Department of Justice and Department of Homeland Security to the local municipalities and police departments for their purchase of ALPR’s.
However, I support private businesses/corporations that want to invest their own money in such devices.
It has (almost) always been our choice to step onto private/corporate-owned property or not.
The public lands are a different matter and should be held to standards that are in keeping with our 4th Amendment Right which reads,”The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” Together, we #ChooseDemocracy and our Constitutional Rights!
