Make sure every Tennessee mother survives childbirth, with doulas, group prenatal care, addiction treatment, and rural telehealth in the counties where the most mothers are dying.
Pregnancy-associated deaths in Tennessee more than doubled from 63 in 2019 to 134 in 2021 and still stood at 96 in 2023.
The state's own Maternal Mortality Review Committee found roughly three-quarters of them preventable.
Black women died at 2.5 times the rate of white women.
More than a third of these deaths happen between 43 days and a year after delivery, long after most mothers have lost contact with the healthcare system.
Tennessee extended TennCare coverage to twelve months postpartum, and coverage on paper means nothing if the services aren't there.
We'll cover doulas through Medicaid, where a review of 26 trials found continuous labor support cuts cesarean deliveries by about 25 percent.
Group prenatal care gets paid for, and addiction treatment goes inside prenatal care in East Tennessee, which accounts for 56 percent of the state's maternal deaths from substance use.
No woman should have to choose between getting help and keeping her child.

Repeal Tennessee's private school voucher and spend every dollar of it on the working conditions that decide whether good teachers stay in the schools that lose them fastest.
Tennessee has been filling its classrooms by lowering the bar to enter them.
Unfilled positions fell from 1,434 in fall 2023 to 817 in fall 2024 while the state issued 6,579 emergency credentials in a single year.
About 13 percent of Tennessee teachers leave public school classrooms annually, against 2,966 who finish a state preparation program.
We'll repeal the Education Freedom Scholarship program and spend all $303.9 million a year of it on what decides whether a qualified teacher is in front of a Tennessee classroom next fall.
A hundred and eleven of the state's 140 districts have no school social worker at all, so we'll hire counselors and social workers toward the 250-to-1 standard, starting where vacancies and emergency credentials run highest.
Every teacher in their first two years gets two full years of trained coaching, because one year of it produces nothing.
Teachers who stay tell the state they stay for staff culture and school leadership.
We'll pay for both.

Get people off the street and into real housing with support at the door, and fund a sober recovery track for those who need a clean environment to heal.
About 2,000 Tennesseans are chronically homeless, up from roughly 1,700 the year before, cycling through emergency rooms, psychiatric hospitals, jails, and shelters at $30,000 to $50,000 a person a year with zero housing outcome.
The answer is well proven: put people in apartments with clinical support and keep them there.
In a five-city randomized trial, 73 percent of high-need Housing First participants were stably housed at twelve months against 31 percent under treatment as usual.
Savings in shelters and emergency services offset 69 percent of the cost.
We'll hold funded providers to the full model: scattered-site apartments, standard leases, no treatment preconditions, and services for as long as they're needed.
Programs that water those elements down house fewer people.
Housing First is clear about its limits, and the trials show it neither worsens nor improves addiction itself.
So we'll fund a sober track alongside it, with first-year recovery housing vouchers and 300 new certified recovery residence beds.

Send a behavioral health professional to every mental health crisis call, by funding city co-responder and alternative-response teams and stationing clinicians in 911 dispatch.
Tennessee cities already built the working models.
Memphis invented Crisis Intervention Team training in 1988, the year after police shot a man in crisis.
Nashville runs a police co-responder program and, separately, a fire-based alternative-response program.
Knoxville's co-responder teams resolved 97 percent of interactions without an arrest in 2024, with zero uses of force, and the city voted to expand from four teams to six.
The job is to scale what Tennessee cities have proven into more cities and into 911 itself.
We'll offer competitive matching grants to cities expanding crisis response, each city free to choose its model.
The grants fund the expansion margin rather than existing operations, and each requires outcome reporting and response-time standards.
We'll station behavioral health clinicians inside metro 911 dispatch so a crisis gets routed to a crisis team at the point of the call.
Far from defunding the police, this frees officers to focus on real crime.
Let cops be cops.

Get addiction treatment into every county, meet every overdose survivor with a same-day path into treatment, and fund the year of recovery that follows.
Tennessee lost roughly 2,600 people to drug overdoses in 2024, seven a day, more than car accidents or gun violence.
The state has 23 licensed opioid treatment programs, the only clinics allowed to dispense methadone, for all 95 counties.
Arresting people out of addiction has never worked.
Treatment does.
We'll expand medication treatment to every county on Vermont's hub-and-spoke model, which pairs regional specialty hubs with local clinic spokes so patients get care close to home.
On top of settlement dollars, we'll put new state money where treatment doesn't exist today.
Surviving an overdose should be the moment treatment starts.
We'll begin the medication in the emergency room at the forty Tennessee hospitals seeing the most overdoses, open ninety respite beds so anyone who says yes has a safe bed that day, fund a thousand recovery housing vouchers for the year after, and make naloxone free in every fire station and school.
Every person pulled back from addiction is a parent who comes home, a worker who shows up, a neighbor who contributes.

Put trauma-informed training, peer support, and wellness services within reach of every Tennessee police department, so officers don't break under the weight of the worst calls.
We send police officers into the worst moments of people's lives, murders, fatal accidents, suicides, active threats, and then act surprised when the job breaks them.
Officers experience post-traumatic stress and depression at up to five times the rate of the general population, and in most years more officers die by suicide than are killed in the line of duty.
Nearly a quarter of officers report suicidal ideation over a career.
Only about one in six sought mental health care in the past year, though one in four screened positive for symptoms.
Burned-out officers make worse decisions under pressure, and communities pay for that.
We'll set a statewide trauma-informed training standard and fund competitive wellness grants, so peer support and confidential counseling are within reach of every department at every rank.
Nashville's police already run free counseling, critical incident support, and a 24-hour crisis line for officers and their families.
Every department in Tennessee should have that.

Get fast mental health help to Tennesseans in crisis, by hiring 160 clinicians, expanding 24/7 crisis response to every county, and opening three crisis stabilization centers.
Tennessee's crisis response network covers all 95 counties on paper.
In practice it cannot meet the demand, and in rural counties response times lag while clinical positions stay vacant.
Tennessee ranks 44th in the country for mental health, with about 52 mental health workers for every 10,000 residents.
Nearly two-thirds of young people with major depression get no treatment at all.
We'll add roughly 160 clinical positions to the community mental health centers the state contracts with, and open three 16-bed crisis stabilization units in Nashville, Memphis, and Knoxville, using renovated or shared space rather than new construction.
That gives someone in psychiatric crisis somewhere to go besides an emergency department.
We'll take mobile crisis teams to round-the-clock coverage statewide, adding new rural satellite teams, and pay loan repayment to clinicians who commit to crisis work.
That is how we staff what we build.
