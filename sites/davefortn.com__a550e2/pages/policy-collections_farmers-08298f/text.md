Free breakfast and lunch for every kid in Tennessee's highest-need schools, sourced from Tennessee farmers and cooked in upgraded school kitchens.
More than one in five Tennessee children lives in a food-insecure household.
In March 2025 the federal government cancelled the local food programs that had already put more than $12 million into Tennessee schools, food banks, and farms.
The state has no replacement plan, so this is the replacement.
Tennessee already counts which of its schools are poorest.
Federal rules let 242 of them feed every student free today, and not one has signed up.
The rules leave part of the meal to whoever will cover it, and nobody does.
We'll cover it.
In year one we'll serve free breakfast and lunch in the 100 highest-need of those schools, and by year three all 242, about 202,000 kids, paid for out of money the state now sends to private schools.
We'll take the federal summer grocery benefit Tennessee refused in 2025 and again in 2026, and we'll fund kitchen grants of $50,000 to $200,000 so schools can cook from fresh ingredients instead of reheating.

Fund crisis services, rural mental health telehealth, and peer support networks for Tennessee farmers, who, according to the National Rural Health Association, die by suicide at up to 3.5 times the rate of the general population.
Tennessee farms are dwindling, down to just over 60,000 farms, and we're projected to lose over a million acres of farmland by 2040.
If we want to save these farms, we need to save the farmers first.
According to the National Rural Health Association, farmers and ranchers die by suicide at up to 3.5 times the rate of the general population.
With farm debt approaching record levels, crop prices down roughly a third since 2022, and 70% of rural counties without a single psychiatrist, stress can turn into despair.
Farmers shouldn't have to face that alone.
The Tennessee Suicide Prevention Network established a Farmers Task Force in 2018, but we still need real infrastructure: agriculture-specific crisis services, expanded rural mental health through telehealth, and peer support networks modeled on programs working in Wisconsin and North Carolina.
Reaching out for help isn't weakness, it's a strength.
Tennessee needs to make sure that help is actually there when farmers need it.

Build food hubs, cold storage, and procurement preferences that keep Tennessee-grown food in Tennessee, and provide tariff buffer grants so trade disruptions don't wipe out Tennessee farmers.
Farm bankruptcy filings under the federal provision written for farmers rose 46 percent nationally in 2025, and 69 percent across the Southeast.
Tennessee has rich agricultural land and has grown dependent on supply chains that stretch thousands of miles, and when those chains break, our communities suffer.
We'll build two regional food hubs with cold storage so Tennessee-grown food stays in Tennessee, and require state institutions, including schools, hospitals, and state facilities, to buy Tennessee-grown first, with the state funding the price difference so school food budgets are held harmless.
Pick Tennessee Products already has nearly 3,200 members.
We'll scale it into a statewide brand with dedicated marketing and retail partnerships, the way Kentucky Proud reached 70 to 80 percent recognition.
And we'll issue tariff buffer grants at roughly $4.60 an acre across Tennessee's 2,775,000 acres of tariff-exposed row crops, reaching the crop diversification, value-added processing, and specialty crops federal aid misses.

Make land access easier for new Tennessee farmers, with state cost-share for land leases, property-tax relief for small operations, and beginning-farmer lease provisions in conservation easements.
More than half of beginning farmers nationwide leave agriculture within their first decade, and Tennessee is no exception.
The barrier is land, and a landowner sitting on acreage has no particular reason to lease it to someone unproven.
Tennessee already runs the pieces: equipment cost-share through the Agricultural Enhancement Program, Extension training in all 95 counties, a matching service that connects new farmers to available land, and a $25 million fund for permanent conservation easements.
What's missing is a reason for the landowner to say yes.
Nebraska and Minnesota solved that with income tax credits, and Tennessee has no income tax, so our tools have to be different.
We'll add land lease cost-share to the Agricultural Enhancement Program, lower the acreage minimum in the Greenbelt law so small operations qualify for agricultural use-value assessment on their property taxes, and direct farmland preservation money toward easements that include beginning-farmer lease provisions.
Every one of those runs through a program Tennessee already has.
Farms & Food Climate Action Collapse ↑ Pay Farmers to Restore Land Tennessee farmers and landowners get paid for practices that build soil carbon, maintain tree cover, and keep nitrogen and phosphorus out of rivers and streams.

Tennessee farmers and landowners get paid for practices that build soil carbon, maintain tree cover, and keep nitrogen and phosphorus out of rivers and streams.
Tennessee farmers lead the country in no-till and reduced tillage, which they use on 93 percent of our cropland tillage acres.
Even so, most landowners receive little or nothing for keeping that land a carbon sink.
The practices are there; the payments aren't.
We'll start those payments.
Farmers who plant cover crops, cut back on plowing, or rotate their grazing will earn the federal rate, which for cover crops is $74.10 an acre, up from the $50 the state pays today.
We'll pay for 50,000 acres and skip any acre that already draws federal cost-share, so every dollar is new money in farmers' pockets.
We'll also protect the land itself by making the state's farmland preservation fund permanent and putting $40 to $60 million into it over four years.
Those dollars will become Tennessee's match for a federal easement program, so each state dollar buys roughly two dollars of easement.
That way the farmers who already do this work get paid for it, and the land that feeds us stays farmland.

Tennessee farmers can fix their own tractors and combines or hire a local mechanic, with manufacturers required to provide diagnostic tools, parts, and software on fair terms.
For years the major equipment manufacturers locked their diagnostic software, restricted access to parts, and steered every repair back through their own dealers.
Federal regulators sued John Deere over it, and in July 2026 the company accepted a ten-year settlement giving farmers and independent shops the same repair tools its dealers get.
That settlement is one company for ten years.
The nearest authorized dealer may still be hours from the field, and the wait can still cost a crop.
We'll pass a right-to-repair statute covering every manufacturer that sells agricultural equipment in Tennessee, requiring diagnostic tools, parts, software, and documentation on fair and reasonable terms.
Federal law already says choosing your own mechanic cannot void a warranty, and our statute makes that promise enforceable in a Tennessee court.
A manufacturer keeps every right it has to refuse a claim for damage an improper repair caused.
It costs the state nothing, and it's a bill we can pass on day one.
