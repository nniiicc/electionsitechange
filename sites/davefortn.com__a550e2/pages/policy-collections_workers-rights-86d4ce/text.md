In 2022, one in four of Tennessee's largest corporations, 95 of the 370 reporting over $1 billion in income, paid zero state excise tax ([Tennessee Department of Revenue data](https://www.wkrn.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/73/2026/03/Tennessee-Corporate-Tax-Loophole-Report.pdf)).
We'll require worldwide combined reporting and restore the franchise tax base the legislature gave away in 2024, redesigned to hold up in court, so every corporation profiting in Tennessee pays its share.
Between 2019 and 2022, an average of 63 percent of the corporations filing in Tennessee paid nothing in excise tax.
Many of them paid the franchise tax as well, and for most the bill was the $100 minimum.
Tennessee closed the old Delaware shell loophole twenty years ago.
What the code still permits is quieter: a company earns money here, books it to an affiliate offshore, and the state taxes none of it, because Tennessee still lets related corporations file their returns as though they were strangers.
We'll require every multistate corporation doing business here to report its worldwide group's income and pay on Tennessee's share, apportioned by actual activity in the state.
Twenty-eight states and the District of Columbia already require this within U.S. borders, and profit-shifting doesn't stop at the water's edge.
We'll also restore the franchise tax base the legislature gave away in 2024, rebuilt to survive the constitutional objection raised against the old version.
Between $539 million and $1.3 billion a year, and it pays for taking the tax off groceries.

Restore independent civil rights enforcement, let cities pass their own nondiscrimination ordinances, and enact statewide protections in employment, housing, and public accommodations covering sexual orientation and gender identity.
In 2025 Tennessee dissolved its independent Human Rights Commission and folded enforcement into the Attorney General's office, and pending cases were dismissed in the transition.
Federal enforcement shrank at the same time, so Tennesseans who face discrimination are left with fewer and weaker places to turn.
No state law here stops a landlord from refusing an apartment, or a restaurant or hospital from turning someone away, over sexual orientation or gender identity.
In employment only a federal backstop remains, and it does not reach employers with fewer than fifteen workers or offer any remedy in a Tennessee court.
Twenty-one states and Washington, D.C. have enacted full protections.
We'll repeal the 2011 law that stops Nashville, Knoxville, Memphis, and Chattanooga from protecting their own residents, enact statewide protections in employment, housing, public accommodations, and lending, and restore enforcement that is genuinely independent.
Without that, protections are promises written on paper.

Raise Tennessee's minimum wage to $21 an hour over four years, starting at $12 the first year, and let cities set their own wage floors above the state level.
Tennessee has no minimum wage of its own.
The floor here is the federal $7.25, unchanged since 2009.
The MIT Living Wage Calculator puts what each of two working parents needs to raise a child in this state at nearly $20 an hour.
We'll close that distance on a published schedule: $12 the first year, $15 the second, $18 the third, and $21 the fourth, indexed to inflation after that so the floor holds its value.
California raised fast food wages to $20, and researchers at Harvard and Berkeley found no drop in jobs, hours, or benefits, though other economists read the data differently.
Small businesses get tax credits, technical assistance, and simpler rules while they adjust.
We'll also repeal the 2013 law that forbids Tennessee cities from setting their own wage floors.
If the legislature drags its feet on $21, the voters of Nashville, Memphis, and Chattanooga shouldn't have to wait for it.
Jobs, Wages & Innovation Economic Wellbeing Collapse ↑ Back Main Street We'll fund the training partnership law Tennessee already has, hold employers to the wages they commit to, and back the downtowns that anchor our small towns.

We'll fund the training partnership law Tennessee already has, hold employers to the wages they commit to, and back the downtowns that anchor our small towns.
Eighty-seven percent of private-sector job creation is homegrown; companies moving across state lines account for three percent.
Tennessee's economic development money leans the other way, and the state law built to line up training with local employers has not funded a grant round since 2016.
We'll fund it.
Nine or ten regional partnerships will pair community colleges and Colleges of Applied Technology with employers in advanced manufacturing, healthcare, and construction.
Every employer signs a hiring commitment at named wages before a dollar moves.
San Antonio's version of this raised participant earnings by about $6,000 a year, with gains still showing twelve years on.
Federal food assistance reimburses half of every eligible state dollar spent training participants, with no ceiling, and Tennessee leaves about $3.7 million of that unclaimed every year.
We'll claim it.
Beyond that, we'll add 15 communities to the Main Street program, weighted toward rural counties, and put two more small business centers and eight advisors into rural Tennessee.
All of it runs about $10.5 million a year, $9.5 million of it state money.

Get up to 12 weeks of partial pay to care for a new baby, a sick family member, or your own serious illness, paid for by a small shared payroll premium.
Tennessee already gives its own employees twelve weeks of paid parental leave.
Every other working Tennessean is on their own when a baby arrives, a parent is dying, or a diagnosis takes them out of work for months.
Colorado stood its program up in three years and paid more than 135,000 claims worth $687 million in the first year alone.
The premium there started at 0.9 percent of wages, split evenly between employer and employee, and has since dropped to 0.88 percent because the program came in under budget.
Small employers are exempt from the employer half, and we'll write that exemption into ours.
On Tennessee's $242 billion wage base, that premium raises roughly $2 billion a year, which funds every benefit and the cost of running the program.
The state's part is a $57.5 million loan to extend the claims platform the Department of Labor already runs, repaid out of premiums once collections begin.
It's insurance: every working Tennessean pays in, and every working Tennessean is covered when they need it most.
Childcare & Families Economic Wellbeing Collapse ↑ End the Benefits Cliff Working Tennesseans keep more of every raise, with childcare and health coverage that phase out gradually instead of vanishing overnight.
Explore → Copied!
Working Tennesseans keep more of every raise, with childcare and health coverage that phase out gradually instead of vanishing overnight.
In a survey of more than 200 lower-income Tennessee families, 85 percent had hit a benefits cliff, half said they had resisted better-paying work, and one in four had turned down a raise.
A raise that costs a family its childcare subsidy leaves them worse off than before.
We'll take the cliffs out one rule at a time.
On health coverage, we'll hold eligibility steady for twelve months at a time so a raise in March doesn't end coverage in April, and use sliding-scale premiums where the rule today is a hard cutoff.
On cash assistance, we'll replace the flat $250 earnings deduction, the sharpest cliff Tennessee controls outright, with a graduated disregard that lets a parent work more hours and come out ahead.
Tennessee is re-procuring the system that decides who qualifies for TennCare, the software where every one of these rules actually takes effect.
We'll write configurable benefit calculation into that solicitation before it goes out, rather than buying that capability later at a change order's price.

Hire Tennessee workers and contractors first for every state-funded construction and infrastructure project.
When Tennessee builds, Tennesseans should build it.
A 2016 state law blocks cities like Nashville from the local-hire programs their own voters approved and bars the state itself from preferring Tennessee workers on state-funded projects.
We'll replace it with a local workforce law for state-funded projects over $500,000.
Contractors will meet hours-based Tennessee-workforce goals, with a good-faith off-ramp instead of punitive debarment.
The goals target economic disadvantage rather than residency mandates.
Built-in safeguards include published and objective bid scoring, a neutral first-source referral center so no official ever hand-picks a hire, certified-payroll reporting with an annual public compliance report, and an apprenticeship floor that turns projects into careers.
This way every dollar Tennessee spends building its own infrastructure can come back as a Tennessee paycheck.

Build Tennessee companies from Tennessee research by co-funding shared lab equipment at our research universities and matching researchers' first dollars when they're ready to commercialize.
Tennessee has the research universities, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and one of the country's largest healthcare industry clusters.
What it doesn't have is the connective tissue between a breakthrough in a lab here and a company here that hires people here.
Georgia has spent about $20 million a year on that problem since 1991 and reports more than $16 billion in research grants, venture capital, and matching funds drawn into the state.
We'll start at a third of that, $7 million a year, aimed only at the hard sciences: medicine, advanced manufacturing, agriculture, and transportation.
When one of our universities recruits a leading researcher, we'll match its investment in lab equipment dollar for dollar, and the state keeps title, so if the researcher leaves, the lab stays.
A researcher ready to commercialize gets matching grants for proof-of-concept work, and we'll take no equity, no board seat, and no share of revenue.
The grant is repaid only if the company is acquired or goes public.
