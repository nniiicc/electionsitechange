The word "Tennessee" comes from Tanasi, a Cherokee town that now lies under Tellico Reservoir.
At Red Clay the Cherokee Nation held its last councils before removal.
Roughly 8,000 people were held at Fort Cass before being forced west, about one in four of them dying in the camps, on the trail, or soon after arrival.
One piece of that history is sitting in the United States Senate right now.
A bill returning 76 acres of ancestral land near Vonore to tribal trust, including the Sequoyah Birthplace Museum and the Chota and Tanasi memorials, passed the House by voice vote without recorded opposition and has gone nowhere since.
Supporting it costs Tennessee nothing, and we'll push it across.
Beyond that bill, we'll give Indigenous sacred sites real enforceable protection in state law, work with the State Board of Education so every Tennessee student learns this as Tennessee history, and accelerate the return of remains and sacred objects.
The University of Tennessee alone reports more than 3,000 ancestors still waiting to go home.

The polluters wrote ten-figure settlement checks, the state pointed towns toward loan programs while every primary claim window closed in 2026, and we'll ring-fence Tennessee's recoveries in a cleanup fund that pays for treatment and testing.
Between 2023 and 2025 Tennessee sampled 607 public water systems and found PFAS, the chemicals that don't break down in water or in people, in 407 of them, 34 of those above the federal limit.
Two national settlements were paying out at the time, 3M's $10.3 billion package and $1.185 billion from Chemours, DuPont, and Corteva.
The state's own guidance pointed those communities toward loan programs and never mentioned the settlements.
Every primary claim window closed in 2026.
What remains is a supplemental fund open through December 2030 for systems that file within 45 days of a new qualifying test.
So we'll require the state to notify every water system within five business days of a result that opens a window, and to publish a running list of open deadlines.
Tennessee's Attorney General has sued more than 20 PFAS manufacturers.
We'll ring-fence every recovery in a cleanup fund that pays for treatment, gets PFAS firefighting foam out of Tennessee fire stations, and tests the wells of the one in ten Tennesseans nobody monitors.

A bipartisan fix cleared committee but died behind the budget, so we'll make sure it's in the budget.
Hunters and anglers pay for much of Tennessee's wildlife agency, and license fees haven't risen since 2015 while the agency's costs have climbed more than 30 percent.
In 2025, the agency withdrew a fee increase after legislators promised dedicated funding instead.
That funding never came, and now the agency is seeking an increase averaging 29 percent.
We'll deliver that funding.
Our wildlife agency will get steady money of its own at no new tax or fee, and conservation money will go back to conservation.
We'll send 20 percent of the state's Tennessee Valley Authority payments in place of property taxes straight to the wildlife fund, roughly $33.6 million a year, without touching what counties and cities receive.
The first roughly $11 million each year will match our full share of federal wildlife grants.
Only $2.2 million of this year's $23.5 million wetlands fund is left for wetlands, so we'll repeal the 2025 changes that let it cover payroll and have the Comptroller certify publicly every year that our conservation revenues stayed with their purpose.
We'll also put $120 million over the term into the Heritage Conservation Trust Fund, which buys land and got nothing this year, renew the trust before it expires, and publish an independent assessment of state parks' upkeep needs.
Those of us who hunt and fish have paid for this agency for generations and were told we wouldn't have to pay more.
This funding keeps that promise and keeps the wild places we all share funded for good.

Tennessee farmers and landowners get paid for practices that build soil carbon, maintain tree cover, and keep nitrogen and phosphorus out of rivers and streams.
Tennessee farmers lead the country in no-till and reduced tillage, which they use on 93 percent of our cropland tillage acres.
Even so, most landowners receive little or nothing for keeping that land a carbon sink.
The practices are there; the payments aren't.
We'll start those payments.
Farmers who plant cover crops, cut back on plowing, or rotate their grazing will earn the federal rate, which for cover crops is $74.10 an acre, up from the $50 the state pays today.
We'll pay for 50,000 acres and skip any acre that already draws federal cost-share, so every dollar is new money in farmers' pockets.
We'll also protect the land itself by making the state's farmland preservation fund permanent and putting $40 to $60 million into it over four years.
Those dollars will become Tennessee's match for a federal easement program, so each state dollar buys roughly two dollars of easement.
That way the farmers who already do this work get paid for it, and the land that feeds us stays farmland.

Tennessee's rivers hold more freshwater species than almost anywhere on Earth, and we have one of the largest national forests facing a logging threat, so we'll protect both and build a wildlife corridor action plan.
Tennessee's forests, rivers, and elevations are what make our state's outdoors so unique, and each is under pressure.
We'll put state and federal dollars behind all three.
The forest is most urgent.
Cherokee is Tennessee's only national forest, and most of its 660,000 acres are fast-tracked for expanded logging.
We'll oppose that publicly and file state comments on every proposed timber sale.
Next our rivers hold more freshwater species than almost anywhere on Earth.
The Cumberland River Aquatic Center had produced 170,990 juvenile mussels across 17 species by early 2021 and has kept producing since.
The work is limited by money, so we'll draw down every federal dollar available, up to 75 percent of planning and 65 percent of implementation, and scale recovery across the Tennessee and Cumberland river systems.
Lastly, we'll help protected places succeed by keeping them connected.
Tennessee has no corridor plan and no crossing program, and never applied to the $350 million federal program that seven other Appalachian states have used.
We'll fund a plan ourselves and seek federal dollars to tie Cherokee to the Cumberland Plateau and West Tennessee's bottomland hardwoods, so what we protect can be held together.

The fund that's supposed to fix that is sitting on a $24 million balance.
Spend the uncommitted portion.
Tennessee buries about a million tons of recyclable material every year, worth roughly $150 million, well short of the state's own 25 percent waste-reduction goal.
Middle Tennessee's largest landfill for household garbage is projected to reach capacity around 2029, and the industry's answer is to site new mega-landfills on rural counties.
We'll do the opposite and spend the recycling money Tennessee already collects.
The Solid Waste Management Fund took in $11.6 million in 2024 and disbursed only $8.8 million, closing the year on a $24.2 million balance.
We'll accelerate grants from the fund's uncommitted portion into drop-off convenience centers, recycling equipment, and composting grants in the rural counties with the worst access.
At least 55 counties and 18 cities have adopted the law letting a community reject a new landfill, and a 2026 state task force is trying to weaken the law's siting criteria at the waste industry's urging.
We'll protect that authority and publish model host-community-agreement standards so counties consenting to host landfills stop getting shortchanged.

We'll publish an annual Wellbeing Report and Environmental Quality Report in plain English, showing whether Tennesseans are measurably better off than last year and at what cost to the land and water we pass on.
Tennessee's agencies produce good data.
Environment and Conservation runs daily-updated viewers on water, air, and site cleanup; Health has surveyed adult health every year since 1984; and the Comptroller added an economic-incentives dashboard in 2025.
Every agency measures its own corner, and nobody puts the picture together.
We'll create a Tennessee Outcomes Commissioner under the Comptroller, on a six-year term that spans gubernatorial transitions, whose job is synthesis rather than collecting anything new.
The Commissioner publishes a Wellbeing Report tracking economic security, health, education, housing, and opportunity across all 95 counties.
An Environmental Quality Report tracks water quality, air quality, soil health, forest cover, and species at risk across the state's major watersheds.
A cap of 30 indicators across both reports, written into the law, keeps them readable.
The Comptroller audits the methodology, the legislature holds an annual hearing on the findings, and the Governor addresses them in the budget message.
Democracy & Government Public Accountability Collapse ↑ Keep Power Public Keep TVA public, put enforceable conditions on the nuclear money Tennessee is spending, and end Tennessee's role as a destination for the nation's radioactive waste.

Keep TVA public, put enforceable conditions on the nuclear money Tennessee is spending, and end Tennessee's role as a destination for the nation's radioactive waste.
The Tennessee Valley Authority is a public utility and must stay that way.
No privatization, no sell-off; the only transfer we'd accept is of our portion of the grid to Tennessee itself.
Through TVA, Tennessee is already a nuclear state.
The Authority runs four reactors at Sequoyah and Watts Bar that generate enough baseload power for more than 4.5 million homes, and is developing a small modular reactor at Clinch River on a $400 million federal grant with up to $50 million in state money.
But the state doesn't license reactors, so we'll control what we can and insist on accountability: that $50 million will come with quarterly public cost reporting, overrun caps with clawbacks, ratepayer protection so overruns stay off power bills, and a full public accounting of waste management and decommissioning.
Tennessee also hosts more licensed radioactive-waste processors than any other state, and processed waste has been reaching ordinary household landfills, a practice we must end immediately.
We'll tighten release standards, require public notice and county consent first, track every out-of-state and foreign shipment, and pay the state's radiological inspectors from fees paid by the industry's use of our land.
