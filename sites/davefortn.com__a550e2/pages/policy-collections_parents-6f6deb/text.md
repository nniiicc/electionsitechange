Free breakfast and lunch for every kid in Tennessee's highest-need schools, sourced from Tennessee farmers and cooked in upgraded school kitchens.
More than one in five Tennessee children lives in a food-insecure household.
In March 2025 the federal government cancelled the local food programs that had already put more than $12 million into Tennessee schools, food banks, and farms.
The state has no replacement plan, so this is the replacement.
Tennessee already counts which of its schools are poorest.
Federal rules let 242 of them feed every student free today, and not one has signed up.
The rules leave part of the meal to whoever will cover it, and nobody does.
We'll cover it.
In year one we'll serve free breakfast and lunch in the 100 highest-need of those schools, and by year three all 242, about 202,000 kids, paid for out of money the state now sends to private schools.
We'll take the federal summer grocery benefit Tennessee refused in 2025 and again in 2026, and we'll fund kitchen grants of $50,000 to $200,000 so schools can cook from fresh ingredients instead of reheating.

Make sure every Tennessee mother survives childbirth, with doulas, group prenatal care, addiction treatment, and rural telehealth in the counties where the most mothers are dying.
Pregnancy-associated deaths in Tennessee more than doubled from 63 in 2019 to 134 in 2021 and still stood at 96 in 2023.
The state's own Maternal Mortality Review Committee found roughly three-quarters of them preventable.
Black women died at 2.5 times the rate of white women.
More than a third of these deaths happen between 43 days and a year after delivery, long after most mothers have lost contact with the healthcare system.
Tennessee extended TennCare coverage to twelve months postpartum, and coverage on paper means nothing if the services aren't there.
We'll cover doulas through Medicaid, where a review of 26 trials found continuous labor support cuts cesarean deliveries by about 25 percent.
Group prenatal care gets paid for, and addiction treatment goes inside prenatal care in East Tennessee, which accounts for 56 percent of the state's maternal deaths from substance use.
No woman should have to choose between getting help and keeping her child.

A sovereign wealth fund for Tennessee's kids, paid for by the wealthiest Tennesseans, that never spends its principal and buys our schools what the state never has.
Tennessee ranks dead last in the country on school spending, about $12,100 a student against a national average near $19,400.
Our kids didn't create that, and they can't fix it.
We'll create the Future Families Fund, a state-owned sovereign wealth fund that never spends its principal, built the way Alaska built its Permanent Fund.
Five sources pay in: a 15 percent cannabis tax, a higher cigarette tax, a contribution on estates above $5 million, a 7 percent contribution on capital gains above $1 million, and voluntary gifts.
We'll seed it with $1.25 billion of last year's surplus.
What the state spends each year is a draw set by formula against the Fund's trailing market value, and everything earned above it is reinvested.
Statute will bar that draw from vouchers, from sectarian schools, and from any line the school funding formula already pays.
The first term's draw goes to child care and finishes it.
We'll put the Fund in the constitution so no future legislature can reach the principal.

Cover Tennessee's poorest adults now through a federal waiver, and expand TennCare fully the day Washington confirms that expanding won't cut the hospital fund.
About 91,000 Tennesseans earn too much for TennCare and too little for any marketplace help at all.
Every year Tennessee doesn't act, federal dollars Tennessee taxpayers already paid go to other states instead.
We'll file a Section 1115 waiver, the federal permission that lets a state run Medicaid its own way, and cover every adult below the poverty line at Tennessee's regular match.
Wisconsin has run that design for a decade under governors of both parties.
Full expansion covers more people for less apiece, and for states that expand, the 2025 federal budget law phases down the hospital assessment that finances TennCare.
With 61 percent of Tennessee's rural hospitals already vulnerable to closure, we won't trade the financing that keeps them open for a better match on fewer people.
The day Washington confirms that penalty doesn't reach a newly expanding Tennessee, TennCare expands to 138 percent of poverty.
We'll add the adult vision benefit TennCare still won't pay for either way.

Repeal Tennessee's private school voucher and spend every dollar of it on the working conditions that decide whether good teachers stay in the schools that lose them fastest.
Tennessee has been filling its classrooms by lowering the bar to enter them.
Unfilled positions fell from 1,434 in fall 2023 to 817 in fall 2024 while the state issued 6,579 emergency credentials in a single year.
About 13 percent of Tennessee teachers leave public school classrooms annually, against 2,966 who finish a state preparation program.
We'll repeal the Education Freedom Scholarship program and spend all $303.9 million a year of it on what decides whether a qualified teacher is in front of a Tennessee classroom next fall.
A hundred and eleven of the state's 140 districts have no school social worker at all, so we'll hire counselors and social workers toward the 250-to-1 standard, starting where vacancies and emergency credentials run highest.
Every teacher in their first two years gets two full years of trained coaching, because one year of it produces nothing.
Teachers who stay tell the state they stay for staff culture and school leadership.
We'll pay for both.

Build food hubs, cold storage, and procurement preferences that keep Tennessee-grown food in Tennessee, and provide tariff buffer grants so trade disruptions don't wipe out Tennessee farmers.
Farm bankruptcy filings under the federal provision written for farmers rose 46 percent nationally in 2025, and 69 percent across the Southeast.
Tennessee has rich agricultural land and has grown dependent on supply chains that stretch thousands of miles, and when those chains break, our communities suffer.
We'll build two regional food hubs with cold storage so Tennessee-grown food stays in Tennessee, and require state institutions, including schools, hospitals, and state facilities, to buy Tennessee-grown first, with the state funding the price difference so school food budgets are held harmless.
Pick Tennessee Products already has nearly 3,200 members.
We'll scale it into a statewide brand with dedicated marketing and retail partnerships, the way Kentucky Proud reached 70 to 80 percent recognition.
And we'll issue tariff buffer grants at roughly $4.60 an acre across Tennessee's 2,775,000 acres of tariff-exposed row crops, reaching the crop diversification, value-added processing, and specialty crops federal aid misses.

Tennessee seniors age in their own homes, with better-paid home health aides, senior nutrition, senior transportation, and elder abuse prevention.
Tennessee still sends about 48 percent of its Medicaid long-term care dollars to institutions, against about 36 percent nationally, and ranks 47th in the country on long-term care.
Nearly 14,000 TennCare members live in nursing facilities, and by 2040 Tennesseans 85 and over will grow 72 percent to 205,000.
The state cannot build nursing homes fast enough to meet that, and it cannot afford to.
Tennessee pays about $93,000 a year for a nursing facility resident and about $40,000 for someone served at home.
We'll fund 400 transitions out of facilities every year on top of what the state already does, publish an annual target, and report the count quarterly.
The moving allowance has sat at a $2,000 lifetime cap, and we'll raise it.
Home health aides here earn about $16 an hour, below the national average, while turnover runs near 80 percent.
We'll raise their pay, build pathways from aide to licensed nurse, and fund the meals, transportation, and elder abuse prevention that make home viable.

Every Tennessee kid gets full medical, dental, vision, and mental health coverage, no matter what their family earns.
CoverKids already insures Tennessee children up to 250 percent of the federal poverty level.
We'll raise that to 300 percent, the highest income at which federal law still pays Tennessee's enhanced match under the Children's Health Insurance Program.
At that match Washington pays about three of every four dollars, more than double the state's share under regular Medicaid.
Kids are the cheapest people in the healthcare system to cover, about $3,600 a year for a Tennessee child, a third less than a working-age adult.
Children in working families who earn just above today's cutoff get full medical, dental, vision, and mental health coverage, and businesses now covering dependents take a real expense off their books.
Beginning in October 2026, federal rules narrow which lawfully present immigrant children draw that match.
We'll cover those children with state dollars rather than let Washington's rules leave them behind.

Get up to 12 weeks of partial pay to care for a new baby, a sick family member, or your own serious illness, paid for by a small shared payroll premium.
Tennessee already gives its own employees twelve weeks of paid parental leave.
Every other working Tennessean is on their own when a baby arrives, a parent is dying, or a diagnosis takes them out of work for months.
Colorado stood its program up in three years and paid more than 135,000 claims worth $687 million in the first year alone.
The premium there started at 0.9 percent of wages, split evenly between employer and employee, and has since dropped to 0.88 percent because the program came in under budget.
Small employers are exempt from the employer half, and we'll write that exemption into ours.
On Tennessee's $242 billion wage base, that premium raises roughly $2 billion a year, which funds every benefit and the cost of running the program.
The state's part is a $57.5 million loan to extend the claims platform the Department of Labor already runs, repaid out of premiums once collections begin.
It's insurance: every working Tennessean pays in, and every working Tennessean is covered when they need it most.

We'll fund Tennessee's new wellness standards into every classroom and close the loophole that lets students skip the personal finance course the state already requires.
Tennessee rewrote its K-12 health and lifetime wellness standards in November 2024, and they reach classrooms for the first time in the 2026-27 school year with nothing behind them.
We'll pay for that rollout: instructional materials matched to the new standards, training for the teachers delivering them, and the content the revision left thin.
That means current nutrition science, mental health literacy, and digital wellness lessons that did not exist when the 2016 standards were written.
On money, Tennessee got there first.
The state has required a half-credit personal finance course to graduate since the class of 2013.
The same rule lets a single Junior Reserve Officers' Training Corps course substitute for it, so a student can finish high school without ever taking it.
We'll close that substitution and modernize what the course teaches, from buy-now-pay-later apps to crypto scams and student loans, so students learn what a lease commits them to before they sign one.

Cover childcare costs for every working Tennessee family earning up to $99,000, with parents free to choose center-based, home-based, or faith-based providers.
On August 26, 2025, Tennessee opened a childcare waitlist.
A family of four earning up to $87,324 still qualifies on income, and qualifying stopped being enough.
On October 1 the state began charging every approved family 5 percent of gross income, about $134 a month for a family of four at the poverty line.
It also dropped the higher rates it paid providers in rural counties and outside business hours.
We'll start clearing that waitlist in year one out of the Future Families Fund's annual draw, and finish it by year four, because these are families the state told they qualified and then told to wait.
In year four we'll waive the copay again below 150 percent of poverty and restore the rural and off-hours rates, because a subsidy is worth nothing where no provider will take it.
Then we'll raise the income line to $99,000 for a family of four.
Parents choose the provider: center-based, home-based, faith-based, whatever works.
Childcare & Families Future Families Collapse ↑ End the Benefits Cliff Working Tennesseans keep more of every raise, with childcare and health coverage that phase out gradually instead of vanishing overnight.
Explore → Copied!
Working Tennesseans keep more of every raise, with childcare and health coverage that phase out gradually instead of vanishing overnight.
In a survey of more than 200 lower-income Tennessee families, 85 percent had hit a benefits cliff, half said they had resisted better-paying work, and one in four had turned down a raise.
A raise that costs a family its childcare subsidy leaves them worse off than before.
We'll take the cliffs out one rule at a time.
On health coverage, we'll hold eligibility steady for twelve months at a time so a raise in March doesn't end coverage in April, and use sliding-scale premiums where the rule today is a hard cutoff.
On cash assistance, we'll replace the flat $250 earnings deduction, the sharpest cliff Tennessee controls outright, with a graduated disregard that lets a parent work more hours and come out ahead.
Tennessee is re-procuring the system that decides who qualifies for TennCare, the software where every one of these rules actually takes effect.
We'll write configurable benefit calculation into that solicitation before it goes out, rather than buying that capability later at a change order's price.

Protect Tennesseans' right to make their own medical decisions, with expanded access to contraception, comprehensive sex education, and real support for pregnant women and families.
Most Tennesseans share the same goal regardless of politics or faith: fewer abortions, healthier families.
The question is which policies actually get us there.
Making abortions illegal doesn't stop them.
It makes them costlier, riskier, and later, the opposite of what the law claimed to protect.
What reduces abortion is preventing unintended pregnancy.
When Colorado expanded free contraception access, teen abortions fell by more than 40 percent.
We'll fund affordable contraception access and rewrite the family life education statutes that today center on abstinence, so Tennessee teaches comprehensive, age-appropriate sex education.
This is the pro-life case for prevention.
Tennessee protected access to in vitro fertilization and birth control in 2025, and legislators have already moved to weaken that law.
We'll enforce and expand it.
Every Tennessean has the right to make their own medical decisions without the government standing in the exam room.
