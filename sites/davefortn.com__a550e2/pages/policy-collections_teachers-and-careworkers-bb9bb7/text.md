A sovereign wealth fund for Tennessee's kids, paid for by the wealthiest Tennesseans, that never spends its principal and buys our schools what the state never has.
Tennessee ranks dead last in the country on school spending, about $12,100 a student against a national average near $19,400.
Our kids didn't create that, and they can't fix it.
We'll create the Future Families Fund, a state-owned sovereign wealth fund that never spends its principal, built the way Alaska built its Permanent Fund.
Five sources pay in: a 15 percent cannabis tax, a higher cigarette tax, a contribution on estates above $5 million, a 7 percent contribution on capital gains above $1 million, and voluntary gifts.
We'll seed it with $1.25 billion of last year's surplus.
What the state spends each year is a draw set by formula against the Fund's trailing market value, and everything earned above it is reinvested.
Statute will bar that draw from vouchers, from sectarian schools, and from any line the school funding formula already pays.
The first term's draw goes to child care and finishes it.
We'll put the Fund in the constitution so no future legislature can reach the principal.

Repeal Tennessee's private school voucher and spend every dollar of it on the working conditions that decide whether good teachers stay in the schools that lose them fastest.
Tennessee has been filling its classrooms by lowering the bar to enter them.
Unfilled positions fell from 1,434 in fall 2023 to 817 in fall 2024 while the state issued 6,579 emergency credentials in a single year.
About 13 percent of Tennessee teachers leave public school classrooms annually, against 2,966 who finish a state preparation program.
We'll repeal the Education Freedom Scholarship program and spend all $303.9 million a year of it on what decides whether a qualified teacher is in front of a Tennessee classroom next fall.
A hundred and eleven of the state's 140 districts have no school social worker at all, so we'll hire counselors and social workers toward the 250-to-1 standard, starting where vacancies and emergency credentials run highest.
Every teacher in their first two years gets two full years of trained coaching, because one year of it produces nothing.
Teachers who stay tell the state they stay for staff culture and school leadership.
We'll pay for both.

Tennessee seniors age in their own homes, with better-paid home health aides, senior nutrition, senior transportation, and elder abuse prevention.
Tennessee still sends about 48 percent of its Medicaid long-term care dollars to institutions, against about 36 percent nationally, and ranks 47th in the country on long-term care.
Nearly 14,000 TennCare members live in nursing facilities, and by 2040 Tennesseans 85 and over will grow 72 percent to 205,000.
The state cannot build nursing homes fast enough to meet that, and it cannot afford to.
Tennessee pays about $93,000 a year for a nursing facility resident and about $40,000 for someone served at home.
We'll fund 400 transitions out of facilities every year on top of what the state already does, publish an annual target, and report the count quarterly.
The moving allowance has sat at a $2,000 lifetime cap, and we'll raise it.
Home health aides here earn about $16 an hour, below the national average, while turnover runs near 80 percent.
We'll raise their pay, build pathways from aide to licensed nurse, and fund the meals, transportation, and elder abuse prevention that make home viable.

We'll fund Tennessee's new wellness standards into every classroom and close the loophole that lets students skip the personal finance course the state already requires.
Tennessee rewrote its K-12 health and lifetime wellness standards in November 2024, and they reach classrooms for the first time in the 2026-27 school year with nothing behind them.
We'll pay for that rollout: instructional materials matched to the new standards, training for the teachers delivering them, and the content the revision left thin.
That means current nutrition science, mental health literacy, and digital wellness lessons that did not exist when the 2016 standards were written.
On money, Tennessee got there first.
The state has required a half-credit personal finance course to graduate since the class of 2013.
The same rule lets a single Junior Reserve Officers' Training Corps course substitute for it, so a student can finish high school without ever taking it.
We'll close that substitution and modernize what the course teaches, from buy-now-pay-later apps to crypto scams and student loans, so students learn what a lease commits them to before they sign one.
