Forty thousand more Tennessee veterans claim the federal benefits they've earned, through better claim filing in every county, veteran-to-veteran peer support, rural digital screening, and on-site employment help at Tennessee's VA medical centers.
Tennessee has about 400,000 veterans, and roughly 163,800 of them receive VA compensation or pension benefits.
The state does not publish whether its county veteran service officers are filing claims, whether those claims get approved, or how much federal money they secure.
Tennessee's veteran suicide rate is 48.2 per 100,000, about four veterans a week, well above the national rate of 35.2.
We'll fund a full-time service officer in all 95 counties, publish claims filed, claims approved, and benefits secured by county, and we'll deploy digital screening tools that work offline so rural veterans don't drive hours for a form.
A veteran-to-veteran peer support network on New York's Joseph P.
Dwyer model starts in 30 high-need rural counties and reaches all 95.
Employment specialists at Tennessee's VA medical centers will run Individual Placement and Support, which put 68.6 percent of veterans with post-traumatic stress disorder into competitive jobs against 57.0 percent in traditional programs.
The state pays only on verified placements.

When disaster strikes every Tennessean will get a warning in time, through a 95-county emergency management assessment, last-mile alert pilots in high-risk areas, and a statewide shelter inventory with live status reporting.
The 2020 Cookeville tornado killed 19 people at 1:50 in the morning and the 2021 Waverly flood killed 20 people because warnings couldn't reach them.
Nighttime tornadoes kill at 2.5 times the rate of daytime events, a disparity directly tied to warning reach.
Tennessee has invested in disaster infrastructure: a $100 million reserve fund, a statewide weather mesonet now under construction, and FirstNet integration for all 118 public safety answering points.
What hasn't been built is the local capacity to use it.
The hard part is the last mile, getting a warning to a house at 2 a.m.
Tennessee also has no published shelter list with accessibility information, despite over 1.7 million adults with disabilities.
We'll fix all three with a 95-county emergency management assessment, last-mile warning pilots in the highest-risk areas, and a statewide shelter inventory with accessibility details and live status reporting so families can find a confirmed-open shelter when a storm hits.

Send a behavioral health professional to every mental health crisis call, by funding city co-responder and alternative-response teams and stationing clinicians in 911 dispatch.
Tennessee cities already built the working models.
Memphis invented Crisis Intervention Team training in 1988, the year after police shot a man in crisis.
Nashville runs a police co-responder program and, separately, a fire-based alternative-response program.
Knoxville's co-responder teams resolved 97 percent of interactions without an arrest in 2024, with zero uses of force, and the city voted to expand from four teams to six.
The job is to scale what Tennessee cities have proven into more cities and into 911 itself.
We'll offer competitive matching grants to cities expanding crisis response, each city free to choose its model.
The grants fund the expansion margin rather than existing operations, and each requires outcome reporting and response-time standards.
We'll station behavioral health clinicians inside metro 911 dispatch so a crisis gets routed to a crisis team at the point of the call.
Far from defunding the police, this frees officers to focus on real crime.
Let cops be cops.

Put trauma-informed training, peer support, and wellness services within reach of every Tennessee police department, so officers don't break under the weight of the worst calls.
We send police officers into the worst moments of people's lives, murders, fatal accidents, suicides, active threats, and then act surprised when the job breaks them.
Officers experience post-traumatic stress and depression at up to five times the rate of the general population, and in most years more officers die by suicide than are killed in the line of duty.
Nearly a quarter of officers report suicidal ideation over a career.
Only about one in six sought mental health care in the past year, though one in four screened positive for symptoms.
Burned-out officers make worse decisions under pressure, and communities pay for that.
We'll set a statewide trauma-informed training standard and fund competitive wellness grants, so peer support and confidential counseling are within reach of every department at every rank.
Nashville's police already run free counseling, critical incident support, and a 24-hour crisis line for officers and their families.
Every department in Tennessee should have that.

Require background checks on every firearm sale, require safe storage when kids are in the home, and create a court-ordered process for temporarily removing firearms from someone in crisis.
Tennessee has a proud tradition of gun ownership, and nothing here changes that.
You can still buy a gun from a private seller in this state with no background check at all, so we'll require one on every firearm sale.
Firearms are the leading cause of death for Tennessee children, and the state's only storage policy today is a sales-tax break on gun safes.
We'll require gun owners to store firearms securely when children are in the home.
For someone in crisis, we'll create a court process to temporarily remove firearms, replacing the 2024 law that barred local governments from acting on their own.
Tennessee lost 878 people to gun suicide in 2023 and 667 to gun homicide.
Indiana's version of this law cut gun suicides by 7.5 percent, Connecticut's by 13.7 percent once it was actively enforced.
The key word is court.
A judge reviews sworn evidence before any order issues, and the owner gets a prompt hearing to contest it.

Repeal the laws that force every Tennessee sheriff to act as a federal immigration agent, so officers can focus on the cases their communities actually need them on.
Local law enforcement answers to Tennessee voters, and the legislature has spent two years taking that choice away.
In 2025 it paid agencies to sign federal immigration agreements and made it a felony for a local official to vote for a sanctuary policy.
In 2026 it required every county sheriff to sign one by January 1, 2027, or lose state funding.
Two Tennessee agencies took part in January 2025; by April 2026 it was forty-nine sheriffs.
We'll champion repeal of both laws and restore each county's right to decide how its own deputies spend their shifts.
Nothing here asks a sheriff to release someone wanted for a violent crime.
Repeal asks the state to stop spending Tennessee deputies on a federal mission.
Most of these agreements run through the jail, and word of what happens there travels a neighborhood faster than any outreach program.
A county where people are afraid to call 911 is not a safer county.
