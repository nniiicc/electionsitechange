Affordable homes that stay affordable, for you and for the family that buys them next.
Tennessee keeps paying to create affordable housing that stops being affordable.
Tax credit restrictions run out, subsidized owners sell at market rate, and the public money walks out the door with them.
A community land trust closes that door.
A nonprofit owns the land, sells the house to an income-qualified family below market, and a ground lease caps what the next sale can bring.
The Lincoln Institute followed 4,108 homes in shared-equity programs over three decades and found 95 percent were still affordable when they resold, through boom, crash, and recovery.
Nashville and Memphis already have trusts on the ground, with a third organizing in Chattanooga.
We'll capitalize them with $11.5 million of federal housing money Tennessee already administers (enough for roughly 115 to 460 permanently affordable homes) and rewrite the state's housing tax credit scoring to favor permanent affordability.
We'll also make it legal to build a duplex.
Small apartment buildings are banned on about 94 percent of residential land in Middle Tennessee, and allowing them by right costs the state nothing.

We'll file a Section 1115 waiver, the federal permission that lets a state run Medicaid its own way, and cover every adult below the poverty line at Tennessee's regular match.
Wisconsin has run that design for a decade under governors of both parties.
Full expansion covers more people for less apiece, and for states that expand, the 2025 federal budget law phases down the hospital assessment that finances TennCare.
With 61 percent of Tennessee's rural hospitals already vulnerable to closure, we won't trade the financing that keeps them open for a better match on fewer people.

We'll add the adult vision benefit TennCare still won't pay for either way.

Give builders ready-made home designs and quick permits, so new homes get built faster, near the jobs, schools, and bus routes Tennesseans already use.
The median home in Nashville now costs more than five times what the median household there earns.
Tennessee cannot set interest rates or lumber prices, and it can decide how long a permit takes and where the state's own housing dollars go.
Most small projects start with months of architectural review and thousands of dollars in design fees before a permit is even filed.
That prices out the builders most likely to put up a duplex or a garage apartment.
We'll publish a library of pre-engineered, code-compliant plans through the state housing agency, free to any builder, and cities can add their own designs to it.
Build from a library plan and the state review fee is waived and the local decision comes back in five business days.
Drawing the whole library costs about $345,000.
Tennessee also awards roughly $25 million a year in federal housing tax credits.
The scoring sheet gives location four points out of a hundred, while a gazebo and a pergola together score the same.
We'll fix the scoring.
Same money, better places.

Every Tennessee kid gets full medical, dental, vision, and mental health coverage, no matter what their family earns.
CoverKids already insures Tennessee children up to 250 percent of the federal poverty level.
We'll raise that to 300 percent, the highest income at which federal law still pays Tennessee's enhanced match under the Children's Health Insurance Program.
At that match Washington pays about three of every four dollars, more than double the state's share under regular Medicaid.
Kids are the cheapest people in the healthcare system to cover, about $3,600 a year for a Tennessee child, a third less than a working-age adult.
Children in working families who earn just above today's cutoff get full medical, dental, vision, and mental health coverage, and businesses now covering dependents take a real expense off their books.
Beginning in October 2026, federal rules narrow which lawfully present immigrant children draw that match.
We'll cover those children with state dollars rather than let Washington's rules leave them behind.

Lower healthcare premiums for Tennessee families by taking over Tennessee's marketplace from the federal government and keeping the fees Tennessee already pays.
About 570,000 Tennesseans buy health insurance on healthcare.gov, and the fees they pay on their premiums, roughly $120 million a year, go straight to Washington.
Tennessee has no say over the platform, the enrollment help, or the fees.
We'll create a Tennessee Marketplace Authority with a citizen board to run the marketplace here as early as the 2029 plan year.
Insurers keep competing exactly as they do now; what changes is who runs the platform and who keeps the money.
Running the marketplace costs about $95 million a year, leaving roughly $25 million.
The surplus funds a reinsurance pool that pays the most expensive claims so insurers can price everyone else lower, targeting a 10 to 15 percent premium cut.
When the enhanced federal credits expired, more than 70,000 Tennesseans dropped coverage in a single year.
We'll cap premiums at 5 percent of household income for families up to 300 percent of the poverty line, and step that subsidy back down if Washington restores its own.

Tennessee's hospitals finance TennCare at the maximum federal law allows, on a revenue base frozen in 2021 and a statute that expires every year.
We'll authorize it for multiple years, push Washington to unfreeze the base, and get the federal answer Tennessee needs in writing.
Tennessee's hospitals tax themselves to pay for TennCare.
The assessment collects about $1.4 billion a year and draws billions more in federal match that flows back out as payment for care.
The tax expires every July, so hospitals budget around financing guaranteed twelve months at a time.
We'll authorize it for multiple years, keeping the protections already written into it: the assessment voids if TennCare funding falls below the recommended budget level, and not a dollar of the trust fund may be moved into the General Fund.
The 2025 federal budget law froze the revenue base at what hospitals earned in 2021, so the financing covers less of its job every year.
Only Washington can unfreeze it, so we'll press Tennessee's delegation for a fix that lets states refresh a frozen base.
That same law cuts the assessment ceiling for states that expand Medicaid, and nobody has said whether it reaches a state expanding now.
We'll demand that answer in writing, because 27 rural hospitals are vulnerable enough that this money is the difference between open and closed.
Healthcare Economic Wellbeing Collapse ↑ Make Childcare Affordable Cover childcare costs for every working Tennessee family earning up to $99,000, with parents free to choose center-based, home-based, or faith-based providers.

Cover childcare costs for every working Tennessee family earning up to $99,000, with parents free to choose center-based, home-based, or faith-based providers.
On August 26, 2025, Tennessee opened a childcare waitlist.
A family of four earning up to $87,324 still qualifies on income, and qualifying stopped being enough.
On October 1 the state began charging every approved family 5 percent of gross income, about $134 a month for a family of four at the poverty line.
It also dropped the higher rates it paid providers in rural counties and outside business hours.
We'll start clearing that waitlist in year one out of the Future Families Fund's annual draw, and finish it by year four, because these are families the state told they qualified and then told to wait.
In year four we'll waive the copay again below 150 percent of poverty and restore the rural and off-hours rates, because a subsidy is worth nothing where no provider will take it.
Then we'll raise the income line to $99,000 for a family of four.
Parents choose the provider: center-based, home-based, faith-based, whatever works.

Open new hospitals and clinics in the 93 of 95 counties without enough care, by finishing and speeding up the repeal of the rule that requires state permission to build them.
Tennessee has lost 16 rural hospitals since 2010, more per capita than any other state.
Three-quarters of the 25 essential-access rural hospitals still open are at high risk of closing.
Ninety-three of 95 counties are designated, in whole or in part, as shortage areas for primary care, dental, or mental health care.
Anyone who wants to open a hospital or many kinds of clinics here still needs a state ruling that the service is "needed." Certificate-of-need review was supposed to hold costs down.
Federal antitrust staff told Tennessee's legislature in April 2026 what they have argued for decades: the review protects the hospitals already in a market and keeps new providers out of the counties shortest of care.
Lawmakers have started unwinding it and exempted new hospitals effective July 2028.
We'll move that date up so providers can break ground now, and repeal what is left for freestanding emergency departments, diagnostic centers, rehabilitation, home care, hospice, and nursing homes.
None of it costs the state a dollar.

State-owned solar and battery storage at Tennessee's own facilities cuts the state's power bills, and gives Tennessee generation assets no federal policy shift can take away.
Federal law requires the Tennessee Valley Authority to buy power from qualifying state-owned generators under 80 megawatts, but only at its own calculated avoided cost.
Lately that is 3 to 4.5 cents a kilowatt-hour, roughly a quarter of what Tennesseans pay at retail.
We won't build a state generation program on selling cheap power back to TVA.
We'll create the Tennessee Renewable Power Authority and put generation where it earns full value: solar behind the meter at prisons, campuses, and office complexes, so every kilowatt-hour avoids 12 to 15 cents of retail cost.
A ten-site battery pilot will test whether the state can cut peak-demand charges at its costliest facilities.
The bonds that build at state facilities are repaid out of the electricity savings there, not the General Fund.
Every megawatt-hour from an Authority-financed project writes a check to the community that hosts it, a flat $2.50 per megawatt-hour split between host county and municipality, owed whether the project is publicly or privately owned.

Drop Tennessee's 4% state grocery tax to zero over three years, saving a family of four up to $400 a year, paid for by closing corporate tax loopholes.
Tennessee is one of eight states that still taxes groceries.
The 4 percent you pay at the register is part of what makes Tennessee's the third most regressive tax system in the country.
Eighty-nine percent of Tennessee voters want the grocery tax cut.
The legislature passed a $58 billion budget in April 2026 without a dollar of relief in it.
We'll drop the state rate to 2 percent in the first year and to zero by the third.
Counties and cities keep their local grocery tax, and the state replaces the local share of what it collects, so no county loses a dollar.
The state gives up $856 million a year, and we'll pay for it by closing the corporate tax loopholes that let profitable companies book their Tennessee earnings somewhere else.
School funding is set by formula and paid from the General Fund, which the corporate reforms keep whole.
A family of four saves up to $400 a year.
For a household making $30,000, that is about two weeks of groceries.
Childcare & Families Economic Wellbeing Collapse ↑ Unlock Renewable Energy in Tennessee Cut rooftop solar payback roughly in half for Tennessee homeowners and create competitive Renewable Energy Zones open to every Tennessee community.

Cut rooftop solar payback roughly in half for Tennessee homeowners and create competitive Renewable Energy Zones open to every Tennessee community.
Right now a Tennessee homeowner who installs solar would wait roughly 20 years to break even because the Tennessee Valley Authority's buyback pays only 3 to 4.5 cents a kilowatt-hour for exported power, and some local utilities pay nothing at all.
We'll fix the economics directly to make lending viable.
Every solar household that enrolls in a buyback program will receive a state-funded credit bringing the total to a fair 11 cents, paired with an upfront rebate of up to 30 percent, a sales-tax exemption on home solar equipment, and instant online permitting.
Together these measures bring payback to roughly 10 to 12 years.
If the Authority adopts a fair buyback rate of its own, then the state's provisions will sunset automatically.
We'll also expand community solar to support families who rent or can't put panels on their own roof.
Lastly we'll designate three to five Renewable Energy Zones through a competitive program open to every local power company in Tennessee, and award zones with matching grants to build solar and storage.
Energy & Infrastructure Climate Action Collapse ↑ Finance Clean Energy Help Tennessee households and small businesses finance heat pumps, weatherization, and targeted solar upgrades, with repayment built into their utility bills and monthly payments capped below their energy savings.

Help Tennessee households and small businesses finance heat pumps, weatherization, and targeted solar upgrades, with repayment built into their utility bills and monthly payments capped below their energy savings.
A $20,000 rooftop solar system or a $3,000 to $5,000 whole-home weatherization is out of reach for most Tennessee families without accessible capital.
Heat pump water heaters alone save up to $550 per year for a family of four.
The savings are real, but the upfront cost is the barrier.
We'll create the Tennessee Green Bank, a state-chartered financing institution capitalized with $100 million in state funds.
Modeled on the Connecticut Green Bank, which has drawn nearly seven dollars of private investment for every public dollar, the Tennessee version will finance residential solar, batteries, heat pumps, and weatherization.
On-bill or income-based repayment ensures monthly loan payments stay below the energy savings each project delivers so households save money from day one.
Manufactured housing will be prioritized since Tennessee has one of the highest manufactured housing shares in the country, the population with the highest energy burden and the highest return on weatherization investment.
Energy & Infrastructure Climate Action Collapse ↑ Bring Down Drug Prices Lower prescription prices for every Tennessean by buying in bulk for state programs and giving everyone a free discount card, starting with insulin and naloxone.

Lower prescription prices for every Tennessean by buying in bulk for state programs and giving everyone a free discount card, starting with insulin and naloxone.
The pharmacy benefit manager system takes a cut at every step between the manufacturer and the counter, and hides the real price from the people paying it.
Tennesseans spend more per person on prescriptions than residents of most states.
Oregon and Washington built the alternative.
Their public purchasing cooperative, now called ArrayRx, buys for programs covering more than half a million people and passes 100 percent of manufacturer rebates back to those programs instead of keeping them.
It has delivered more than $155 million in savings since 2018.
Nevada joined it outright and had discount cards in residents' hands within a year.
Tennessee's cooperative purchasing law already lets state agencies buy together, and we'll pass a short bill extending that power to every resident.
Joining costs the state nothing.
No state-run drug factories, no new agency, just seven million people at the table instead of one patient at a time.

Spend $10 million to forgive up to $1 billion in medical debt and lift that weight off the families carrying it.
Tennesseans hold more past-due debt than the average American, and medical bills contribute to a majority of personal bankruptcies nationally.
Most of that medical debt already sits on a collector's ledger, written off by the hospital and sold for a fraction of its face value.
Undue Medical Debt buys those portfolios on the same market and forgives them outright, with no tax consequence to the family.
It already operates in Tennessee, where it has erased roughly $142 million for about 73,000 people.
We'll scale that statewide with $10 million in the first year, enough to retire roughly $665 million in medical debt, more or less depending on what portfolios come up for sale.
After that, up to $5 million a year keeps us buying.
Ten million dollars is a rounding error in a state budget, and it clears the bills that push Tennessee families into bankruptcy.
