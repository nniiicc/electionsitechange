Match city transit investments with state dollars, set statewide bus rapid transit design standards, and fund corridor feasibility studies for connecting Tennessee cities by rail.
Nashville, Memphis, Knoxville, and Chattanooga are each building transit on their own.
Nashville voters approved a $3.1 billion plan and Memphis won federal money for bus rapid transit, and no dedicated state operating funding or state-led rail program ties any of it together.
We'll create a Tennessee Transit Investment Program that pays cities 10 to 20 cents on every dollar they raise through the local transit surcharge, capped at $25 million a year statewide with no jurisdiction taking more than 40 percent of the pool.
Statewide design standards will require dedicated lanes, buses every 10 minutes at peak, and committed institutional anchors like hospitals and universities before construction, which can raise ridership as much as 60 percent.
Memphis needs this most: its transit authority has lost roughly three-quarters of its ridership since 2013 and remains one of the few major American metros with no dedicated transit funding.
Rail starts with $7.5 million for corridor feasibility studies, so when a city proves demand and wins federal dollars, Tennessee shows up as a partner.

Fund crisis services, rural mental health telehealth, and peer support networks for Tennessee farmers, who, according to the National Rural Health Association, die by suicide at up to 3.5 times the rate of the general population.
Tennessee farms are dwindling, down to just over 60,000 farms, and we're projected to lose over a million acres of farmland by 2040.
If we want to save these farms, we need to save the farmers first.
According to the National Rural Health Association, farmers and ranchers die by suicide at up to 3.5 times the rate of the general population.
With farm debt approaching record levels, crop prices down roughly a third since 2022, and 70% of rural counties without a single psychiatrist, stress can turn into despair.
Farmers shouldn't have to face that alone.
The Tennessee Suicide Prevention Network established a Farmers Task Force in 2018, but we still need real infrastructure: agriculture-specific crisis services, expanded rural mental health through telehealth, and peer support networks modeled on programs working in Wisconsin and North Carolina.
Reaching out for help isn't weakness, it's a strength.
Tennessee needs to make sure that help is actually there when farmers need it.

Run scheduled bus service between the roughly three-quarters of Tennessee counties without fixed-route transit and their regional hubs, with at least two round-trips per weekday, $5 round-trip fares, and free service for seniors and veterans.
Roughly three-quarters of Tennessee's 95 counties have no fixed-route bus at all.
What they have is demand-response service you book a week ahead.
That works for a one-off errand and not for a shift that starts at six every Tuesday morning, an evening class, or a standing appointment at the regional hospital.
We'll set up opt-in Regional Transit Authorities through Tennessee's nine Development Districts, using the Human Resource Agency networks driving those routes as the operators, with no unfunded mandate on any county.
Each participating county seat gets at least two fixed-route round-trips per weekday to its nearest regional hub.
Fares are capped at $5 round trip, free for seniors and veterans, and schedules published in the standard format so the routes show up in map apps.
New state operating dollars will sit on top of the roughly $21 million a year in existing transit investment grants and the federal rural transit money Tennessee has been leaving on the table.

The streets that need sidewalks are local.
We'll expand Green Corridors into opt-in partnerships with any community that wants one, offering matching grants, technical assistance, and transportation department pilots for speed tables, wider crosswalks, protected bike lanes, and pedestrian-priority zones.
Each community decides what fits, because a town square in Pikeville needs something different from a corridor in Murfreesboro.

Washington awards more than $4 billion a year in small business research grants.

We'll cover that cost with a $3,000 reimbursable stipend for preparing a competitive federal submission.

Eleven Tennessee counties are classified as distressed on the state's own index.

We'll keep it that way, get the federal bridge money already reserved for rural counties onto their bridges, and fix roads before they fail.
Tennessee is one of only six states that pays for its roads without debt, on a 26 cents a gallon gasoline tax set in 2017.
That discipline is worth protecting.
The repair backlog is $38 billion, and it falls hardest on rural counties, where locally owned bridges are rated poor more often than state-owned ones, 4.7 percent against 3.8 percent.
We'll draw down Tennessee's $226 million federal bridge allocation before the window closes.
At least 15 percent of it must go to locally owned bridges at up to 100 percent federal share with no county match.
Tennessee had put only 44 percent of it under contract by March 2026.
Small rural counties can't navigate that paperwork alone, so we'll bundle their projects and provide the engineering help that puts the money within reach of every eligible county.
We'll also shift maintenance toward sealing and treating roads while they're still in fair condition, which cuts lifecycle costs by roughly 25 percent against waiting for reconstruction.

Faster, more reliable, more affordable internet in every Tennessee county, by expanding the electric co-op and municipal broadband model that already works in Chattanooga.
Chattanooga's city-owned electric utility built the first community-wide gigabit network in the country, financed with revenue bonds and a federal grant, not a dollar of local tax money.
The $220 million network returned $2.69 billion in community benefits in its first decade, and its fiber division now sends $48 million a year back to the electric side, holding power rates down.
Electric cooperatives have been free to sell retail internet here since 2017, and we'll back their buildouts across rural Tennessee first.
City utilities are still fenced in: state law forbids a municipal utility from selling broadband outside its electric territory, and a federal court held that only the legislature can lift that.
We'll lift it, keeping the protections already in state law, including a comptroller-reviewed business plan and a ban on subsidizing broadband out of electric rates.
Federal money is putting wire in the ground for the addresses that have none.
The co-op and municipal lanes add competition, lower prices, and local accountability once that wire is laid.
Every county, within five years.

Open new hospitals and clinics in the 93 of 95 counties without enough care, by finishing and speeding up the repeal of the rule that requires state permission to build them.
Tennessee has lost 16 rural hospitals since 2010, more per capita than any other state.
Three-quarters of the 25 essential-access rural hospitals still open are at high risk of closing.
Ninety-three of 95 counties are designated, in whole or in part, as shortage areas for primary care, dental, or mental health care.
Anyone who wants to open a hospital or many kinds of clinics here still needs a state ruling that the service is "needed." Certificate-of-need review was supposed to hold costs down.
Federal antitrust staff told Tennessee's legislature in April 2026 what they have argued for decades: the review protects the hospitals already in a market and keeps new providers out of the counties shortest of care.
Lawmakers have started unwinding it and exempted new hospitals effective July 2028.
We'll move that date up so providers can break ground now, and repeal what is left for freestanding emergency departments, diagnostic centers, rehabilitation, home care, hospice, and nursing homes.
None of it costs the state a dollar.

Healthcare you can afford regardless of insurance, brought to every Tennessee county through new satellite clinics, mobile health units, and trained community health workers.
At a community health center, the bill is set by what you earn, not by whether you have insurance.
Tennessee has 30 such organizations running about 220 sites, and whole counties have no site at all.
We'll put state matching money behind every new site that opens in an underserved county, $23 million to build satellite campuses and mobile units, and $8 million a year to keep them staffed.
Tennessee's medical schools are already sending dental care units into rural counties; that should be the norm rather than the exception.
We'll also get community health workers paid through TennCare.
They are trusted people from their own neighborhoods, trained to connect clinical care to daily life, and they reduce emergency room visits.
Tennessee funds their training and a state association accredits the employers who hire them, and TennCare still won't cover the work as a benefit.
More than half the states already do, and we'll file the amendment that makes Tennessee the next one.

Tennessee buries about a million tons of recyclable material every year, worth roughly $150 million, well short of the state's own 25 percent waste-reduction goal.
Middle Tennessee's largest landfill for household garbage is projected to reach capacity around 2029, and the industry's answer is to site new mega-landfills on rural counties.
We'll do the opposite and spend the recycling money Tennessee already collects.
The Solid Waste Management Fund took in $11.6 million in 2024 and disbursed only $8.8 million, closing the year on a $24.2 million balance.
We'll accelerate grants from the fund's uncommitted portion into drop-off convenience centers, recycling equipment, and composting grants in the rural counties with the worst access.
At least 55 counties and 18 cities have adopted the law letting a community reject a new landfill, and a 2026 state task force is trying to weaken the law's siting criteria at the waste industry's urging.
We'll protect that authority and publish model host-community-agreement standards so counties consenting to host landfills stop getting shortchanged.

Get more doctors into rural Tennessee by paying for medical school for kids who promise to come back, and by claiming every federal physician slot and dollar Tennessee is eligible for.
Tennessee spends about $48 million a year training doctors through TennCare, and none of it depends on where those doctors end up practicing.
We train them and watch them leave, while rural emergency departments cut hours because they cannot staff a shift.
In 2007 Mississippi started identifying rural kids who wanted to be doctors, paid for medical school in exchange for a commitment to come home, and tracked what happened.
Fifteen years on, about 85 percent of those who completed the commitment were still practicing in rural Mississippi.
The program works because it doesn't fight human nature; you pay for the training and you enforce the deal.
A Tennessee version will start with one anchor training institution and an opening cohort of 25 to 50 rural-origin students, with contract enforcement and retention tracking from day one.
We'll fill all thirty physician visa waiver slots the state holds each year and fund the match to the federal maximum on loan repayment.
About $6 million a year, planted now because a pipeline takes eight years to bear.
