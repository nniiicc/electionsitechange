House district 105
Same ballot.
Two very different records.
Here's where Ken Borton and Rachel Teeter stand — side by side.
Why they're running
Ken Borton — in his own words
Borton traces his start in politics to a 2010 run-in with the DNR.
Conservation officers ticketed him over his "Snowman Cam" — a birdfeeder livestream he ran with his wife — which the DNR alleged was an illegal deer-baiting pile.
He fought it in court and the charges were dismissed.
He says the experience convinced him the law itself should change.
He served as an Otsego County commissioner, then won the open 105th seat in 2020.
He casts himself as a check on government overreach, especially the DNR.
"I had never, not ever, considered running for a political office."
Sources: Michigan Country Lines · Bridge Michigan
Rachel L.
Teeter — in her own words
I'm a single mom and a small business owner, and I've been the mom who couldn't pay the daycare bill at times — and I always believed it would get easier for the next generation, but it hasn't.
I've watched more and more neighbors slip into survival mode, working two and three jobs and still falling behind.
I'm a project manager by nature, so I asked myself, what do we all need to survive, and let's start there: food, housing, healthcare, and jobs — the four things that hit every family, no matter how they vote.
This isn't Republican versus Democrat anymore; it's working people versus the billionaires pulling further ahead while the rest of us do more with less.
I'm running to bring real help to everyone in the 105 — not someday after an election, but now.

Where Rachel stands
–
Borton — his recordBorton's campaign lists no plan to expand healthcare access or lower costs — his stated goals are taxes, regulation, and natural resources.
His health-related bills this term have been narrow and technical, like an ambulance-licensing update and a change to what practical nurses are allowed to do.
Most of his health-policy energy has gone to opposing expanded abortion access, including a bill to add new licensing rules for abortion clinics.
There is nothing on record aimed at lower premiums, broader coverage, or the rural provider shortages families here feel most.
Healthcare

Rachel — where we standI spent years in healthcare administration, much of it at Munson, and I saw firsthand how much of people's time, money, and health gets lost inside a broken system — patients skipping care they can't afford, and staff buried in paperwork instead of caring for people.
Moving to a single-payer system would make things simpler and fairer for patients and providers alike, and it would take the cost of coverage off the backs of small businesses and employers, who shouldn't have to be in the insurance business to hire good people.

Borton — his recordHousing affordability isn't part of Borton's platform.
His campaign goals center on taxes, regulation, and "encourage managed growth" — language about the pace of development, not the cost of a home.
The housing bills he has actually worked on deal with foreclosure and property-redemption paperwork, not supply or affordability, and he hasn't made the short-term-rental and seasonal-buyer pressure driving up local prices part of his agenda.
For a district where working families and young people are being priced out, there's no plan on record to help them stay.

Borton — his recordOn agriculture, Borton has a real record — this is home turf.
He chairs the Agriculture & Rural Development budget subcommittee, sponsored a bipartisan farm-export law, and is backed by the Farm Bureau's AgriPAC.
But farming and food access aren't the same thing.
He has no plan on record to help families afford groceries, and a food-assistance bill he backed this term would add new documentation requirements — more paperwork standing between struggling families and the help they need.

Borton — his recordBacks the House GOP roads plan (HB 4180–4187), branded "fix roads without raising taxes." It's largely a swap — it removes the sales tax on fuel and replaces it with an equivalent fuel tax that goes entirely to roads, and it sends schools other money to make up what they'd otherwise lose.
The trade-off is a hole of hundreds of millions of dollars in the state's main budget that lawmakers still have to sort out, which could squeeze other services.
The plan passed with bipartisan support.
Roads & taxes
✓
Rachel — where we standEverybody in the 105 wants better roads — we're the ones driving them to work, to school, and to the doctor, and watching them crumble every spring.
I'll always be straight about the trade-offs, instead of selling a "no new taxes" slogan for a plan that quietly moves money around and leaves a hole somewhere else.
But fixing our roads should never come at the expense of the programs families count on — we shouldn't have to choose between good roads and taking care of people.
Roads that last through Northern Michigan winters, dollars that reach our rural counties, and a plan that's honest about who pays.
✕
Borton — his recordVoted yes on the 2024 tax break (SB 237) that exempts giant data centers from sales and use taxes through 2050 — a giveaway to some of the wealthiest tech companies in the world.
Lawmakers who opposed it warned it carried no real protections for our water or for everyday customers' utility bills.
These centers use enormous amounts of power and water, and utilities stand to profit from the new demand — the same utilities whose PACs fund his campaign.
A bipartisan group of legislators is now trying to repeal these tax breaks over exactly these concerns.
Data centers
✓
Rachel — where we standI'm all for good jobs and new investment — but not by handing billion-dollar tech companies a free pass while working families pick up the tab in higher electric bills.
Here's my rule: our communities decide.
If a town doesn't want a data center, it doesn't come — and if a community does want one, the developer should be legally on the hook, with the money set aside up front, to return the land and water to the condition they found it.
These centers guzzle power and water, and nobody knows if they'll even exist in five years.
I won't let the people of the 105 get stuck with the poisoned water, the ruined land, and the cleanup bill after the corporations move on.
