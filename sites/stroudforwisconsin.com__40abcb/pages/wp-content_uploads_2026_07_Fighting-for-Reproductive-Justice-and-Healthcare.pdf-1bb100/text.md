​Fighting for Reproductive Justice and Healthcare​ ​When I talk to people on the campaign trail, healthcare and abortion rights come up time and time​ ​again as priority issues.
Reproductive justice is a healthcare issue for a simple reason: abortion is a​ ​decision that one should make in consultation with a medical professional.
Our government should not​ ​be involved in the reproductive decisions that we make about our bodies.
However, our government​ ​does have a responsibility to ensure access to healthcare, a growing problem in our district.​ ​While there is a healthcare provider shortage throughout the country, rural and remote areas are​ ​particularly burdened.
I’ve heard of 6 to 8-month waiting lists for mental health appointments—even​ ​for the most serious cases—and dentists are now booking cleanings in 9-month intervals rather than 6.​ ​There is also an acute shortage of emergency room physicians; and emergency care in rural areas is​ ​already challenging since providers can’t always rely on specialists for help.​ ​Most emergencies begin with a call to 911, and I’ve recently learned that our EMS infrastructure is​ ​under extraordinary strain.
The bottom line is that we need more people working in dispatch and EMT​ ​roles, as well as more resources to adequately compensate and equip our first-responders, whether​ ​they are volunteers, paid-on-call, or full-time.
Increased social isolation, the continuing scourge of the​ ​opioid epidemic, and a rapidly aging population all contribute to more calls than our first-responders​ ​can sometimes keep up with.​ ​So what should we do about these problems?
There are two clear legislative issues that we need to act​ ​on as soon as possible: accept Medicaid expansion through the Affordable Care Act so that more​ ​people are insured, and raise the Medicaid reimbursement rate in our state so that providers are​ ​compensated fairly for their services.
When Kentucky did these two things, they saw improved​ ​outcomes for patients, decreased costs, and an increase in providers.
But the other issues laid out​ ​above don’t have solutions that are obvious to people without relevant experience.
But here’s the good​ ​news: the people doing the work have ideas!
They know what it would take to make it easier for more​ ​people to enter into and remain in these critical professions without watering down standards.
But isn’t​ ​that always true?​​The people who do the work know​​what they need.​​The job of an elected official is​ ​to convene affected groups, ask questions, listen, and then work like hell to get results.
This is what I’ve​ ​spent my career doing, and this is what I intend to do as your state representative.​ ​Some of the ideas I’ve heard so far have to do with ensuring that required coursework and training fit​ ​the needs of the job; re-evaluating whether licensing exams are appropriately targeted; and developing​ ​initiatives to recruit and retain professionals, from physicians to paid-on-call EMTs to caregivers.
(We​ ​can add veterinarians to this list, as rural vet care is also an area of need.) My background makes me​ ​wonder whether community programs related to reducing social isolation and building community​ ​networks might also help so that an elder who falls and can’t get up knows that they can call a neighbor​ ​rather than a medic who might be needed to respond to a car accident or an overdose.​ ​Finally, we need to recognize that everything that shapes quality of life—from water to housing to our​ ​public schools—impacts our ability to recruit and retain healthcare professionals to our region.
The​ ​four core issues of this campaign thread together like a rope that binds our community.
Working to​ ​improve each of these areas helps us make progress with another.​ ​Given the evolving legal landscape, it’s helpful to clarify where things stand with abortion in Wisconsin.​ ​As most people know, the 2024​​Dobbs​​case that overturned​​Roe​​v.​​Wade​​didn’t end abortion care in the​ ​U.S., but it did send the issue to the states.
States either immediately set about banning or protecting​ ​abortion, or they reverted to laws on the books prior to 1973.
In Wisconsin, an 1849 law went into​ ​effect, a statute that was understood by many to mean that anyone who performed an abortion would​ ​be guilty of a felony.
This wasn’t just speculation; Sheboygan’s District Attorney said that​​he intended​​to​ ​prosecute abortion providers​.
In December 2023 Dane​​County Judge Diane Schlipper ruled that the law​ ​does not apply to elective abortions, and Planned Parenthood of Wisconsin resumed offering abortion​ ​services.
This case is​​likely to go​​before the Wisconsin​​Supreme Court.​ ​The strongest possible legal protection for women and providers would be for the legislature to pass a​ ​law that guarantees our rights to have autonomy over our own bodies.
As is true with many different​ ​issues, we need to get our house in order at the state level so that we’re buffered from whatever​ ​happens federally.
At this point, we would be foolish not to take seriously threats to ban contraception,​ ​IVF, and other medical procedures.​ ​Beyond whether abortion is legal, reproductive justice asks additional questions: who has access to​ ​reproductive healthcare?
How safe is it for people to give birth in this state?
Who has clean drinking​ ​water and healthy food options?
Who has access to high-quality preschools?
Reproductive justice helps​ ​us to see that an individual’s decision about whether to keep a pregnancy is just one part of the puzzle.​ ​We also have to consider the conditions that allow a person to bring a child into a safe and secure​ ​world.​ ​On Friday I was knocking doors in Ashland and met a man who was outside playing with two young​ ​kids.
He wasn’t on my list, but he came up to talk to me and asked what I was going to do to help​ ​address the opioid problem in our community.
As we talked, I learned that at the age of 70, he’s raising​ ​his 7 year-old grandson because his daughter died from an overdose.
He said his daughter tried​ ​treatment but it wasn’t enough because she also needed housing, someone who would hire her​ ​despite her record, and mental health counseling.
He said the idea that the state would tell any​ ​woman, but especially someone struggling with addiction, that they can’t make a decision about their​ ​own bodies is ludicrous.
As we talked it was clear that we didn’t agree on everything, but we agreed​ ​about this: we’ve got to get our priorities straight as a state and as a country and that starts with doing​ ​a much better job of taking care of each other.​ ​It’s been said that a society can be judged by how well it takes care of its kids and its elders, and on that​ ​measure we have a lot of work to do.
But I know that the people of this state want more from our​ ​elected officials in Madison than gerrymandered maps have allowed.
Now is the time to raise the bar​ ​and expect more from our leaders.
Only then can we start to make real progress on these critical issues.​
