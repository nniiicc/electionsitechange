My Approach
When I craft a bill, I’m looking for a common-sense solution to a real problem people in Colorado are facing.
For me, it starts with a simple question: What problem are we trying to solve?
Before I create a new law, I want to know that the problem is real, that people are actually being affected by it, and that the bill is going to make things better, not just create another layer of government, regulation, or more words in statute.
Sometimes we see bills that sound good on paper but are really trying to solve a problem that doesn’t exist, or a problem that is so rare that creating a statewide law doesn’t make sense.
I call those “fake problems.”
Colorado has plenty of real challenges that deserve our attention.
We should spend our time and energy solving those instead of looking for problems to legislate.
Common sense matters.
A bill shouldn’t pass simply because it has a good-sounding title or because it fits neatly into a political agenda.
We should look at how it will actually work, who it will affect, what it will cost, and whether there is a better way to accomplish the same goal.
I'm an engineer.
My approach follows from my training: identify a real problem, listen to the people affected by it, and find a practical solution that makes their lives better.
That’s the standard I use when deciding which bills are worth supporting.
Here are some of the bills I worked on in my first term and the real problems they were intended to solve.

HB26-1038
County Commissioner Redistricting
Requires County Commissions elected by their district electors (i.e., not elected at-large) to use an independent redistricting commission to draw their district lines.
This process would help ensure that district boundaries are determined through a fair and impartial process rather than by elected officials who may have a personal or political interest in the outcome.
The independent commission would be responsible for developing district maps that comply with applicable legal requirements and promote equitable representation

SB26-182
Updated Clean Energy Plan Municipally Owned Utility
Any municipal utility, including CSU, may apply for an extension on their 80% emissions reduction
target, currently due by 2030.
CSU believes they can’t currently hit that target without significantly raising electricity rates.
To get that extension, CSU must file a revised Clean Energy Plan by the end of 2026 with the
Colorado Air Pollution Control Division (APCD).
The new plan must detail energy sources and timelines needed to get to the 80% reduction and must be verified by the APCD.
The plan must also close Nixon, CSU’s remaining coal plan, by December 31, 2032 at the latest.
If no new plan is submitted or a submitted plan is not approved, Nixon must close in 2029 as planned.
Once a new Clean Energy Plan is approved, CSU must annually report their progress.
In exchange for this delay, CSU must further reduce their greenhouse gas emissions by 95%
between 2035 and 2040 as long as they can do so without impacting grid reliability.

SB26-051
Age Attestation on Computing Devices
Establishes a framework for commercial computing devices that have their own App Store and operating system linked by a user account (think Apple and Google devices) to send age bracket information to apps.
The Colorado Privacy Act, passed in 2023, requires companies to obtain parental consent before obtaining, using, sharing, or processing data from minors.
This bill provides a mechanism to ensure apps can’t claim ignorance of a minor’s age.
The mechanism allows parents to attest to the age of their child, meaning there is no need to upload documents such as a birth certificate or a photo of the child.

Interlock Device for Impaired Drivers

The goal is to reduce incidents of drunk driving, especially by repeat offenders, and thus reduce injury and death from impaired driving.

SB26-021
Clean Fleet Replacing Aging Diesel Trucks
This bill allows the Clean Fleet Enterprise to give grants to small businesses to replace old (pre-2009) heavy duty diesel trucks (26,000 lbs and greater) with new (post-2018) heavy duty diesel trucks to to help lower emissions.
Such grants are available for 5 years only with the hope that zero-emission heavy duty trucks will become commercially viable by the end of the period.
The Clean Fleet Enterprise may only use up to 20% of their funds for such grants.

HB26-1041
Electronic Vehicle Records
Allows the use of electronic certificates of title for vehicles at the purchasers’ request in all transactions, including cash purchases and out-of-state transfers.
This would streamline the vehicle title process by reducing paperwork, processing times, and the need for physical title documents.
The measure could also make vehicle transactions more convenient for purchasers, dealerships, and the state while providing a more efficient way to maintain and transfer vehicle ownership records.

SB25-068
Municipal Utility Unclaimed Utility Deposit Program
The Colorado Low-income Energy Assistance Program (LEAP) is a federally funded program to assist eligible Coloradans with their winter heating costs.
Colorado Springs Utilities has a supplementary assistance program called COPE which assists with electricity, gas and water.
This bipartisan bill allows CSU to help fund COPE with their unclaimed deposit monies that would otherwise go to the state LEAP program.

HB25-1165
Geologic Storage Enterprise Geothermal Resouces
Geothermal power and carbon capture and storage are cutting edge clean energy technologies.
However, carbon capture currently lacks dedicated funds for stewardship of completed or abandoned sites, and there are challenges for permitting new
geothermal powerplants depending on how deep they go.
This bipartisan bill requires carbon capture companies to pay into a site cleanup fund, and clarifies the legal environment for building geothermal energy, setting up Colorado to lead in new clean
energy tech.
