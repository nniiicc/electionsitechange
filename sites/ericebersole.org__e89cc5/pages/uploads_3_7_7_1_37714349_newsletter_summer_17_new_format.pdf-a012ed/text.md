In the 2017 session, Delegate Stein and I worked on a bill to limit a little known but significant practice by colleges – award displacement.
Universities send students an initial financial aid award letter with an offer of an institutional scholarship.
If a student works hard to find and win a private scholarship, some universities withdraw their initial institutional scholarship and redirect it to another student, negating the impact of the private scholarship and virtually taking it for their own.
This may be done to comply with federal and state laws.
More often, it is done to redistribute the aid.
The college may award the money they have taken away for other scholarships, including, for example, athletic scholarships-- which may not be the awarding group’s intention.
Scholarship money is raised through hard work and fundraising and the awarding group should be able to choose their criterion for its use (most often students with financial hardship).
Scholarship recipients say it essentially punishes them for taking the initiative to find other sources of financial aid.
The bill we passed led to a new law that took effect on July 1, making Maryland the first state to ban scholarship displacement by public colleges.
We are not done yet.
We want to extend this ban to private colleges, especially those that accept state funds.
We also want any colleges that may be exempt from the ban to give a full report to each student at the outset of their acceptance, telling them how much money they may raise and use in scholarships, according to federal FAFSA guidelines, in order to keep them from raising money they cannot use.
Be on the look-out for this in 2017.

I was lead sponsor on a bill to create a commission to reduce that impact.
The commission asked counties to account for the amount of testing they are doing, beyond PARCC.
This prompted some reflection by those jurisdictions and there were some voluntary decreases of benchmark and preparatory testing.
The commission report also prompted successful legislation to limit testing to 2% of the school year hours.
In 2018, I will introduce legislation that will reduce the impact of testing on the rest of the school.
Instead of shutting most instruction down to test for a whole morning/afternoon on one subject area, and pulling certified teachers out of other subject classes, my legislation will require that high stakes testing be limited to the time of one class period.
As well as being pedagogically sound - students of that age should not be expected to successfully sit for three consecutive hours of testing – this will also minimize disruption by having the test administered over a few days in the subject and class being tested.
Beyond that, the legislation will permit any staff member that the principal deems competent and willing to administer the test.
This will free up instructors to continue to practice good instruction and preparation while testing is occurring.
I know from my years in teaching that proctoring skills are accessible to more than only certified teachers and principals, who know their staff, will be able to choose anyone they know to be competent for this task.
As I have reported here previously, I co-sponsored legislation with my Howard County colleague Delegate Atterbeary, to create a workgroup to help direct the Kirwan Commission in their work on the implementation of universal Pre-K here in Maryland.
We met six times over the summer and we have made some important recommendations about the need for funding and a mixed delivery system involving county school systems and community providers.
This is a vital first step towards making a highquality early education available to every child in Maryland.
The return on investment of Pre-K instruction alone is over five times the cost of providing it.
So not only is it good for the students, it is good for the state.
Our report will be available publicly soon and the Kirwan Commission will finish its work by the end of December, allowing the legislature to take any action necessary in 2018.
Stay tuned!
ELECTION LEGISLATION: SCAM PACS AND ACCESS TO POLLS Last session I sponsored successful legislation to close a loophole that enabled sidestepping current contribution limits.
This will stop some of the big money being used to support candidates.
Now it is time to protect contributors to campaigns and causes as well.
In 2018, I will sponsor legislation that will regulate “scam PACs.” There are unregulated issue and candidate PACs that collect money with aggressive fundraising and then spend up to 90% of the funds on their own administrative salaries and costs, creating a financial benefit for the PAC’s creators, but not for the stated purpose of the PAC.
The legislation I will introduce will require public and visible reporting of fund use for these PACs in their fundraising campaigns.
It is simply truth in advertising to keep people who are contributing their hard earned money from being fooled.
One of the strongest beliefs I have about voting is that more people will vote when access is made easier.
And I want to get as many people to vote as we can.
Currently, anyone can request a mail in ballot (sometimes known as an absentee ballot) whether or not they will be in town on Election Day.
This makes sense to me.
I would much prefer to make my decisions at my kitchen table where I can consider my selections slowly and carefully.
I wouldn’t even have to make a trip to the polls to vote.
I could just mail it at my convenience.
However, several constituents have expressed that mailing feels uncomfortable, either because they feel it makes their vote unsafe or just because it lacks the notion of going to the polls.
I will introduce legislation to create “drop off boxes” at polling places for those who want to vote at home, but would still like to see their vote go “in the slot.” The board of elections of Maryland likes the idea.
They always want to encourage voters.
We may not be able to get the drop off boxes up and running for 2018, but the idea has taken seed and I will push to get this concept off the ground soon.
