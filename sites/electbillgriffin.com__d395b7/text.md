A Public Safety and Neighborhood Action Plan for Northeast Philadelphia Public safety is about more than crime statistics.
It is about whether families feel comfortable in their homes, seniors feel safe walking to the store, businesses can operate without fear of theft, and children can play outside in neighborhoods that are clean, stable, and secure.
Reducing crime requires strong law enforcement and consequences for people who commit crimes.
But it also requires something more: strong neighborhoods.
As State Representative, my approach will combine law enforcement, accountability, state resources, neighborhood revitalization, and community leadership.
I will use every tool available to the office—not only my vote in Harrisburg, but also the ability to bring people together, advocate for resources, demand answers, and help residents improve their own communities.
1 Confront Crime and Support Law Enforcement I will support the men and women of law enforcement and work to provide police with the resources necessary to prevent crime, apprehend offenders, and solve cases.
That means pursuing state resources for police recruitment and retention, training, investigative technology, forensic capabilities, and programs that improve violent-crime clearance rates.
I will also review Pennsylvania laws involving repeat offenders, sentencing, probation, and parole with public safety and victims at the forefront.
Government must also examine what happens after an arrest.
Residents deserve transparency and accountability throughout the criminal justice system from arrest and prosecution through sentencing, probation, and parole.
2 Use the Influence of the Office to Get Results A State Representative cannot direct Philadelphia Police, the District Attorney, or city departments.
But that does not mean a State Representative is powerless.
I will use the influence of the office to bring together the Philadelphia Police Department, City Council, city agencies, the Pennsylvania State Police, Attorney General, Pennsylvania Commission on Crime and Delinquency, schools, civic associations, businesses, and community leaders.
When a problem crosses governmental boundaries, my office will work to get the responsible people to the same table.
When residents cannot get an answer from government, we will advocate for one.
When taxpayer-funded programs are not producing results, I will ask questions and use legislative oversight to seek accountability.
I will establish a Northeast Philadelphia Public Safety and Neighborhood Working Group to identify problems, determine who is responsible for addressing them, establish objectives, and follow up on results.
3 Bring Public-Safety Resources Back to Northeast Philadelphia Pennsylvania provides millions of dollars through programs supporting law enforcement, crime investigation, violence prevention, victims, school safety, youth programs, and neighborhood improvement.
My office will actively identify these opportunities and help eligible police agencies, schools, nonprofits, civic organizations, and community groups connect with available resources.
Potential opportunities include funding administered through the Pennsylvania Commission on Crime and Delinquency for local law enforcement, violent-crime investigations, investigative technology, violence prevention, victims, school safety, and after-school and summer programs.
Pennsylvania economic-development programs can also encourage businesses to invest in qualifying neighborhood revitalization and crime-prevention projects.
But grants are taxpayer dollars, not free money.
I will advocate for Northeast Philadelphia to receive its fair share while also insisting upon transparency, measurable objectives, and accountability for results.
4 Rebuild Our Streets and Neighborhoods We cannot arrest our way out of every neighborhood problem.
Crime and quality of life are connected to the condition of our communities.
Abandoned properties, illegal dumping, broken streetlights, deteriorating commercial corridors, graffiti, abandoned vehicles, and neglected public spaces can undermine residents’ confidence in their neighborhoods.
My office will work with city and state officials to address these problems while helping residents organize to improve their own blocks.
I want to expand the concept of Adopt a Street by connecting residents with existing block captain programs, civic associations, Town Watch, city services, community organizations, businesses, schools, and available grants.
Government should help residents who are willing to help themselves.
That could mean organizing neighborhood cleanups, establishing new block captains, improving landscaping, addressing abandoned vehicles and properties, supporting Town Watch organizations, organizing block parties, improving commercial corridors, and connecting neighborhood organizations with businesses willing to invest in their communities.
5 Empower People to Help Themselves Some of the strongest resources in Northeast Philadelphia are already here: the people who live here.
My goal is not to create another government bureaucracy.
It is to make government work better for the people already working to improve their neighborhoods.
My office will help residents learn how to organize a block, access city services, find available grants, establish community partnerships, and communicate effectively with government agencies.
I also want to strengthen partnerships among block captains, civic associations, churches, schools, recreation centers, businesses, police, and community organizations.
When residents know one another, communicate with one another, watch out for one another, and take pride in their streets, neighborhoods become stronger.
6 Prevention Must Be Part of Public Safety Confronting crime also means preventing the next generation of crime.
I will support effective after-school programs, summer activities, mentoring, job opportunities, career and technical education, and other initiatives that give young people productive alternatives and pathways to success.
Programs receiving taxpayer funding should have measurable objectives.
We should continue programs that demonstrate results, improve those that can be improved, and reconsider those that cannot demonstrate that public money is accomplishing its intended purpose. ★ The Commitment I spent more than 35 years in education learning that leadership is not simply identifying a problem.
Leadership means bringing people together, developing a plan, assigning responsibility, measuring progress, and following through.

An Education Action Plan for Philadelphia Education has been my life’s work.
For more than 35 years, I served Philadelphia’s children and families as a special education teacher, administrator, and principal.
I have worked with students, parents, teachers, support staff, unions, administrators, and communities, and I have seen both what works in education and what happens when the system fails the children it is supposed to serve.
My education philosophy begins with one simple principle: Children come first.
Our schools exist because of children.
We fund them because of children.
Every major decision involving curriculum, staffing, spending, facilities, safety, or educational policy should therefore begin with one question: How will this benefit the children we are entrusted to educate?
Putting children first does not mean ignoring teachers, parents, administrators, or other adults.
Supporting the people who educate and care for our children ultimately benefits students.
But the needs of the system can never become more important than the children the system exists to serve.
1 Demand Academic Excellence and Accountability I believe in strong public schools.
But supporting public education does not mean accepting failure.
When large numbers of children are unable to read or perform mathematics at grade level, small improvements should not become an excuse for complacency.
Progress should be recognized, but persistently low achievement must be treated as a call to action.
We need to identify what is working in successful schools and school districts and bring those practices into struggling schools.
We need measurable improvement plans, transparent reporting, responsible use of taxpayer dollars, and accountability for results.
When something is not working, we should change it.
We cannot continue doing the same things while expecting different outcomes.
As State Representative, I will use legislation, funding, oversight, and the influence of the office to push for measurable improvement and greater accountability in Philadelphia education.
2 Make Every School Safe and Healthy Children cannot learn effectively when they do not feel safe, and teachers cannot teach effectively in an unsafe environment.
School safety begins with the buildings themselves.
Students and staff deserve schools that are free from dangerous asbestos exposure, have adequate ventilation, reasonable temperatures, functioning mechanical systems, and facilities that are properly maintained.
Philadelphia must establish clear priorities for repairing dangerous and hazardous conditions and be transparent about what needs to be fixed, how much it will cost, and when the work will be completed.
Safety also means maintaining order inside our schools.
Teachers and principals need clear, consistent standards for addressing violence, bullying, drugs, theft, and serious disruptions.
Consequences should be fair, consistently applied, and appropriate to the circumstances while respecting due process and protections for students with disabilities.
No student’s right to an education should include the right to prevent other children from learning and receiving their education.
3 Give Parents a Real Voice: Parents Are Our Partners Parents are not an obstacle to education.
They are essential partners in it.
Parents deserve timely, understandable information about their children’s education and a meaningful opportunity to participate in important decisions affecting them.
Schools should actively seek parental involvement, listen when parents raise concerns, and, in a reasonable manner, inform parents and seek appropriate permission when significant decisions affecting their children are being considered.
This is especially important in special education.
Parents of children with IEPs should understand their rights, the services available to their children, and how to navigate the system.
They should never feel that they need to be educational experts or attorneys simply to advocate effectively for their children.
Parents know their children.
Their voices matter.
4 Provide Educational Choice and Opportunity No two children are exactly alike, and one educational model cannot meet every child’s needs.
I support strong neighborhood public schools, but I also support meaningful educational choices for families, including charter schools, Catholic and other nonpublic schools, approved private schools, virtual education, and scholarship opportunities such as Pennsylvania’s Educational Improvement Tax Credit program (EITC).
Choice can also encourage schools to improve.
When families have alternatives, educational institutions must continually demonstrate that they are meeting the needs of the children they serve.
When a school is persistently failing a child, our first responsibility should be to improve that school.
But the child should not be required to sacrifice years of education waiting for adults to fix it.
The system should serve the child.
The child should not be required to serve the system.
5 Modernize Education for the 21st Century The world our children are entering is changing rapidly.
Education must change with it.
Traditional instruction still has an important place in education, but it cannot be our only approach.
Schools should increasingly teach students how to think critically, communicate, collaborate, solve problems, use technology responsibly, and apply what they learn to real situations.
Project-based learning can bring mathematics, science, reading, writing, technology, social studies, and other subjects together around meaningful problems and projects.
Our goal should not simply be producing better test-takers.
Our schools should be developing problem-solvers who are prepared for college, careers, trades, entrepreneurship, and life in the 21st century.
Teachers must also receive the training, resources, and support necessary to make that transition.
Their instructional time should be protected, and unnecessary processes that take them away from teaching should continually be reviewed and streamlined.
6 Reform Governance and Put Communities Back at the Table Philadelphia’s educational problems cannot be addressed without accountability at the top.
I support a comprehensive examination of School District finances, programs, administration, facilities, and academic performance.
Taxpayers, parents, and educators deserve to know where education dollars are going and whether those expenditures are producing results for children.
I also believe Philadelphia should reconsider how its Board of Education represents its communities.
I support developing a system that provides meaningful geographic representation from across Philadelphia, including representation corresponding to the different regions of the School District.
Community members and parents should have a meaningful role in identifying the people who represent them, even where final appointment authority remains with the mayor under existing law.
Board members should not simply represent a school system.
They should represent the families and communities that system serves.
If sustained reform does not produce meaningful improvement, Pennsylvania must also be willing to examine additional governance reforms rather than accepting continued failure as inevitable. ★ The Commitment For more than 35 years, I have worked inside Philadelphia’s schools.
I have taught children with disabilities.

Through that work, I collaborated with teachers and parents to help produce individualized education plans for every student with a disability in the School District of Philadelphia and IU 26 since 2005.
I have worked with teachers, support staff, unions, administrators, police, community organizations, and families.
That experience taught me that successful schools are not created by one person, one program, or one political party.
They are created when children, parents, teachers, administrators, communities, and government work together toward a common purpose.
There will always be disagreements about education.
But there should be one principle upon which all of us can agree: Our schools exist for our children.
Every dollar we spend, every policy we adopt, every program we create, and every decision we make should ultimately be measured against that responsibility.
Children come first.
Together we can make things better.
