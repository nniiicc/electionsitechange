Trust in Government Starts with Transparency
Most people, regardless of political affiliation, agree on at least one thing: government should be transparent.
It is one of those rare ideas that brings nearly everyone together.
Ask ten people what they want from government, and you will get ten different answers.
Ask those same ten people if they want transparency, and suddenly you have a unanimous vote.
If only everything were that easy.
And yet, despite broad agreement on the principle, trust in government continues to decline.
That raises an important question.
If everyone agrees transparency matters, why does it so often feel like it is missing?
Part of the problem is that transparency is often misunderstood.
Too many times, it is reduced to a technical requirement rather than a meaningful commitment.
Meetings are posted.
Documents are filed.
Legal boxes are checked.
Somewhere, buried three clicks deep on a website, there is probably a PDF that technically answers your question… assuming you know exactly what you are looking for and have a free afternoon to find it.
On paper, everything may be “public.”
But that does not always mean it is clear.
There is a difference between making information available and making it understandable.
There is a difference between compliance and communication.
True transparency is not about doing the minimum required to avoid criticism.
It is about intentionally helping people understand what is happening and why.
In my time serving on the town council, I have seen how important that distinction is.
Local government operates closer to the people than any other level, and yet even there, it can sometimes feel difficult for residents to follow decisions or understand the reasoning behind them.
Not because anyone is trying to hide something, but because the process itself is not always designed with clarity in mind.
Let’s be honest, if you have ever tried to follow a budget discussion for the first time, it can feel like stepping into the middle of a conversation where everyone else already read the script.
Acronyms get thrown around, numbers move quickly, and by the time you think you understand one piece, the discussion has already moved on to something else.
That is where trust begins to erode.
When people do not understand how decisions are made, they begin to fill in the gaps themselves.
Assumptions take the place of information.
Frustration replaces confidence.
Even good decisions can be questioned if they are not clearly explained.
Transparency, then, is not just about access.
It is about clarity.
It means communicating decisions in a way that people can actually follow.
It means explaining not only what is being done, but why it is being done.
It means ensuring that important information is not buried in lengthy documents or scattered across multiple sources, but presented in a way that is straightforward and accessible.
But transparency alone is not enough.
It must be paired with accountability.
Accountability means that those in leadership are willing to stand behind their decisions.
It means answering questions directly, not avoiding them.
It means acknowledging mistakes when they happen and correcting them rather than hoping no one noticed.
Because they usually do.
No leader gets every decision right.
That is not the standard people are looking for.
What they are looking for is honesty, consistency, and a willingness to take responsibility.
People are far more willing to accept a difficult decision that is clearly explained than a perfect decision that is poorly communicated.
When transparency and accountability work together, something important begins to happen.
Trust is built.
Not overnight, and not through a single decision, but over time.
People begin to see that their leaders are not just making decisions, but are willing to explain them.
Not just holding authority, but accepting responsibility.
That kind of trust changes the relationship between government and the people it serves.
It turns frustration into engagement.
It replaces suspicion with understanding.
It creates a foundation where disagreements can still exist, but without the assumption that something is being hidden.
At its core, leadership is not about control.
It is about stewardship.
Those entrusted with positions of authority are responsible not only for making decisions, but for making them in a way that respects the people they serve.
That includes being open, being clear, and being willing to be held accountable.
Transparency is not a slogan.
It is a discipline.
And if we practice it well enough, maybe someday finding information from the government will not feel like a scavenger hunt.
