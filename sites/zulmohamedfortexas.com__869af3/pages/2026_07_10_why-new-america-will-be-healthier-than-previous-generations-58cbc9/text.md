Every American should have access to quality medical care without facing financial ruin because they became sick, suffered an injury, or needed lifesaving medication.
We, as a country, can make universal health care a central part of a broader effort to reduce costs, improve access, and create a system that puts patients ahead of bureaucracy and excessive profits.
As senator, I would work toward a system in which every American has access to affordable, dependable health coverage while protecting patients’ ability to choose their doctors and receive the care they need.
Create a Path Toward Universal Coverage Millions of Americans remain uninsured or underinsured.
Even people with insurance can face deductibles and out-of-pocket expenses that make medical care difficult to afford.
Congress has the authority to create and fund federal health programs, regulate aspects of the insurance market, and reform existing programs such as Medicare and Medicaid.
A senator can introduce legislation, build coalitions, negotiate reforms, and vote for measures that move the country toward universal coverage.
The objective should be simple: every American should have meaningful access to health care.
Universal coverage could be pursued through different models, including expanding Medicare eligibility, creating a federal public insurance option, strengthening existing public programs, or developing a broader national health insurance system.
The details should be debated carefully, but doing nothing should not be our long-term policy.
Let Americans Choose a Public Health Insurance Option One practical step toward universal health care would be creating a strong public insurance option available to Americans who want it.
People should not be forced to remain with an insurance company they dislike simply because their employer selected it.
A public option could compete with private insurers while giving individuals and families another choice.
If private insurers can provide better service at competitive prices, they should be able to compete.
But Americans should also have access to a dependable public alternative.
Lower Prescription Drug Prices No American should have to choose between buying medication and paying for groceries, housing, or electricity.
Congress can take stronger action to lower prescription drug costs by expanding the government’s ability to negotiate prices, increasing competition, addressing anticompetitive practices, encouraging faster access to generic and biosimilar medications, and improving transparency throughout the pharmaceutical supply chain.
Taxpayers also invest billions of dollars in medical research.
When public funding contributes to the development of important treatments, the public deserves a system that considers affordability as well as innovation.
Protect People With Preexisting Conditions A universal health care system must guarantee that people cannot be denied meaningful coverage because of their medical history.
Cancer, diabetes, heart disease, pregnancy, disabilities, and other health conditions should never become reasons for someone to lose access to affordable insurance.
Coverage also needs to be dependable.
Insurance means very little if a person technically has a policy but cannot afford the deductible required to use it.
Expand Access to Primary and Preventive Care America spends enormous amounts treating advanced illnesses that sometimes could have been identified or managed earlier.
Universal health care should emphasize primary and preventive care, including regular physical examinations, screenings, vaccinations, prenatal care, and management of chronic conditions.
Congress can also strengthen community health centers and other providers serving rural and underserved communities.
Making basic care easier to obtain can help patients address health problems before they become emergencies.
Address the Shortage of Doctors and Nurses Insurance coverage does not guarantee access if there are not enough medical professionals available.
America needs a long-term strategy for expanding its health care workforce.
Congress can support additional medical residency positions, nursing education, scholarships, loan assistance, and incentives for medical professionals who practice in underserved communities.
We should also examine unnecessary barriers that prevent qualified professionals from entering or remaining in the health care workforce while maintaining strong standards for patient safety.
Protect Rural Hospitals Universal coverage must include rural America.
Rural hospitals and clinics can face severe financial pressures, leaving residents traveling long distances for emergency treatment, maternity services, specialists, and routine medical care.
Federal health policy should recognize the unique challenges of rural medicine.
Congress can support rural hospitals, telehealth infrastructure, medical workforce incentives, and innovative approaches that keep essential services available in smaller communities.
Make Mental Health Part of Health Care Mental health care should not be treated as an afterthought.
A comprehensive system should improve access to qualified mental health professionals and strengthen the integration of mental and physical health services.
Congress can also work to strengthen behavioral health workforces, community treatment programs, crisis services, and enforcement of requirements intended to ensure appropriate mental health coverage.
When someone needs treatment, the system should make obtaining help easier—not create another obstacle course.
Reduce Medical Debt An unexpected illness should not destroy a family’s financial future.
Medical debt can follow families for years and can result from circumstances completely outside their control.
Expanding affordable coverage is one of the most important ways to prevent that debt from occurring in the first place.
Congress should also examine hospital billing transparency, financial-assistance practices, surprise charges, and consumer protections surrounding medical debt.
Health care policy should recognize a basic reality: getting sick is not a financial decision.
Cut Administrative Waste One of the greatest frustrations in American health care is its complexity.
Patients, doctors, hospitals, insurers, pharmacies, and government programs operate within an enormous administrative system.
Physicians and nurses should be spending their time treating patients rather than fighting unnecessary paperwork.
Health care reform should simplify billing, improve electronic communication between systems, standardize appropriate administrative processes, and make prices easier for patients to understand.
Universal health care should not mean creating more bureaucracy.
It should be an opportunity to simplify the bureaucracy we already have.
Preserve Choice and Quality Expanding coverage should not mean sacrificing quality.
Patients should continue to have meaningful choices among doctors, hospitals, and other qualified providers.
Doctors should retain the ability to make medical decisions based on the needs of their patients.
Any universal system must also encourage medical research, technological advancement, new treatments, and innovation.
The goal should be universal access combined with high-quality care.
Make Health Care Reform Fiscally Responsible Universal health care requires an honest discussion about costs.
Americans already pay for health care through insurance premiums, deductibles, copayments, taxes, employer contributions, prescription costs, and other expenses.
Reform should therefore consider the total amount families and businesses spend—not simply the size of a government program.
Any proposal I support should include transparent financing, realistic cost projections, protections against waste and fraud, and mechanisms for controlling unnecessary spending.
Promises are easy.
Building a sustainable system is harder, and that is what Congress should focus on.
Health Care Should Serve Patients America has extraordinary doctors, nurses, hospitals, researchers, medicines, and medical technology.
What we lack is a system that consistently makes those resources accessible and affordable to everyone.
As senator, I would fight for a path toward universal health care that expands coverage, lowers prescription costs, protects people with preexisting conditions, strengthens rural and community health care, improves mental health services, reduces medical debt, and holds the health care industry accountable.
Universal health care should ultimately be about something much simpler than politics: when an American becomes sick, that person should be able to get medical care.
No family should have to fear bankruptcy because someone gets cancer.
No senior should have to ration medication because it is too expensive.
No working parent should avoid seeing a doctor because of a deductible they cannot afford.
A healthier America will also be a stronger America.
