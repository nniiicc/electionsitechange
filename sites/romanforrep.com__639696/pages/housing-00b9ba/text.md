1) Protect (Human) Homebuyers The playbook is simple.
A private equity firm arrives with cash and buys homes by the dozen, sometimes an entire subdivision straight from the builder.
A young couple with a pre-approval letter cannot compete with an all-cash offer that closes in two weeks.
So the couple loses.
Then the firm converts the houses to rentals, and because it now owns a large share of the neighborhood’s rental stock, it sets the price.
The same couple who couldn’t buy the house now pays whatever the landlord asks to rent one just like it.
This does not happen to neighborhoods in isolation.
Take Georgia, for example.
Institutional landlords now own roughly 72,000 single-family rental homes in metro Atlanta—about 30 percent of that market and ten times the national share.
In Paulding County, corporations own 78 percent of all single-family rentals.
In Henry County, 64 percent.
Three companies alone control almost 20,000 houses across the core metro counties.¹ And predictably, in the zip codes where these firms concentrated, homeownership falls and rents rise.
We should act now to prevent this from happening to Metro Detroit.
Once a firm owns a third of a county’s rental stock, no legislature can unwind it.
Prevention costs nothing.
The cure costs a generation of homeowners.
2) Build More Starter Homes Why do so few builders put up attainable single-family homes nowadays?
It isn’t a conspiracy if you follow the incentives.
Michigan funds local government through property taxes, and property taxes track assessed value.
Every township, city, and village therefore follows the same logic: put the most valuable house possible on every lot.
A $700,000 home on a half-acre generates more revenue than a $300,000 home on the same half-acre, and it demands roughly the same in roads, water, and police.
Nobody at the planning commission has to say this out loud.
The math says it for them.
The fashionable answer embraced by leftists and big developers is “YIMBYism”—stripping local governments of their zoning authority so that developers build whatever the market will bear.
I oppose this policy, and I want to be precise about why.
“Yes in my backyard” policies achieve affordability through density.
They deliver cheaper units by stacking more of them on each parcel: fourplexes on single-family lots, apartment blocks where subdivisions stood.
These types of developments are not what my constituents mean when they can’t afford a house.
They mean a house.
Worse, YIMBYism leaves the underlying incentive untouched.
The municipality still wants maximum revenue per parcel; it simply loses the tools to shape what developers build.
Developers gain a free hand to remake community character, and residents who spent decades investing in their neighborhood hear that they should take it up with Lansing.
My approach is different, and to my knowledge no one in Lansing has proposed it.
Instead of overriding local decisions, change the payoff.
Michigan already returns a share of state revenue to municipalities through revenue sharing.
Tie an increased share to attainable housing: when a community approves starter homes, the state makes up the property-tax revenue that community forgoes.
Local control stays intact.
Community character stays intact.
The only thing that changes is that doing the right thing stops costing our communities money.
3) Cut Building Costs Every conversation about housing eventually arrives at the same number.
The Home Builders Association of Michigan estimates that regulation adds roughly $94,000 to the price of a new home in this state.² When John Bitely, a West Michigan builder, first heard that figure, he didn’t believe it.
He told the association the number looked inflated, then spent three days going through his own project costs to prove it wrong.
He came within 1 percent.³ ​ The National Association of Home Builders’ 2026 updated estimate puts the national figure at roughly $132,000, and building code changes over the past decade alone account for about $24,000 of it.⁴ Michigan builds fewer homes as a share of its housing stock than every Great Lakes state except Illinois.⁵ Something stands in the way, and it is not a shortage of dirt. ​ The fix begins with the code.
Not every regulation is red tape.
Fire codes save lives, and nobody proposes to touch them.
But Michigan has layered on requirements over the past decade whose gains in safety and efficiency never matched their cost.
The 2021 energy code, which the state adopted over builders’ objections, adds an estimated $15,000 per home—in exchange for savings so modest that the homebuilders association argues the code violates Michigan’s own seven-year payback law.⁶ We should review the most recently adopted regulations first, with a simple test: did this rule deliver real gains in safety or efficiency, or did it deliver only cost?
Rules that fail the test go. ​ Permitting is the other half.
Permitting decisions belong with local governments, but local control cannot mean unlimited delay.
Today a municipality can require a traffic study, then a wetland study, then a revised traffic study, then a new site plan, with no deadline and no end.
Builders don’t fight this; they leave.
The state’s homebuilders association points to one recent Michigan project that took four years because of the back-and-forth with local government.⁷ ​ The reform is straightforward.
Local governments keep the decision, but they must make it within a required timeline.
They can approve, deny, or request specific, enumerated changes.
They cannot request studies indefinitely until the developer throws up his hands and walks away.
A “no” is a legitimate answer.
Silence is not.
