1) Fix the Grid!
Severe weather is inevitable, and we can all understand occasional outages.
But the sluggish response time to September’s major storm was no anomaly.
In fact, it’s par for the course when it comes to power restoration in Michigan.
Last year, we ranked dead last out of the 50 states and Washington, D.C. in restoration speed.
States with similar climates like Ohio and Indiana resolve their outages in less than half the time we do.¹ When you look at the state of our energy infrastructure, it’s pretty obvious just how far behind we are.
Why do we continue to drape wires through tree canopies on wooden poles, just waiting for the next ice storm?
The excuse that “undergrounding is too expensive” is a result of Michigan-specific policy failures.
Michigan law assesses a utility’s poles, wires, and mains as personal property, taxed by the township where they sit.² When the legislature exempted manufacturers’ equipment from the personal property tax in 2014, it specifically carved out utility property.³ That means every dollar DTE spends burying a line carries a property tax bill on top, and that bill lands on your electric rates.
So, when the Michigan Public Service Commission (MPSC) rejects an undergrounding project as too expensive, the tax is part of why.
We should treat grid hardening the way we already treat new factories.
Extend the manufacturing exemption to new undergrounding and storm-hardened distribution equipment, and reimburse townships for the lost revenue through the same essential-services mechanism the state uses for manufacturers.
Utilities will lose an excuse, and it won’t cost local governments a dime.
2) Hold Them Accountable When discussing the topic of utility companies, I often get asked, “Why don’t we break up the monopolies and let companies compete for distribution?” This is a great question, because this is how other network services like cell service and broadband function.
When it comes to energy, however, scale makes a monopoly model more practical.
Under the current system, there is only one set of the poles, wires, and transformers that physically connect your house to the grid.
"Competition" would mean a second and third set of poles down every street.
This would make any crisis response a nightmare.
When an ice storm knocks out power to 400,000 homes at 2 o’clock in the morning, you want one company with every truck in the state under one dispatcher, not three companies guarding their own trucks and arguing over whose block comes first.
The problem is not the existence of the monopolies themselves—it’s the flawed incentives we’ve set for them.
Michigan’s current model for regulating utility rates is a cost-of-service model.
In this system, the utility spends money on the grid, and the MPSC grants it a fixed return on that spending (i.e., a fixed return on equity).⁴ That means the utility makes the same return regardless of how much time you spend without power.
We can change this.
In a performance-based model, the MPSC sets measurable targets for outcomes such as power restoration times, outages per customer, and cost per customer against peer competitors.
If the utility manages to beat the target, it earns a higher return on equity.
But if it falls short, return on equity is reduced or customers are entitled to refunds.
Michigan took its first step toward this model in 2025, but the MPSC capped the total incentive and penalty at $10 million for 2026.⁵ Against $1.4 billion in profits, that is barely a rounding error.
I propose significantly increasing this cap.
Some may call this micromanagement.
It is the opposite.
Micromanagement is what we do now: the MPSC spends a rate case litigating whether DTE’s tree-trimming budget is prudent, line by line, for months.
A performance-based approach stops telling DTE how to do its homework and starts grading its test results.
3) Cut Building Costs Changing our regulatory incentives is just one of many tools available to make energy more affordable.
If we want to bring down costs in the long run, the mix of energy sources we decide to use must predominantly be determined by cost and reliability.
Any mandate that portions of the energy supply must come from certain sources prevents utilities from optimizing for cost, forcing them to pass the bill onto ratepayers.
Democrats in Lansing passed one of these mandates in 2023.
Public Act 235 requires 50 percent renewable electricity by 2030, up from about 12 percent when the bill passed, then 60 percent by 2035 and 100 percent “clean” by 2040.⁶ The commission’s own chairman told lawmakers the 60 percent target alone requires roughly 209,000 additional acres of wind and solar.
To find that land, the legislature gave the commission authority to override local zoning.⁷ Washington enacted a mandate of its own last year.
In May 2025, the Department of Energy ordered Consumers Energy to keep the J.H.
Campbell coal plant running past its scheduled retirement, re-extending the order five more times.
Consumers reported a net cost of $259 million⁸ through June of this year—about $615,000 a day—which it now seeks to recover from customers.⁹ When the state or federal government picks the fuel, the utility stops optimizing for price and reliability and starts optimizing for compliance.
An all-of-the-above approach means the MPSC’s resource planning process weighs all sources on the same two criteria, and the cheapest reliable mix wins.
