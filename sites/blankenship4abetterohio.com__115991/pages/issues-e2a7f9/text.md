EDUCATION
In Ohio, public tax dollars are constitutionally required to fund only public schools.
Despite this, EdChoice Expansion Voucher Recipients use these funds without state oversight or accountability.
According to Article 6, Section 2 of the Ohio Constitution, the General Assembly must ensure a thorough and efficient system of common schools through taxation or other means, supplemented by the school trust fund.
Importantly, no religious group or sect is permitted to have any exclusive right to or control over any portion of the state's school funds. [1]In 2025, Ohio allocated $1.09 billion to private school vouchers, including $492.8 million directed specifically toward Education Choice Expansion Voucher Providers.
This marks the second consecutive year of nearly universal school voucher availability in the state, showing substantial growth in the EdChoice program.
EdChoice Expansion vouchers aim to help families afford private school tuition, and the program is especially popular among those already attending private schools.
[2] Notably, many recipients of these vouchers do not fall into the low-income category.
The top scholarship amounts are $6,166 for grades K-8 and $8,408 for grades 9–12, with eligibility based on income up to 450% of the Federal Poverty Level ($140,400 for a family of four in 2024).
For families earning above this threshold, the scholarship amounts decrease as income rises.
[3]HB 186, passed by the Ohio Legislature in 2025, ends The Fair School Funding Plan and will significantly reduce funding for school districts.
[4] In Tax Year 2027, 170 districts could lose at least 10% of their property tax revenue, resulting in potential layoffs, larger classes, and fewer academic programs.
[5] Eliminating this funding, which helped rural and poor districts compete with wealthier ones, means underfunded schools will receive nearly $3 billion ($2,753,552,883) less. [6]Sources for this article:

Yes To SB-374
This bill proposes changes to existing law that currently allows certain data center projects to receive partial or full exemptions on sales and use taxes for equipment and infrastructure.
Why This Matters to District 68: More than half of Ohio’s 217 data centers are Central Ohio.
Licking County currently has 2 operational data centers (Meta and Google), 3 under construction (Microsoft, Cologix, and Amazon) and 6 more planned.
If completed, the county would have 11 data centers.
These are physical facilities that house computers, servers, and systems used to run AI applications.
The environmental and economic impacts these centers should be of concern, especially in rural Ohio, where tech companies are buying up farmland.
These facilities have an enormous environmental footprint.
They can exceed 10,000 square feet, house over 5,000 servers, and consume vast amounts of water and electricity around the clock.
They produce air, water, and noise pollution.
Data centers’ water use could strain local supplies, leading to water shortages, groundwater conflicts, and aquifer contamination.
Data centers’ use of electricity will drive up the cost of electricity and gas for consumers.
Approximately 80% of water used evaporates, releasing chemicals, some toxic, into the air and water.
SB 374 is an attempt to put some breaks on centers that consume massive amounts of resources while putting very little money back into the economies of the counties that host them.
BILLS I OPPOSE
HB-754, Require fetal death register; fetal heartbeat life certificate: I oppose this bill because it expands government control into deeply personal medical decisions.
It creates a system that tracks pregnancies and outcomes, places additional burdens on doctors, and risks turning moments of personal loss into matters of state oversight.
Women experiencing miscarriage should be met with compassion, not paperwork.
Doctors should be focused on care, not compliance.
This bill goes too far, and it moves us in the wrong direction.
SB-1, Advance Ohio Higher Education Act: SB-1 fundamentally shifts control of our universities away from educators and into the hands of politicians.
It weakens workers’ rights by stripping collective bargaining protections and banning faculty strikes.
It dictates what can and can’t be taught, eliminates programs based on arbitrary thresholds, and creates systems that discourage open discussion in the classroom.
The bill talks about intellectual diversity, but you don’t achieve that by banning programs or restricting ideas.
Real education is about critical thinking, exposure to different perspectives, and the ability to challenge and grow.
This bill moves us in the opposite direction.
It centralizes power, limits flexibility, and risks undermining the quality of higher education in Ohio.
School Vouchers: I respect that every parent wants the best for their child, but we also have a responsibility to protect and strengthen our public education system.
Right now, Ohio’s voucher program raises serious constitutional concerns about whether we are meeting our obligation to maintain a strong, fully funded public school system.
When you look at the data, the majority of voucher recipients were already attending private schools.
That means this program isn’t expanding access, it’s subsidizing decisions that were already being made.
At the same time, public dollars are being pulled out of public schools, leaving fewer resources for the majority of families, especially working- and middle-class communities that rely on them the most.
If we’re serious about opportunity, we should be investing in the system that serves everyone, not weakening it.
