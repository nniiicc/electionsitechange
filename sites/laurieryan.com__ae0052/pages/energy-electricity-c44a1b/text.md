This is a relatively narrow enabling/incentive bill rather than a massive mandate, but here are substantive criticisms based on the text, similar policies, and evidence on floating solar (FPV):
1.
Subsidies and Costs Passed to Ratepayers/Taxpayers: It authorizes grants and financing support via NYSERDA (often funded through ratepayer surcharges or state budgets).
Floating solar already has higher upfront capital costs (typically 10-25% more than ground-mounted due to platforms, anchoring, mooring, and corrosion-resistant gear).
Taxpayers/ratepayers subsidize what may not be the most cost-effective option when rooftops, parking canopies, brownfields, or degraded land are available.
2.
Environmental Risks to Aquatic Ecosystems:
• Shading and reduced photosynthesis: Panels block sunlight, potentially harming algae, aquatic plants, and the base of the food chain (insects, fish).
Impacts vary by site, depth, and coverage.
• Temperature and oxygen changes: Can cool surface water, alter stratification/habitat suitability for fish (warm- vs. cold-water species), and affect dissolved oxygen.
Cornell research found increased greenhouse gas emissions (nearly 27%) on small ponds.
3.
Health risks
Potential chemical leaching from materials/panels, altered hydrodynamics, bird/waterfowl impacts, and long-term biodiversity effects.
The bill calls for “best practices” and monitoring, but this is reactive—better to avoid risky sites upfront.
Reservoirs used for drinking water raise public health concerns about water quality.
4.
Technical and Reliability Issues:
• Higher maintenance due to corrosion, biofouling, wave/wind stress, and difficult access.
Durability data is limited (many installations <10 years old).
Extreme weather (winds, waves, ice in NY winters) has damaged systems elsewhere.
• Anchoring/mooring challenges in varying water levels, currents, or freezing conditions common in New York.
5.
Opportunity Cost and Better Alternatives: New York has abundant rooftops, parking lots, landfills, and brownfields for solar.
Policies pushing floating solar (and overriding local concerns) divert focus from lower-risk, cheaper options.
Critics (e.g., some Republicans and residents) prefer parking canopies or rooftops over altering water bodies.
6.
Limited Oversight and “Education” Framing: The bill is promotional (incentives + education) with vague criteria.
“TBD” fiscal note and reliance on future rulemaking mean limited upfront scrutiny.
It could lead to rushed or poorly sited projects if grants create momentum.
7.
Broader Context in NY Policy: This fits a pattern of aggressive renewables targets amid reliability concerns, interconnection delays, and rising electricity costs.
Floating solar is niche/innovative in some contexts (e.g., Asia), but pushing it via subsidies in a temperate climate with many natural water bodies risks unintended consequences without strong, site-specific environmental reviews.
Bottom line: The bill is not catastrophic—it’s mostly enabling study/grants with some monitoring language—but it uses public resources to promote a technology with higher costs, ecological trade-offs, and engineering challenges when superior low-impact alternatives exist.
Outcomes depend heavily on implementation, siting rules, and actual monitoring.
Stronger bills would prioritize degraded land/rooftops first and require rigorous, independent environmental impact studies before any grants.

AI Summary
This bill aims to prohibit the sale of gasoline-powered leaf blowers and lawn mowers in the state of New York by January 1, 2027.
Specifically, the bill requires the commissioner of environmental conservation to develop and implement rules and regulations that will effectively ban the retail sale of these gas-powered lawn care devices.
The proposed legislation is part of a broader effort to reduce emissions from small gas-powered landscaping equipment, which are known to contribute to air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions.
By setting a clear deadline of January 1, 2027, the bill provides manufacturers, retailers, and consumers with a transition period to adapt to the new requirements, potentially encouraging the adoption of electric or battery-powered alternatives for lawn maintenance equipment.
The bill would amend the state's environmental conservation law by adding a new section that mandates this sales prohibition, and it is set to take effect immediately upon passage.
