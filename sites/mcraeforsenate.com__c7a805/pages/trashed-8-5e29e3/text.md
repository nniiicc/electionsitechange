Rail Is Risk Reduction.
Not Job Elimination.
District 20 depends on transportation corridors that are already under strain.
The question isn’t whether we change how freight moves.
It’s whether we design the change — or react after something goes wrong.
Canyon Roads Weren’t Built
for This Much Pressure.
District 20 has two distinct transportation challenges.
In the Uintah Basin, thousands of truck movements carry energy products through rural communities and mountain corridors every day — raw, volatile product on roads designed for a different era.
In the Wasatch Back, canyon roads that were never designed for resort traffic are now handling commuters, freight, tourists, and emergency vehicles all at once.
Both problems have the same root cause: transportation systems that weren’t planned for the load they’re now carrying.
Hazardous energy products moving by default highway trucking through rural communities and canyon corridors.
Volume that doesn’t scale.
Risk that doesn’t have to be there.
Provo Canyon, SR-248, and US-189 handling tourism, commuters, and freight simultaneously.
Emergency routing compromised.
Air quality worsened by concentrated vehicle emissions.
Rail as a Hardened
Freight Corridor.
The Uinta Railway — projected to be operating around 2029 — has the potential to move energy products from the Basin by rail instead of relying entirely on Highway 40.
That’s not about eliminating truck jobs.
It’s about decoupling hazardous highway exposure from long-haul freight.
The model: trucks haul locally and unload into rail terminals, where rail handles the long-distance transport.
That keeps drivers working, keeps the local economy strong, and reduces some of the long-haul pressure on our highways.
- Crude moves by hardened rail corridor — not default highway trucking
- Trucks shift to last-mile, lower-risk local work — jobs stay
- Fixed rail terminals allow unloading into tank storage — reduces mobile exposure
- Emergency response centered on fixed facilities — not improvised on canyon roads
- Throughput predictability improves — energy economics stabilize
The Spiro Tunnel
Deserves a Serious Look.
I submitted comments into the Environmental Impact Statement for the Heber Valley corridor because that corridor is one of the biggest transportation pressure points in the region.
Most people only think about it as a highway issue.
If we only expand roads we’re going to keep chasing congestion forever.
The Spiro Tunnel — an existing tunnel from the mining era connecting Heber to Park City — represents a corridor that’s already there.
If light rail were ever feasible through that corridor, it could allow commuters to move between Heber and Park City without adding more pressure to SR-248 and the canyon roads.
Ideas like that require engineering review, environmental study, and community input.
But planning ahead means being willing to look at real solutions now — not waiting until the infrastructure problem becomes impossible to fix.
The Olympics Are a Deadline,
Not a Celebration.
The 2034 Winter Olympics create a hard planning deadline for District 20’s transportation systems.
By then we need to demonstrate that we can move people and freight safely under peak demand conditions — without overloading highways, compromising air quality, or blocking emergency routing.
- Passenger and mass transit rail planning provides corridor redundancy
- Rail protects emergency routing when highway capacity is maxed
- Reduced vehicle concentration means better air quality during inversions
- Transit options reduce ski season stacking on canyon roads
- Rail plus trails builds recreation infrastructure alongside transportation
