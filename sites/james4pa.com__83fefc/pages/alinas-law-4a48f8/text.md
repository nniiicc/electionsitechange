Legislative Priorities · Proposed Legislation Alina’s Law: stronger protection from domestic violence.
I support advancing Alina’s Law with a practical framework for court-ordered electronic monitoring, proximity alerts, and a coordinated response when someone subject to a Protection From Abuse order poses a continuing, substantial danger.
Read the Full Proposal (PDF) My Commitment Give courts and survivors better tools to respond to danger.
A protection order needs a workable enforcement plan.
My priority is to advance legislation that connects carefully ordered monitoring with timely alerts, trained personnel, and clear responsibility for responding.
This revised proposal builds on the existing effort to pass Alina’s Law.
It pairs protections for survivors with evidence-based risk assessment, notice and hearings, judicial findings, and limits on the collection and use of location data.
How the Proposal Would Work Protection backed by a clear process.
Monitoring based on risk and judicial findings Courts would consider a validated lethality or intimate partner violence risk assessment and relevant history.
Monitoring would generally require clear and convincing evidence of a continuing, substantial risk and a finding that less restrictive conditions are inadequate.
The draft also establishes a presumption of monitoring in specified aggravated-assault cases, with written reasons required for an exception.
Exclusion zones and proximity alerts A judge would set appropriate distances around a survivor’s home, workplace, school, or other locations.
Approved technology could also support a moving protective radius, with narrow exceptions for court-ordered appointments or custody exchanges.
Survivors control participation in alerts Participation in the alert system would require informed consent.
A survivor could decline or withdraw without a penalty.
The court would then review whether monitoring without survivor alerts remains necessary.
A staffed response around the clock Trained personnel would handle alerts 24 hours a day, seven days a week, with protocols for contacting the parties and coordinating with law enforcement.
Verified breaches and device tampering would be documented and escalated.
Due process and privacy safeguards The proposal requires notice and an opportunity to be heard, provides counsel for indigent defendants at monitoring hearings, and allows review as circumstances change.
Location data would be limited, encrypted, subject to access restrictions, and deleted under retention standards.
Monitoring orders would also prohibit firearm and ammunition purchase or possession for their duration.
Funding and accountability A statewide funding framework would support installation, monitoring, and 24/7 coverage, with cost-sharing based on ability to pay.
Anonymized annual reports and periodic evaluations would help assess safety outcomes and program effectiveness.
These are features of the revised proposal linked above.
Electronic monitoring can support enforcement and warning; it cannot guarantee a survivor’s safety.
The Existing Legislative Effort Carry Alina’s Law forward.
Alina’s Law is named for Alina Sheykhet, a University of Pittsburgh student killed in 2017.
Her family and Alina’s Light have advocated for stronger protections for survivors of domestic violence.
Earlier Pennsylvania proposals sought to authorize electronic monitoring in PFA cases.
House Bill 1150 in the 2023–2024 session was amended into a different domestic-violence funding proposal and ended the session without becoming law.
The effort continued in the 2025–2026 session through House Bill 104, sponsored by Representative Anita Kulik, and Senate Bill 123, sponsored by Senator Vincent Hughes.
As of October 8, 2026, the official histories list HB 104 as referred to House Judiciary on January 14, 2025, and SB 123 as referred to Senate Judiciary on January 22, 2025.
The revised proposal on this page is a campaign legislative priority, separate from the filed text of those bills.
