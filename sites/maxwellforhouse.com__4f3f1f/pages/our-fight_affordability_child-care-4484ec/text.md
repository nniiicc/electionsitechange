Finding a place The support gap After school What changed Our fight The price is one barrier.
A place is another.
Even if a family can pay, there may not be a licensed place available.
In 2024, Montana’s licensed capacity could cover less than half of estimated demand.
For infant care, it was about one-third.
The state counted about 21,100 licensed places against 46,290 children under six in working-parent households.
More capacity is needed, but a license alone does not guarantee an open, staffed place.

Gaps are 100% minus the reported coverage.
What this gap does and does not measure Estimated demand is based on children in households where all available parents work, not a waiting-list count.
The infant-demand estimate uses children under two.
Licensed capacity is not the number of vacant places or children actually enrolled.
Informal care and some tribal, school and other legally unlicensed programs are not captured in the licensed-capacity measure.
Eligible for help.
Still not reached.
A program on paper is not the same as help reaching a family.
A Montana Budget & Policy Center analysis estimated that Best Beginnings reached only a small share of income-eligible children.
The gap was especially wide for school-age children.
The report identified a mismatch between licensing rules and afterschool programs as a barrier to accepting scholarships.

The 94% figure is 100% minus 6%.
How to read these estimates The analysis uses fiscal year 2024 recipients and population and poverty estimates from earlier Census data.
Its eligibility denominator estimates children in families below 185% of the federal poverty level; it is not a count of approved applications.
Nonparticipation does not mean a family applied and was denied.
These are historical participation estimates, not current eligibility advice or a finding that every nonparticipant needs paid care.
School ends.
The workday doesn’t.
Families need options after the last bell and during school breaks, not only before kindergarten.
Yet one major afterschool funding round awarded less than a third of the dollars Montana programs requested.
For the 2024–25 school year, 26 applicants requested funding and 13 grants were awarded.
That is a gap in support for programs, not a measure of the entire state’s afterschool budget.

Some progress.
Unfinished work.
The 2025 Legislature created a new funding foundation.
But proposals to widen scholarship access and support the people providing care did not become law.
Passing something is a start.
Closing the gaps is the test.
Enacted · HB 924 A new funding foundation The Montana Early Childhood Account received a one-time $10 million transfer and a share of GO Trust interest.
It creates a way to support affordability and the workforce, but it is not a guaranteed $10 million every year.
GO Trust overview, August 2025 .
This describes the funding structure, not measured results or grants already delivered.
Vetoed · HB 456 Support for caregivers stopped short A bill to qualify most child care workers for Best Beginnings passed the Legislature but was vetoed.
The people families rely on to provide care can face the same struggle paying for care for their own children.
The Pulp, October 8, 2025 , reporting on the veto and the worker-assistance program.
Failed · HB 457 A wider doorway to help never opened The proposal to expand Best Beginnings eligibility failed in the House.
The affordability gap remains a reason to examine who gets help, who is left just outside the rules, and how to make additional support sustainable.
