In MIT’s basic-needs budget for two working adults and two children in Yellowstone County, child care is the largest single expense: $26,292 a year.
That is $2,191 a month and about 52% more than the model’s housing cost.
A family still has to pay for food, transportation, medical care and everything else.
A modeled family budget, not a quote from a local provider.
Actual costs depend on children’s ages, care arrangements and assistance.
The price is one barrier.
A place is another.
Even if a family can pay, there may not be a licensed place available.
In 2024, Montana’s licensed capacity could cover less than half of estimated demand.
For infant care, it was about one-third.
The state counted about 21,100 licensed places against 46,290 children under six in working-parent households.
More capacity is needed, but a license alone does not guarantee an open, staffed place.
Eligible for help.
Still not reached.
A program on paper is not the same as help reaching a family.
A Montana Budget & Policy Center analysis estimated that Best Beginnings reached only a small share of income-eligible children.
The gap was especially wide for school-age children.
The report identified a mismatch between licensing rules and afterschool programs as a barrier to accepting scholarships.
School ends.
The workday doesn’t.
Families need options after the last bell and during school breaks, not only before kindergarten.
Yet one major afterschool funding round awarded less than a third of the dollars Montana programs requested.
For the 2024–25 school year, 26 applicants requested funding and 13 grants were awarded.
That is a gap in support for programs, not a measure of the entire state’s afterschool budget.
Some progress.
Unfinished work.
The 2025 Legislature created a new funding foundation.
But proposals to widen scholarship access and support the people providing care did not become law.
Passing something is a start.
Closing the gaps is the test.

A new funding foundation
The Montana Early Childhood Account received a one-time $10 million transfer and a share of GO Trust interest.
It creates a way to support affordability and the workforce, but it is not a guaranteed $10 million every year.
A bill to qualify most child care workers for Best Beginnings passed the Legislature but was vetoed.
The people families rely on to provide care can face the same struggle paying for care for their own children.
The proposal to expand Best Beginnings eligibility failed in the House.
The affordability gap remains a reason to examine who gets help, who is left just outside the rules, and how to make additional support sustainable.
We’re fighting for a Montana where parents can work, children can thrive, and the people caring for them can afford to stay in the job.
These are the priorities we want to advance, not promises that the work is already done.
Help that reaches families
Push for scholarship access that reflects real family costs, with a simpler path to applying and keeping help.
Measure success by families reached, not just eligibility on paper.
Keep caregivers in care
Revisit support for child care workers’ own families and more predictable provider payments.
Affordable care depends on a workforce that can afford to keep doing it.
Places that fit a workday
Prioritize infant care and workable paths for afterschool and summer programs to participate in assistance.
Expand access without treating safety as something to trade away.
Funding with follow-through
Use the new funding foundation responsibly.
Track staffed places, family costs, provider stability and who actually receives support, so we can see what works and where gaps remain.
Our kids deserve care.
Our families deserve a chance.
These gaps are not a reason to give up on Montana.
They are a reason to get to work.
We can fight for a future where raising a family and making a living belong in the same sentence.
