The property-tax rebates going to homeowners are a good sign of trying to ease costs.
I support legislation that directly helps us afford a home and that reduces rental costs.
But who wins — and who loses?
The 2025 session passed legislation easing construction costs and hurdles for low- and moderate-income housing projects.
That will help if we can actually increase supply, which is one of the biggest reasons homes and rent are so expensive.
The problem: it helps the people who build housing more than the people who buy or rent it.
Lowering the cost to build a house doesn’t force realtors or owners to lower prices.
The same legislation completely ignored renters and left 130,000 Montana renters without any tax rebate.
You can hope your landlord passes his savings on.

Rent and childcare can chew up half a paycheck, student loans and consumer debt pile interest, and Gen Z and Millennials own homes at far lower rates than earlier generations at the same age.
The elderly and long-time homeowners were sold the idea that if they paid off their house they’d be safe.
Then property taxes and insurance jumped 60%+ and utility bills climbed 25–30% since 2020.
Fixed incomes don’t inflate with 17% electricity spikes and double-digit health-insurance hikes.
That’s a punishment for getting old.
Banks and card issuers take about $15 billion a year in late fees and $12+ billion in overdraft fees, in $27–$35 hits concentrated on the poorest customers.
Airlines have raked in $12.4 billion in seat fees since 2018.
Add convenience fees on bills and creeping charges in telecom and travel, and you’ve built a system that quietly taxes anyone with irregular income or a bad month.
The ridiculousness is not that people are struggling.
It’s that the data makes it crystal clear why they’re struggling, and yet most public debate still blames “the kids,” “the boomers,” or “bad choices” instead of confronting a policy design that turned housing, energy, childcare, health care, and basic banking into extraction machines.
The collapse in the young generation’s prospects and the squeeze on the working and retired isn’t just a moral failure.
It’s a math problem we created, and we absolutely have the tools to undo it if we stop pretending this is normal.
