How the island estimate works
Our illustration assumes 20% down, a $57.6 million loan, a 30-year term and a hypothetical fixed 6.5% annual interest rate.
Annual gross income is modeled so principal and interest equal 30% of income.
The result is $14,562,847 a year, rounded to $14.6 million.
Monthly principal and interest = L × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)−360], where L is $57,600,000 and r is 0.065 ÷ 12.
Annual income = monthly payment × 12 ÷ 0.30.

Home values jumped.
Wages didn’t keep up.
The affordability squeeze did not happen overnight.
During the pandemic-era surge, Montana home values rose far faster than hourly pay.
That gap helps explain why working hard and saving money can still feel like falling behind.
Rent takes more.
Life gets less.
At Montana’s estimated mean renter wage, one full-time worker’s affordable rent budget falls well short of the two-bedroom benchmark.
That leaves less room for groceries, child care, transportation and the savings that help a family get ahead.

Progress is possible.
Keep the focus on homes.
Montana’s March 2026 ARPA housing evaluation reports support for 746 affordable housing units across 11 projects in eight counties.
That includes both new homes and existing homes rehabilitated.
It is real progress to recognize, without pretending the affordability problem is solved.
Families are the point.
Whatever the argument in Helena, we cannot lose sight of the people trying to build a life here.
Our focus is a Montana where families can put down roots, stay in their communities and plan for tomorrow.
