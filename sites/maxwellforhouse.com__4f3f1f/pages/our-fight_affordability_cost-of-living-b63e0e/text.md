The cost of going without.
Affordability is not just about the balance at the end of the month.
It can determine whether someone gets medical care when they need it.
In 2024, 12% of Montana adults reported a time in the previous year when they needed to see a doctor but could not because of cost.
That is a measured barrier to care.
It does not, by itself, quantify the later health or financial consequences.

Progress matters.
So does what’s left.
The goal is not to pretend nothing is improving.
It is to ask whether improvements are reaching families after the necessities are paid for.
Recognize real gains The state reports that inflation-adjusted wages grew an average of 1.6% annually since 2022.
That is progress worth recognizing, but a statewide average does not describe every household’s budget.

Lower inflation isn’t a lower bill A price can stop rising and still be much higher than it used to be.
Slower growth helps, but families still have to afford the level where costs settle.
For example, a hypothetical $1,000 bill that rises to $1,400 stays $400 higher even if it stops increasing.
Compare like with like A single worker’s wage is not a whole household’s income.
A new buyer’s mortgage is not an existing renter’s payment.
We need comparisons that fit the family and the costs being measured.
That is why the two-paycheck example uses an explicit household and work schedule, not the income of all households mixed together.
Lower everyday costs.
Show real results.
We’re fighting for a Montana where work can support a stable life.
That means judging proposals by what families can actually afford and access, not just by the size of an announcement.
Look at the whole household Count rent, care, utilities, transportation, food and medical costs together.
A saving on one bill is not enough if the cost simply moves somewhere else.
Turn help into access Measure homes people can actually live in and staffed child care places families can use.
Eligibility, permits and funding announcements are not the same as results.
Show who benefits Ask what a proposal means for renters, homeowners, single parents and rural and tribal communities.
Be clear about who is helped, who is excluded and who pays.
Measure what families keep Track net bills, earnings and the room left for emergencies and the future.
Distinguish lasting improvements from a temporary rebate or a cost passed to someone else.
These are our standards for evaluating and developing policy, not claims that a particular proposal has already delivered savings.
Implementation, tradeoffs and fiscal details still matter.
