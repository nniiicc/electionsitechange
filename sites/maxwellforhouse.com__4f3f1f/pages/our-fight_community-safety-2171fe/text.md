I will work to bring a Montana version of Florida’s Marchman Act to our state: a clear, recovery-focused path for families to seek assessment and treatment during a dangerous substance-use crisis.
To me, the gaps in our current approach are too important to leave alone.
We need a process that families, first responders, courts and care providers can actually use, with strong safeguards and somewhere appropriate for a person to receive care.
This is a campaign goal, not an enacted Montana law or a promise that involuntary treatment will prevent every crisis.
The goal is protection and a chance at recovery, not punishment for having an addiction.
My father struggles with alcoholism.
While I was in Florida, he became so intoxicated that our family was at risk.
I was worried about my children, my siblings and myself, and I was worried about him.
My family was able to use Florida’s Marchman Act to get him out of the home and into a care center before anyone was hurt.
We did not want him harmed, and we did not want him to harm anyone else.
In his state, I believed a fight was likely.
I believe that intervention kept violence from happening and protected all of us, including my father.
That experience is why I want Montana families to have a clear path to help before a crisis becomes a tragedy.

Montana is not starting from nothing.
MCA § 53-24-302 already provides an alcohol-focused court commitment process that specified family members and others may petition to use, subject to statutory criteria and rights.
It requires a finding that adequate, appropriate and likely beneficial treatment can be provided.
The separate mental-health commitment definition excludes addiction or intoxication alone, while recognizing that a mental disorder can co-occur with chemical dependency (MCA § 53-21-102).
That distinction matters when a crisis centers on substance use rather than a qualifying mental disorder.
Florida’s Marchman Act provides for involuntary substance-use assessment and treatment when legal criteria are met.
Its criteria include loss of self-control due to substance use together with specified impaired-judgment or serious-harm conditions; refusal of services alone is not enough (Florida Statutes § 397.675).
My proposal is to learn from that approach, modernize Montana’s substance-use framework and make it workable here, not simply copy Florida’s law.
The details need Montana constitutional and clinical review, input from families and people with lived experience, and a funded implementation plan.
The personal account above describes my experience, not a guarantee of outcomes for other families.

Billings’ Community Service Officers handled 8,582 incidents in 2025, up 35.1% from 2024.
The annual report describes a team of ten nonsworn CSOs.
Their duties include cold property-crime reports, minor crashes, traffic control and investigative support.
That is meaningful work, and it deserves to be measured honestly.
More incidents handled does not tell us how many crimes were prevented or how many sworn-officer hours were saved.
We should track those outcomes, not assume them.
