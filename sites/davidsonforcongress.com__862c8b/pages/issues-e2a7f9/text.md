The 6th Amendment guarantees a speedy and public trial by an impartial jury, notice of the accusation, confrontation of witnesses, compulsory process for screening biased witnesses, and assistance of professional counsel.
The 7th Amendment preserves the right to jury trial in certain civil suits, and limits re-examination of facts already tried by a jury.
The 8th prohibits excessive bail, excessive fines, and cruel and unusual punishments.
This enforces proportionality and humanity in pretrial detention, monetary penalties, and sentencing.

Sound money is essential to defending freedom.
Define Sound Money.
Sound money means currency that serves as a stable store of value, an efficient means of exchange, and an immutable record of account.
It protects the purchasing power of wage earners and savers rather than systematically transferring wealth through inflation and asset-price distortions.
The US Dollar (USD) has enjoyed the enormous privilege of being the world’s reserve currency.
That status is not automatic, or permanent, and our current fiscal and monetary policy threatens our future as the global reserve currency.
In 2020, I founded the Congressional Sound Money Caucus.
It was obvious that Congress would keep massive spending, governments would compel less production due to forced closures, and the Federal Reserve would cover for it all (ultimately doubling the size of its balance sheet).
Ongoing deficit spending has inflated asset prices, widened the wealth gap, and eroded the dollar as a store of value.
Sound money is not a slogan; it’s the only sustainable future.
The Federal Reserve.
The Fed has failed its dual mandate.
Its asset purchases and balance-sheet expansion create monetary inflation that first shows up in asset prices (benefiting those who already own assets) and then in consumer prices that hit ordinary Americans hardest.
By covering unlimited deficits—buying Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities—the Fed removes the natural market signal that would force fiscal discipline.
An independent central bank’s job is to preserve sound money, not to paper over congressional overspending or engage in central planning.
I support auditing the Fed, separating its regulatory functions from monetary policy (because regulatory tools create further distortions), and reforms that prevent it from automatically monetizing deficits.
In extreme cases, if the institution cannot be reformed to prioritize sound money, more fundamental change may be required.
The Fed should set policy based on market metrics consistent with price stability, not political convenience.
Interest Rates.
Interest rates ultimately reflect two components: the time value of money (compensation for expected inflation so that purchasing power is preserved) and default risk.
Both are trending up.
Why then should you expect lower interest rates?
The Federal Reserve influences certain rates and can distort markets, but the market ultimately prices risk.
Artificially low rates and quantitative easing fueled the inflation we experienced; they also encourage malinvestment.
I have repeatedly highlighted the perverse incentives created by interest on excess reserves (IOER/IER).
Before 2008 the Fed did not pay banks interest on excess reserves; banks had to manage their own balance sheets and deploy capital.
Today the Fed pays banks a risk-free rate (recently around 4.4%) simply to park reserves, effectively paying them not to lend into the real economy.
That is why I introduced the Prohibition on IOER Act.
When the Fed cuts its policy rate, long-term market rates can still rise if inflation or credit-risk expectations increase—exactly what we have seen.
Command-and-control rate setting is not the same as a free-market price signal.
Ending the subsidy to idle reserves and restoring market discipline would do more for productive lending and growth than repeated political pressure for rate cuts.
