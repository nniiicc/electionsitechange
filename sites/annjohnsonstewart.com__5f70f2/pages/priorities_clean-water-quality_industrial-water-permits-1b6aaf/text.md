Industrial Water Permits
Overview: Minnesota’s groundwater is a finite resource that belongs to the public, not just industrial mega-users.
Senator Johnson Stewart’s legislation ensures that high-volume commercial operations—like hyperscale data centers and bottling plants—are subject to rigorous scientific review before they can drain millions of gallons from local aquifers.
Key Takeaways:
- Strict Permitting Triggers: Mandating direct DNR oversight (S.F. 3852) for any commercial operator requesting more than 100 million gallons of water annually.
- Mandatory Aquifer Testing: Requiring rigorous 72-hour hydrogeologic stress tests to prove that commercial pumping won’t deplete the surrounding water table.
- Protecting Homeowners: Preventing “well interference” by legally ensuring that corporate water extraction never drops the aquifer below the intake pipes of local residential and municipal wells.
Closing the Industrial Water Loophole: Scientific Rigor for Mega-Users
When commercial operations or hyperscale data centers attempt to draw hundreds of millions of gallons from local groundwater, they shouldn’t be allowed to hide behind municipal permits.
We need independent DNR review, hydrogeologic testing, and local accountability.
Senator Ann Johnson Stewart chief-authored S.F. 3852 to establish strict new transparency standards for mega-users.
Under this proposed legislation, any new or modified commercial water use exceeding 100 million gallons per year (or 50% of a city’s allocation) would be required to apply for its own independent state permit.

Why “Piggybacking” on City Permits Puts Municipalities at Risk
In Minnesota, municipalities obtain water appropriation permits from the DNR to supply residential homes, small businesses, and essential services.
However, under current regulatory practices, a large industrial user—such as a data center or bottling plant—can connect to a city’s utility system and consume vast quantities of groundwater under the city’s umbrella permit.
A clear example of this regulatory gap occurred in Elko New Market, where a major commercial water bottling operation was authorized to draw up to 365 million gallons of groundwater per year.
Because the facility utilized the city’s existing municipal permit framework, it effectively bypassed the independent state environmental review typically triggered by high-volume commercial extractions.
The Danger to Cities
- Loss of Control: Cities become the legal “permittee,” leaving municipal taxpayers legally and financially responsible if total water withdrawal depletes local aquifers or violates state conservation limits.
- Obscured Usage Data: High-volume commercial consumption gets mixed into public drinking water metrics, making it difficult for hydrogeologists to track industrial impact.
The S.F. 3852 Fix: This legislation would mandate that mega-users apply directly to the DNR for an individual water-use permit, rather than modifying a city permit, if their demand exceeds 100,000,000 gallons per year or 50% of the city’s annual allocation.
The scale of this issue is immense.
A single hyperscale data center can consume up to 5 million gallons of water per day for server cooling—an extraction rate equivalent to the daily usage of an entire residential suburb or a town of 50,000 people.
A prime example occurred in Farmington, Minnesota, where a proposed data center sought to draw 900 million gallons of water annually while pushing to remain under the city’s municipal umbrella permit to avoid independent accountability.
When massive commercial usage is shielded inside domestic permits, state hydrologists lose the ability to accurately monitor regional aquifer health.
Ultimately, this loophole forces everyday residents to absorb the risks of well depletion and the financial burden of rapid municipal infrastructure strain.
Engineering Insight: The 100M Gallon Trigger
Definition: The statutory threshold in S.F. 3852 requiring a separate DNR permit for high-volume commercial water users.
The Impact: Protects city councils from being forced into acting as unfunded regulatory middlemen for multi-national corporations.
The Underground Physics: Cones of Depression & Well Interference
Pumping large volumes of groundwater isn’t like drinking from a glass; it changes the physical pressure and water table level in the surrounding hydrogeologic formation.
- The Cone of Depression: When a high-capacity commercial pump operates continuously, it draws down the water level around the wellhead in a V-shaped pattern known as a Cone of Depression.
- Well Interference: If a commercial cone of depression overlaps with nearby shallow domestic wells or city water supply wells, water levels can drop below the intake pumps of local homes.
Under Minnesota Rules 6115.0730, heavy appropriators can be held liable for causing well interference, but investigating these failures after the fact forces local homeowners to rely on temporary emergency water supplies while lengthy state investigations take place.
Mandatory Aquifer Testing: S.F. 3852 would strengthen preventive protections by authorizing the DNR Commissioner to mandate rigorous hydrogeologic aquifer tests prior to permit issuance.
This would require applicants to pump test wells while monitoring nearby test sites to verify aquifer drawdown before commercial operations begin.
Engineering Insight: Cone of Depression
Definition: The localized downward slope in the water table caused by heavy well pumping.
The Danger: If unmeasured, a severe cone of depression causes “well interference,” leaving nearby home wells sucking dry air.
The Fix: S.F. 3852 requires hydrogeologic pump testing upfront to map drawdown before permits are finalized.
During these mandatory 72-hour stress tests, state hydrologists monitor the surrounding aquifer network while the commercial applicant pumps water at their maximum requested rate.
This creates a real-world baseline, allowing engineers to map the exact radius of the cone of depression and verify the recharge rate of the aquifer.
If the test proves that the industrial pumping will draw the water table below the intake pipes of neighboring municipal or private residential wells, the permit cannot be issued.
Shifting the Financial Liability Off Suburban Homeowners
When an unmonitored commercial user overburdens an aquifer or city utility network, the cost of fixing the problem falls on local residents:
- Emergency Infrastructure Capital: Upgrading municipal treatment plants, lowering municipal pumps, or drilling deeper municipal wells costs taxpayers millions of dollars.
- Residential Well Costs: Homeowners experiencing well interference must pay out-of-pocket for temporary water supplies and well driller inspections while investigations take place.
- Fiscal Responsibility: By requiring large industrial entities to hold their own independent DNR permits, S.F. 3852 would ensure that financial liability, monitoring obligations, and mitigation costs rest squarely on the commercial operator—not on local taxpayers or municipal water utilities.
Water protection isn’t just about environmental stewardship—it’s about fiscal protection for our local communities.
