From the Desk of Senator Zimmer
One thing I love about this job is the way it presents interesting questions about our past, present, and future, and about the way we solve thorny problems for our communities.
Here’s an interesting question I’ve been chewing on recently.
In the late 1970s, in a quest to find more uses for Iowa corn, the Iowa Corn Production Board introduced a 10% ethanol blend.
Recognizing the potential benefit of this ethanol blend as an alternative energy source, the Federal Renewable Fuel Standards were adopted in 2005, accelerating ethanol production and use across the country.
The ethanol blend is produced by grinding corn (dry milling) into a powder/flour, creating a mixture with water, and adding heat.
The heating process accelerates fermentation and releases carbon dioxide (CO2).
Now, too much CO2 is extremely harmful to the human body, so, in 2008, the Section 45Q tax credits were established for the purpose of incentivizing carbon reduction/capture – also known as sequestration.
As production of ethanol grew – over 60% of Iowa corn is now used for ethanol production – so, too, did the need for reliable sequestration measures.
So much so that in 2024 the Iowa Utilities Commission (IUC) ruled that carbon sequestration served a public benefit.
Here's the hiccup: in ruling that carbon sequestration served a public benefit, the IUC allowed pipeline companies to use eminent domain for construction.
This, understandably, hit a nerve with many around the state.
Homeowners have visited the Capitol on an almost-daily basis petitioning the legislature to override the IUC’s decision and prevent the use of eminent domain for pipeline construction.
Things came to a head during the 2025 legislative session when Senate Republicans, after years of stonewalling, were forced to bring a bill to the floor to ban the use of eminent domain for private gain – the pipeline at the heart of the homeowners’ concerns will belong to a private corporation.
The bill passed but was eventually vetoed by Gov.
Kim Reynolds.
A similar bill passed through the Iowa House during the 2026 legislative session, but Senate Republicans refused to bring it to the floor for a vote, effectively killing the issue for the year.
Concurrently, while the debate around eminent domain and carbon sequestration pipelines raged, another policy issue was rapidly developing: data center construction.
Many local communities have expressed concerns regarding data centers’ energy and resource use, especially as they relate to water.
Server towers produce a ton of heat, and early iterations of data centers required vast amounts of water to serve as a coolant.
But maybe there’s an alternative solution.
Pop quiz: what do soda pop, vodka, decaffeinated coffee, fire extinguishers, and data centers all have in common?
Answer: they can all use carbon dioxide.
I had a fascinating conversation not long ago wherein I was challenged to reconsider CO2 as a commodity rather than a waste product to simply be piped away and stored.
One suggestion was that CO2 could be used as a refrigerant to cool data centers instead of water.
I was intrigued by the suggestion, so I dove into the research and discovered that while no U.S. centers were using this technique, some smaller data centers in Canada were.
I spoke with a representative of one of the French/Canadian HVAC companies installing these cooling systems and he told me the company had design plans to build and implement CO2 cooling units in the United States that were capable of handling large (500,000+ sq.ft.) data centers.
Our demand for everything digital is transforming our lives and the technology required to meet those demands is evolving every day.
Carbon dioxide represents a potential shared connective tissue between these two seemingly separate policy issues – data center construction and carbon sequestration – at the center of our state’s public discourse.
Using processed, sequestered CO2 from our local ethanol plants to cool data centers instead of water could be a potential solution for protecting our local aquifers.
Maybe it gets us closer to a resolution on the issue of eminent domain for pipeline construction.
This could be a way for us to move the conversation forward on both issues and reframe the way we view carbon dioxide as a resource commodity rather than a waste product to be shunted away.
It’s an interesting idea to think about!
Back to school: supporting public education
The start of a brand new school year is just around the corner now.
In fact, by the time you’re reading this it may have already started!
Iowa kids are heading into their new classrooms to meet new teachers and take the next step on their educational journey.
It’s an exciting time of year for children and adults alike.
It’s also a good time to reflect on the state of education in Iowa – where are we, how did we get here, and what can we do better to give Iowa kids a brighter future?
According to the state’s certified enrollment numbers, almost 92% of Iowa’s school-age kids attended public schools in the 2025-26 school year – that’s nearly 475,000 kids.
This means that our public schools represent one of the best and most worthwhile investments we can make in our state’s future.
Unfortunately, some of the adults in the room – Republican lawmakers at the State Capitol – have spent the past decade undermining our investment in public education and stifling access to important student and family resources.
State funding for public education has fallen so far behind the rate of inflation that many school districts start each budgeting cycle with the threat of program elimination, classroom consolidation, or teacher layoffs.
If that weren’t enough, those same lawmakers have approved steep funding cuts to Iowa’s Area Education Agencies (AEAs), stripping many schools of resources for classroom technology, special education, or professional development.
Iowa’s educational landscape is further complicated by an ever-expanding voucher program.
Private schools get unlimited access to taxpayer funds without any of the oversight or accountability required of public schools.
Private schools taking public funds can raise tuition rates without warning and deny admittance to anyone without public oversight or justification while draining resources from the public schools that educate the vast majority of kids.
I believe that public dollars should be for public schools, full stop, but if we can’t agree on that, we should at least be able to agree that everyone should have to play by the same rules, right?
Our state was built on an educational foundation.
It’s the promise we make to ourselves that future generations will be prepared for adulthood in world-class learning environments with highly trained and capable educators.
When lawmakers chip away at that foundation, it diminishes the future Iowa that we all want.
As the new school year kicks off, let’s remember to celebrate the schools and educators whose efforts have been neglected and unsupported by some at the Capitol, but whose work is so important.
Let’s keep fighting to give Iowa’s kids the support they deserve.
Let’s make public education in Iowa the envy of the world.
To all the students, parents, and teachers: have a wonderful school year!

Iowa EMT program is the first in the nation approved for Workforce Pell Grants: Eligible students can now use the federal Pell Grant aid for a 14-week EMT program at Iowa Central Community College, helping more future first responders access training without taking on unnecessary debt.
Workforce Pell allows eligible students to use Pell grants for students enrolled in short-term, accredited programs lasting eight to 15 weeks.
Eligible programs must meet rigorous earnings, job placement, and completion benchmarks.
They also require approval from both the governor and U.S. education secretary.
Pell Grants traditionally have been reserved for students who show financial need and are pursuing undergraduate degrees.
Short-term credentials can be an important path into a career, especially when training is affordable, high-quality, and connected to real workforce needs.
This new federal grant builds upon Iowa’s long work in supporting job training and certifications with the Iowa Kibbie Grants and high school dual enrollment career and technical training programs.
