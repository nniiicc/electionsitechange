Skip to content Facebook X-twitter Instagram Menu Contribute Austin American-Statesman Johnson would return the attorney general’s office to its mission October 6, 2026 Given the fiercely partisan nature of Attorney General Ken Paxton’s 12-year tenure — challenging the 2020 presidential election results, suing a COVID vaccine manufacturer, antagonizing Muslims and investigating whether schools are “teaching woke critical race theory” — Texans could be forgiven for thinking the agency’s job is to press a political agenda.
But it’s not.
Or at least it shouldn’t be.
With Paxton pursuing a U.S.
Senate seat, voters in the Nov.
3 election have an opportunity for a much-needed reboot to the office that’s supposed to defend state agencies, protect consumers and enforce the state’s open records law.
We strongly recommend state Sen.
Nathan Johnson, a Dallas attorney with a demonstrated fidelity to the Constitution, as the leader to reset the attorney general’s office to serve all Texans, not one party.
“The number one thing I plan to do is reestablish a culture of ethical public service,” Johnson, the Democratic nominee, told our Editorial Board.
That would include retaining the AG staffer doing the core, apolitical work — combating consumer fraud, ensuring people pay court-ordered child support and prosecuting human trafficking cases — and replacing those bent on partisan litigation.
Contrast that with the focus articulated by state Sen.
Mayes Middleton, the Republican nominee best known for culture war crusades like policing bathrooms and pushing prayer in public schools.
Middleton, a Galveston-based attorney who runs his family’s oil and gas company, did not meet with us.
But he told attendees at the Conservative Political Action Conference in March that as attorney general he would “use every resource and tool to back up President Trump and his America First agenda.” Call us old fashioned, but the state’s top legal officer should represent the interests of Texans, not the president.
Sometimes the two diverge.
Look no further than the border wall construction that Texans broadly oppose in the Big Bend region: An attorney general working for Texans would fight the feds, not fold to the president while crews needlessly tear up a treasured landscape at a steep cost to taxpayers.
Johnson, on the other hand, pointed to a situation where he would have sued the president of his party: when the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services under the Biden administration took aim at Texas’ mechanism for raising local funding for Medicaid, a dispute involving billions of dollars.
“You should have an attorney general who will sue over that, because your party’s president isn’t always going to be right,” Johnson said.
“They might not like the way we finance stuff in Texas, but it’s legal, and if you don’t like it, change the law.” Perhaps the most stark difference between the two major party candidates centers on the First Amendment’s protection of religious freedom.
Johnson says the Constitution is clear: the government cannot push a religious agenda.
Middleton has asserted that America is a Christian nation and “there’s no such thing as separation of church and state” — raising concerns he would use the attorney general’s legal powers to favor one faith tradition over others.
Voters also have the choice of Libertarian candidate Tom Oxford, an attorney from Nederland.
The attorney general’s office has experienced extraordinary turmoil during Paxton’s scandal- ridden tenure: the firing of whistleblowers, an FBI investigation, the Texas House impeaching Paxton (and the Senate acquitting him), all connected to the use of agency resources to help a prominent Paxton donor and facilitate Paxton’s extramarital affair.
The next head of this sizable agency — which has 4,000 employees, including about 750 attorneys — will have to rebuild the culture and reassert its priorities.
But to what end?
Using the state’s chief legal agency to pursue a religious and ideological agenda, as Middleton has pledged to do, would be a corruption of a different kind.
A misuse of a public trust to advance private goals.
Johnson represents not only a clean break from the Paxton era but a fresh focus on Texans’ shared interests.
He is not only the best choice for attorney general, he is the urgently needed one.
