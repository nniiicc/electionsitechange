My race
Why I'm running
I grew up identifying as a Democrat because I was against foreign wars, pro free speech, and against government-enforced cultural norms.
I turned 18 in 2008 and cast my first vote for Barack Obama, persuaded by his campaign promises: End the wars, bring healthcare costs down, rein in warrantless surveillance.
I thought those were the right promises then, and I still do now.
Those promises were broken; Republicans have done no better with theirs.
The practice of breaking promises has not changed over the years, and neither have my values.
So I'm running for the office where a legislator actually can keep promises small enough to check: the state House.
Our institutions have lost public trust, either because they authorized the discord and division that we see today or they were powerless to prevent it.
Confidence in self-governance isn't restored by speeches.
It's restored by results.
A state representative's job is narrower than national politics likes to admit.
We don't decide federal policy from the Colorado House.
What we do decide is how transportation dollars get spent, whether police can take property from people they haven't convicted, whether Colorado Guard members get sent overseas without Congress voting on it, and whether voters have a say in housing decisions made for their communities.
I'm running because those questions deserve someone who will answer them honestly, without igniting further division through government control.
I've spent years reading Colorado legislation closely, and here's the pattern: the parts that matter are buried.
Bills frequently get titles that don't describe what they do.
They contain provisions nobody asked for, added after the hearings.
When residents show up to testify, they're pushing back on things they were never told about.
That's not an accident of a busy legislature.
It's a routine.
That only changes if we have representatives setting a new precedent.
My background
Who I am
I'm not a native, but I got here as fast as I could: I moved to Gunnison at 18 for Western Colorado University and never left the state.
I'm a software developer and data analyst with a background in physical science, and my work turns complicated public data into something anyone can use, with tools that make campaign finance, voting records, and legislation readable without needing to be a specialist.
That's the same instinct behind this campaign.
Government should be transparent to the people who pay for it.
I came to Colorado from the Chicago suburbs, and what I found was a state with a habit most of the country has lost: trusting regular people to decide things for themselves.
Colorado's voters, not its politicians, made this the first state to approve women's suffrage at the ballot box, in 1893, twenty-seven years before the federal amendment.
In 1992, Colorado's voters did something no other state has ever done: they amended their own constitution to require that politicians ask permission before raising taxes.
TABOR — the Taxpayer's Bill of Rights — isn't really about taxes.
It's about who decides.
Coloradans reserved that call for themselves.
Colorado's voters ended marijuana prohibition in 2012, and Washington still hasn't caught up.
Nobody handed those decisions down.
People here voted for them, and history keeps ruling in their favor.
That tradition is still alive in Jeffco.
I've watched neighbors show up to hearings, read the ordinances themselves, and vote on the future of their own blocks.
I'm running to give that tradition a seat in the legislature.
Beyond the day job, I serve as Chair of the Jefferson County affiliate of the Libertarian Party of Colorado and as the state party's Legislative Director, roles that have given me a close look at how state-level legislation actually gets made, who shows up to influence it, and where the gaps are between what the legislature passes and what residents experience.
